The term change management is used with two meanings: for the controlled handling of changes to the project scope and for guiding changes within an organisation. Both play an important role in IT projects.

## Changes to the project scope

During a project, new wishes almost always come up: an additional feature, a changed design, another interface. If these wishes are simply implemented, the scope grows gradually while time and budget stay the same. This phenomenon is called **scope creep** and is a common cause of failed projects.

### Change process

Every change that affects scope, schedule or costs therefore goes through a defined process:

1. **Submit a change request:** Whoever wants a change submits a written **change request**. It describes what is to be changed and why.
2. **Analyse the impact:** The project team checks what effects the change has on scope, schedule, costs, risks and other work packages.
3. **Decide:** The client or a change control board decides whether the change is accepted, rejected or postponed.
4. **Update the plans:** If it is accepted, the project order, plans and budget are updated.
5. **Implement and document:** The change is implemented and recorded in the change log.

:::note
In agile projects, changes are welcome. But even there they are not simply implemented; they are added to the product backlog as a new item and prioritised by the Product Owner.
:::

## Organisational change management

Many IT projects change the way people work: a new ERP system replaces familiar processes, and a ticket system makes the work of the support team visible. Organisational change management makes sure that the people affected understand, accept and support the change.

### Lewin's three-stage model

Kurt Lewin describes change in three stages:

1. **Unfreezing:** The need for change is explained, and the status quo is questioned. Those affected should understand why something has to change.
2. **Moving:** The new processes and systems are introduced, accompanied by training and support.
3. **Refreezing:** The new becomes a habit. Old ways are switched off, and successes are made visible.

John Kotter later extended this model to eight steps, starting with creating a sense of urgency and ending with anchoring the change in the corporate culture.

### Resistance

Resistance to change is normal. Common causes are:

- fear of the unknown or of being overwhelmed
- concern about one's own job or influence
- lack of information about reasons and goals
- bad experiences with earlier changes
- additional effort during the changeover

Helpful countermeasures are early and honest information, involving those affected, training, visible support from management and taking objections seriously. Resistance often contains justified hints about weaknesses in the planned solution.
