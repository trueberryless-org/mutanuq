A project team is more than the sum of its members. How well a team works together depends on how it develops, which rules it sets itself and how its members talk to each other.

## Team development according to Tuckman

In 1965, the psychologist Bruce Tuckman described typical stages that teams go through:

| Stage       | Description                                                                                         | Task of the project manager                            |
| ----------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Forming     | Members get to know each other, are polite and cautious, and roles are still unclear.                | give orientation, explain objectives and rules         |
| Storming    | Different opinions and ways of working clash, and there are conflicts over roles and influence.       | address and moderate conflicts, do not suppress them   |
| Norming     | The team agrees on rules and roles, and trust and cohesion grow.                                    | record agreements, hand over responsibility            |
| Performing  | The team works efficiently and independently towards its goals.                                     | support, remove obstacles, step back                   |
| Adjourning  | The project ends, and the team breaks up.                                                           | acknowledge successes, shape the farewell, secure experience |

The stages do not always follow each other neatly. If new members join or the objectives change, a team can fall back into an earlier stage.

## Team roles

Besides the formal project roles, people also take on informal roles in a team. Meredith Belbin distinguishes, among others:

- **action-oriented roles:** shaper, implementer and completer finisher drive the work forward and focus on results.
- **people-oriented roles:** coordinator, teamworker and resource investigator hold the team together and make contacts.
- **thought-oriented roles:** plant, monitor evaluator and specialist contribute ideas, critical analysis and expertise.

A good team needs a mix of these roles. If a team consists only of plants, many ideas emerge, but few finished results.

## Team culture

**Team culture** covers the shared values, rules and habits of a team. It can be shaped deliberately:

- agree on **ground rules**, such as punctuality, availability, dealing with mistakes and decision rules
- choose a **project name** and a logo to strengthen identification
- **celebrate successes**, for example when a milestone is reached
- hold **retrospectives** in which the team regularly talks about its collaboration
- foster an open **culture of learning from mistakes** in which problems are raised early

## Communication

### Four-sides model

According to Friedemann Schulz von Thun, every message has four sides:

- **Factual content:** What am I informing about?
- **Self-disclosure:** What do I reveal about myself?
- **Relationship:** What do I think of you, how do we stand towards each other?
- **Appeal:** What do I want you to do?

The sentence "The server has crashed again" can inform factually, express annoyance, contain a reproach to the administrator and the request to finally take care of it. Misunderstandings arise when sender and receiver emphasise different sides.

### Feedback rules

Good feedback helps the team to improve. The following rules help:

- use I-messages ("I noticed that …") instead of you-messages ("You always …")
- describe concrete behaviour, do not judge the person
- give feedback promptly, not weeks later
- also mention positive things
- as the receiver, listen, ask questions and do not justify yourself immediately

### Active listening

With active listening, you show the other person that you understand them: you listen attentively, summarise what was said in your own words and ask when something is unclear.

## Meetings

Meetings cost a lot of working time. To be worthwhile, they should be well prepared:

- **Preparation:** define the goal, send an agenda with time slots in advance, invite only the necessary people.
- **Execution:** start on time, assign moderation and minute-taking, stick to the agenda, record decisions and tasks.
- **Follow-up:** send the minutes promptly and follow up on the agreed tasks.

Regular meetings at a fixed time, for example every Monday at 9 a.m., are called **recurring meetings** or regulars.

### Minutes

There are two types of minutes:

- **Action minutes** record only decisions, results and tasks. They are the usual form in everyday project work.
- **Verbatim or discussion minutes** also reproduce the course of the discussion. They are used when it must be possible to trace how a decision was reached.
