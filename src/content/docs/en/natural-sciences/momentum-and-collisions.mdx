Alongside energy, momentum is the second great conserved quantity in mechanics. It is used to calculate collisions, explosions and recoil, that is processes in which very large forces act for a very short time and the force itself is not known in detail.

## Momentum

The **momentum** (linear momentum) of a body is the product of mass and velocity:

$$
\vec p = m \cdot \vec v, \qquad [p] = \mathrm{kg\,m/s} = \mathrm{N\,s}
$$

Momentum is a vector and points in the direction of motion. A $10\,\mathrm{t}$ lorry at $36\,\mathrm{km/h}$ has a momentum of $10\,000 \cdot 10 = 10^5\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s}$, as much as a $1\,\mathrm{t}$ car at $360\,\mathrm{km/h}$.

Newton originally formulated his second law in terms of momentum:

$$
\vec F = \frac{\mathrm{d}\vec p}{\mathrm{d}t}
$$

For constant mass, this gives $\vec F = m \vec a$. The momentum form, however, also holds when the mass changes, as in a rocket.

## Impulse

If a force acts during the time $\Delta t$, the momentum changes by the **impulse** $\vec I$:

$$
\vec I = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \vec F\,\mathrm{d}t = \Delta \vec p, \qquad \text{for a constant force: } I = F \cdot \Delta t
$$

In the $F$-$t$ graph, the impulse is the area under the curve. For a given change in momentum, the force is smaller the longer the collision lasts. Crumple zones, airbags, cycle helmets and bending your knees when landing after a jump are all based on this.

:::tip[Example: football]
A $0.45\,\mathrm{kg}$ ball is kicked from rest to $25\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The foot is in contact for $10\,\mathrm{ms}$. Impulse $I = 0.45 \cdot 25 = 11.25\,\mathrm{N\,s}$, average force $F = 11.25/0.01 = 1125\,\mathrm{N}$, which equals the weight of about $115\,\mathrm{kg}$.
:::

## Conservation of momentum

> In a closed system (no external forces), the total momentum is conserved in magnitude and direction.

$$
\sum \vec p_\mathrm{before} = \sum \vec p_\mathrm{after}
$$

The law follows directly from Newton's third law: the internal forces between the bodies are equal and opposite, so their impulses cancel. It holds for **every** collision, even when mechanical energy is lost. The centre of mass of a closed system therefore moves uniformly in a straight line, whatever happens inside.

:::tip[Example: recoil of a rifle]
A rifle ($4\,\mathrm{kg}$) fires a bullet ($10\,\mathrm{g}$) at $400\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Before, everything is at rest and the total momentum is zero. Afterwards: $0.01 \cdot 400 + 4 \cdot v = 0$, so $v = -1\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The rifle moves backwards at $1\,\mathrm{m/s}$.
:::

## Collisions

In a collision, two bodies act on each other for a short time. Momentum is always conserved; kinetic energy only in an elastic collision.

| Type of collision | Momentum | Kinetic energy | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **perfectly elastic** | conserved | conserved | billiard balls, gas molecules |
| **partially elastic** (real collision) | conserved | partly converted into heat and deformation | tennis ball on the ground |
| **perfectly inelastic** (plastic) | conserved | maximum loss, bodies move on together | rear-end collision with cars locked together, balls of modelling clay |

### Perfectly elastic collision

For a **head-on central collision** (motion along one line) with masses $m_1$, $m_2$ and velocities $v_1$, $v_2$ before the collision, conservation of momentum and energy give:

$$
v_1' = \frac{(m_1 - m_2)\,v_1 + 2 m_2 v_2}{m_1 + m_2}, \qquad
v_2' = \frac{(m_2 - m_1)\,v_2 + 2 m_1 v_1}{m_1 + m_2}
$$

Special cases with the second body at rest ($v_2 = 0$):

- **equal masses**: $v_1' = 0$, $v_2' = v_1$. The balls exchange their velocities (Newton's cradle).
- **very large mass** $m_2$ (wall): $v_1' = -v_1$, the body bounces back with the same speed.
- **very small mass** $m_2$: $v_2' \approx 2 v_1$.

:::tip[Example: elastic collision]
A ball with $m_1 = 2\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $v_1 = 3\,\mathrm{m/s}$ hits a stationary ball with $m_2 = 1\,\mathrm{kg}$.

$$
v_1' = \frac{(2 - 1) \cdot 3}{3} = 1\,\mathrm{m/s}, \qquad v_2' = \frac{2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3}{3} = 4\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

Momentum check: before $2 \cdot 3 = 6$, after $2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 4 = 6\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s}$. Energy check: before $\frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 \cdot 9 = 9\,\mathrm{J}$, after $1 + 8 = 9\,\mathrm{J}$.
:::

### Perfectly inelastic collision

The bodies stick together after the collision and share a common velocity:

$$
v' = \frac{m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2}{m_1 + m_2}
$$

The lost kinetic energy goes into deformation and heat.

:::tip[Example: rear-end collision]
A car ($1500\,\mathrm{kg}$, $20\,\mathrm{m/s}$) runs into a stationary car ($1000\,\mathrm{kg}$), and the two lock together.

$$
v' = \frac{1500 \cdot 20}{2500} = 12\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

Kinetic energy before $\frac{1}{2} \cdot 1500 \cdot 20^2 = 300\,\mathrm{kJ}$, after $\frac{1}{2} \cdot 2500 \cdot 12^2 = 180\,\mathrm{kJ}$. $120\,\mathrm{kJ}$ (40 %) are converted into deformation and heat.
:::

:::tip[Example: ballistic pendulum]
A bullet ($10\,\mathrm{g}$) lodges in a hanging wooden block ($2\,\mathrm{kg}$), which then swings up by $5\,\mathrm{cm}$. Two steps are needed, because energy is lost in the collision:

1. Swing (conservation of energy): $v' = \sqrt{2 g h} = \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.05} = 0.990\,\mathrm{m/s}$
2. Collision (conservation of momentum): $v_1 = \frac{(m_1 + m_2)\,v'}{m_1} = \frac{2.01 \cdot 0.990}{0.01} = 199\,\mathrm{m/s}$

Only about $0.5\,\%$ of the bullet's kinetic energy remains as kinetic energy of the pendulum.
:::

### Partially elastic collision and coefficient of restitution

Real collisions lie in between. The **coefficient of restitution** $k$ compares the relative velocities after and before the collision:

$$
k = \frac{v_2' - v_1'}{v_1 - v_2}, \qquad 0 \le k \le 1
$$

$k = 1$ is elastic, $k = 0$ perfectly inelastic. If a ball falls from height $h$ onto the ground and bounces back to $h'$, then $k = \sqrt{h'/h}$.

### Oblique collision

If the bodies do not meet centrally, conservation of momentum applies to each component separately ($x$ and $y$). In an elastic collision of two equal masses, one of them at rest, the balls fly apart at a right angle afterwards, which is used in billiards.

## Recoil and the rocket principle

A rocket ejects gas backwards at the exhaust velocity $u$ (relative to the rocket). By conservation of momentum, it gains momentum forwards. It needs no air to "push against" and therefore also works in space.

The **thrust** is the product of exhaust velocity and mass flow rate:

$$
F_\mathrm{thrust} = u \cdot \frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}t}
$$

An engine with $u = 3000\,\mathrm{m/s}$ that ejects $250\,\mathrm{kg}$ of propellant per second delivers $750\,\mathrm{kN}$ of thrust.

Integrating the momentum balance over the burn time (without gravity and air resistance) gives Tsiolkovsky's **rocket equation**:

$$
\Delta v = u \cdot \ln\frac{m_0}{m_\mathrm{e}}
$$

with initial mass $m_0$ and final (burnout) mass $m_\mathrm{e}$. With $u = 3000\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and a mass ratio of $10$, $\Delta v = 3000 \cdot \ln 10 = 6.91\,\mathrm{km/s}$. Reaching Earth orbit needs about $7.8\,\mathrm{km/s}$, plus losses due to gravity and air resistance. Because of the logarithm, more propellant alone cannot achieve this, which is why **multistage rockets** are built that drop their empty tanks.
