Every electronic device consists of a large number of materials: plastic housings, circuit boards made of glass-fibre-reinforced epoxy resin, copper tracks, solder, silicon chips, glass in the display, ceramic capacitors and magnets containing rare earths. Their properties follow from structure and [chemical bonding](/en/natural-sciences/chemistry-chemical-bonding/).

## Plastics

**Plastics** (polymers) consist of very long chain molecules (**macromolecules**) built from many small units, the **monomers**. A polyethylene molecule contains thousands of ethene units. Most plastics are made from crude oil or natural gas.

### Making plastics

| Reaction             | Monomers                                        | By-product          | Examples                                    |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| **Polymerisation** (addition polymerisation) | molecules with a C=C double bond; the double bond opens and the monomers link up | none | PE, PP, PVC, PS, PTFE, PMMA |
| **Polycondensation** | molecules with two functional groups that react with elimination of a small molecule | usually $\mathrm{H_2O}$ (or HCl) | polyester (PET), polyamide (nylon), polycarbonate, phenolic resin |
| **Polyaddition**     | molecules with two reactive groups; atoms are rearranged and added | none | polyurethane (PUR), epoxy resin |

$$
n\,\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{{-}\!\left[CH_2{-}CH_2\right]_n\!{-}} \qquad \text{(ethene → polyethylene)}
$$

Polymerisation usually proceeds as a chain reaction triggered by an **initiator** (radical former).

### Thermoplastics, thermosets and elastomers

What matters for the behaviour is whether, and how strongly, the chains are linked to each other.

| Group           | Structure                                    | Behaviour on heating                          | Examples                                     |
| --------------- | -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **Thermoplastics** | linear or branched, non-crosslinked chains held together only by intermolecular forces | soften and melt, can be reshaped, welded, easily recycled | PE, PP, PVC, PS, PET, PA, PC, ABS, PMMA (acrylic glass), PLA (3D printing) |
| **Thermosets**  | closely meshed, three-dimensionally crosslinked by covalent bonds | do not melt, decompose at high temperature; hard, dimensionally stable | epoxy resin (FR-4 circuit boards), phenolic resin (Bakelite), melamine resin |
| **Elastomers**  | loosely crosslinked                          | rubber-elastic, do not melt                   | vulcanised rubber (tyres), silicone rubber, EPDM seals |

The usual circuit board material **FR-4** consists of woven glass fibre in epoxy resin with a flame retardant (FR). As a thermoset it withstands soldering temperatures without softening.

### Plastics and their uses

| Plastic                        | Abbreviation | Recycling code | Use                                              |
| ------------------------------ | ------------ | -------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| polyethylene terephthalate     | PET          | 1              | drinks bottles, films, textile fibres (polyester) |
| high-density polyethylene      | PE-HD        | 2              | canisters, pipes, bottle caps                    |
| polyvinyl chloride             | PVC          | 3              | cable insulation, window frames, pipes           |
| low-density polyethylene       | PE-LD        | 4              | films, carrier bags                              |
| polypropylene                  | PP           | 5              | housings, packaging, car parts                   |
| polystyrene                    | PS           | 6              | yoghurt pots, expanded polystyrene (EPS), insulation |
| others                         | O            | 7              | PC, ABS (keyboard and device housings), PA       |

**Additives** change the properties: plasticisers make PVC flexible, flame retardants (partly brominated, increasingly restricted in the EU) are required for electronics housings, and dyes and UV stabilisers extend service life.

### Recycling and the environment

| Method                             | Principle                                                      |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **mechanical** recycling           | sort, wash, shred, melt, reshape; works well only for single-type thermoplastics |
| **chemical** (feedstock) recycling | breaking down into monomers or oil and gas (pyrolysis), from which new plastics are made |
| **energy recovery**                | incineration with energy recovery; the material is lost        |

Plastics hardly degrade in nature. Over decades they break down into **microplastics** (particles below $5\,\mathrm{mm}$), which accumulate in water, soil and organisms. **Bioplastics** are either bio-based (made from renewable raw materials, for example PLA from corn starch) or biodegradable; the two do not automatically go together. PLA only degrades reliably in industrial composting plants.

## Metals and alloys

Metals are mostly obtained from **ores** (oxides, sulfides) by reduction. Iron, for example, is produced in the **blast furnace** with coke, which reacts to form carbon monoxide:

$$
\mathrm{Fe_2O_3 + 3\,CO \rightarrow 2\,Fe + 3\,CO_2}
$$

An **alloy** is a metallic mixture of at least two elements, at least one of them a metal. Foreign atoms hinder the sliding of lattice planes, so alloys are usually harder and stronger than the pure metals.

| Material          | Composition                              | Properties and use                                   |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **Steel**         | iron with at most $2.06\,\%$ carbon      | strong, forgeable; construction, machines            |
| **Cast iron**     | iron with more than $2.06\,\%$ carbon    | brittle, easy to cast; engine blocks                 |
| **Stainless steel** | steel with at least $10.5\,\%$ chromium, often nickel | passivates itself; kitchens, medicine, housings |
| **Brass**         | copper and zinc                          | easy to machine; connector pins, fittings            |
| **Bronze**        | copper and tin                           | corrosion-resistant; bearings, bells                 |
| **Solder** (lead-free) | e.g. SAC305: $96.5\,\%$ Sn, $3\,\%$ Ag, $0.5\,\%$ Cu | melting range about $217$ to $220\,\mathrm{°C}$ |
| **Solder** (leaded) | $\mathrm{Sn_{63}Pb_{37}}$              | eutectic, melts at $183\,\mathrm{°C}$; in the EU only permitted for exemptions under the RoHS Directive |

Important conductor metals and their electrical conductivity at $20\,\mathrm{°C}$:

| Metal     | Conductivity in $\mathrm{MS/m}$ | Note                                              |
| --------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| silver    | $62$                            | best conductivity, expensive                      |
| copper    | $58$                            | standard for cables, PCB tracks, chip interconnects |
| gold      | $45$                            | corrosion-resistant, contacts and bonding wires   |
| aluminium | $37$                            | light, overhead lines; recycling needs only about 5 % of the energy of primary production |

## Semiconductors

**Silicon** is the second most abundant element in the Earth's crust, but it occurs only in compounds, mainly as silicon dioxide (quartz sand). For chips it must be extremely pure, with fewer than one foreign atom per roughly one billion silicon atoms.

### Producing ultra-pure silicon

1. **Metallurgical silicon**: quartz is reduced with carbon in an electric arc furnace at about $2000\,\mathrm{°C}$; purity about $98$ to $99\,\%$.

   $$
   \mathrm{SiO_2 + 2\,C \rightarrow Si + 2\,CO}
   $$

2. **Trichlorosilane**: metallurgical silicon reacts with hydrogen chloride to form liquid trichlorosilane, which is purified to a very high degree by distillation.

   $$
   \mathrm{Si + 3\,HCl \rightarrow SiHCl_3 + H_2}
   $$

3. **Siemens process**: trichlorosilane is decomposed with hydrogen on silicon rods heated to about $1100\,\mathrm{°C}$. This produces polycrystalline ultra-pure silicon (purity up to $99.999999999\,\%$).

   $$
   \mathrm{SiHCl_3 + H_2 \rightarrow Si + 3\,HCl}
   $$

4. **Single crystal**: in the **Czochralski process** a seed crystal is slowly pulled, while rotating, from the silicon melt. The result is a cylindrical single crystal that is sawn into thin **wafers** (today usually $300\,\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter) and polished.

### Doping

Pure silicon hardly conducts at room temperature, because all four valence electrons are tied up in bonds to neighbouring atoms. **Doping**, the deliberate introduction of a few foreign atoms, increases conductivity by orders of magnitude:

| Doping      | Foreign atom (main group)                | Effect                                                        |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **n-type**  | phosphorus, arsenic (V, 5 valence electrons) | one extra electron is free to move (donor)                |
| **p-type**  | boron, gallium (III, 3 valence electrons) | a missing bond acts as a mobile positive **hole** (acceptor) |

At the boundary between a p-region and an n-region a **pn junction** forms, the basis of diodes, transistors, solar cells and LEDs. Besides silicon, compound semiconductors of groups III and V are used: gallium arsenide (high frequency), gallium nitride (blue LEDs, fast chargers) and silicon carbide (power electronics in electric cars).

### Chip manufacturing

A microchip is made in hundreds of process steps in a **cleanroom**, in which dust particles are strictly limited, because a single particle can interrupt a conductor track:

1. **Oxidation**: an insulating $\mathrm{SiO_2}$ layer grows on the wafer.
2. **Photolithography**: a light-sensitive photoresist is applied and exposed through a mask, today with deep ultraviolet light ($193\,\mathrm{nm}$) or extreme ultraviolet (EUV, $13.5\,\mathrm{nm}$). The exposed (or unexposed) areas are developed and removed.
3. **Etching**: where no resist protects it, the underlying layer is removed by wet chemistry or plasma.
4. **Doping** by **ion implantation**: foreign atoms are accelerated and shot into the exposed regions; the wafer is then heated to anneal it.
5. **Deposition** of further layers by chemical (CVD) or physical vapour deposition (PVD), **chemical mechanical polishing** (CMP) for planarisation.
6. **Metallisation**: several layers of copper interconnects connect the transistors.
7. Testing, sawing the wafer into individual **dies**, **packaging** (housing, bonding wires or flip chip).

Steps 2 to 6 are repeated for every layer. Semiconductor manufacturing uses a lot of energy, ultra-pure water and special chemicals such as hydrofluoric acid and fluorinated gases.

## Glass and ceramics

**Glass** is an **amorphous** solid: a solidified melt without a regular crystal lattice. It therefore has no fixed melting point but softens over a range of temperatures.

| Type of glass    | Composition                              | Use                                            |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| soda-lime glass  | $\mathrm{SiO_2}$, $\mathrm{Na_2O}$, $\mathrm{CaO}$ | windows, bottles (about 90 % of all glass) |
| borosilicate glass | with boron oxide, low thermal expansion | laboratory glassware, baking dishes           |
| fused silica (quartz glass) | pure $\mathrm{SiO_2}$         | optics, semiconductor manufacturing            |
| aluminosilicate glass, chemically strengthened | ion exchange of Na for K at the surface creates compressive stress | smartphone displays |
| optical fibre    | ultra-pure $\mathrm{SiO_2}$, core doped with germanium dioxide (higher refractive index) | optical fibre cables |

**Ceramics** consist of inorganic, non-metallic substances that are shaped and fired (sintered). Technical ceramics are hard, heat-resistant and electrically insulating: aluminium oxide for substrates and insulators, barium titanate as the dielectric in multilayer ceramic capacitors (MLCC), silicon carbide and silicon nitride for highly stressed components.

## Raw materials for electronics

| Raw material            | Use                                                                 |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| copper                  | PCB tracks, cables, motor windings                                  |
| gold, silver, palladium | contacts, bonding wires, conductive pastes                          |
| tin                     | solder                                                              |
| tantalum                | tantalum electrolytic capacitors                                    |
| indium                  | indium tin oxide (ITO), transparent electrode in touchscreens       |
| gallium, germanium      | compound semiconductors, optical fibres                             |
| lithium, cobalt, nickel, graphite | lithium-ion batteries                                     |
| **Rare earths**: neodymium, dysprosium, praseodymium | neodymium-iron-boron permanent magnets in hard disks, loudspeakers, electric motors, wind turbines |
| europium, terbium, yttrium | phosphors in displays and LEDs                                   |
| erbium                  | fibre amplifiers in optical networks                                |

The **rare earth elements** are 17 metals: scandium, yttrium and the 15 lanthanides. They are not especially rare in the Earth's crust, but rarely occur in concentrations worth mining, and separating them is chemically demanding and polluting. China recently mined about 60 % of the rare earths and processed about 85 to 90 %. The EU therefore adopted the **Critical Raw Materials Act** in 2024 to strengthen mining, processing and recycling in Europe.

Tin, tantalum, tungsten and gold are considered **conflict minerals**, because in some regions their mining finances armed groups. EU Regulation 2017/821 has required importers to carry out due diligence on their supply chain since 2021. Child labour has been documented in cobalt mining in the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

## E-waste

According to the _Global E-waste Monitor 2024_, **62 million tonnes** of e-waste were generated worldwide in 2022, of which only **22.3 %** was documented as collected and recycled. An increase to 82 million tonnes is expected by 2030. E-waste contains valuable metals (**urban mining**: a tonne of circuit boards contains considerably more gold than a tonne of gold ore), but also pollutants such as lead, mercury, cadmium, brominated flame retardants and refrigerants.

In the EU, the **WEEE Directive** 2012/19/EU governs collection and recovery; in Austria it is implemented by the **Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment Ordinance** (EAG-VO). Private individuals can hand in old devices free of charge at municipal collection points. Retailers with at least $150\,\mathrm{m^2}$ of sales area for electrical goods must take back a similar old device free of charge when a new one is bought. The **RoHS Directive** 2011/65/EU restricts hazardous substances such as lead, mercury and cadmium in new devices.

:::caution[Batteries do not belong in residual waste]
Lithium batteries and other batteries belong in battery collection. Damaged batteries in residual waste or e-waste repeatedly cause fires in refuse trucks and sorting plants. Tape over the terminals when collecting them.
:::
