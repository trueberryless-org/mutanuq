**Dynamics** explains why bodies move the way [kinematics](/en/natural-sciences/kinematics/) describes. The cause of every change in the state of motion is a **force**. In 1687, Isaac Newton formulated three principles in the _Principia_ from which the whole of classical mechanics follows.

## Force

A **force** $\vec F$ is a vector quantity with magnitude, direction and point of application. It can accelerate or deform a body. Its unit is the **newton**:

$$
1\,\mathrm{N} = 1\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot \frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s^2}}
$$

One newton accelerates $1\,\mathrm{kg}$ at $1\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. On Earth, a bar of chocolate ($100\,\mathrm{g}$) weighs about $1\,\mathrm{N}$. If several forces act, you add them as vectors to obtain the **resultant force** $\vec F_\mathrm{res} = \sum \vec F_i$. Conversely, a force can be resolved into **components**, for example parallel and perpendicular to a surface.

## Newton's laws

### First law: law of inertia

> A body remains at rest or in uniform straight-line motion as long as no resultant force acts on it.

The property of keeping the state of motion is called **inertia**; its measure is mass. That is why luggage slides forwards when you brake, and why cars need seat belts.

### Second law: law of acceleration

> The resultant force on a body equals the product of mass and acceleration.

$$
\vec F_\mathrm{res} = m \cdot \vec a
$$

More generally, $\vec F = \frac{\mathrm{d}\vec p}{\mathrm{d}t}$ with momentum $\vec p = m \vec v$ (see [Momentum and Collisions](/en/natural-sciences/momentum-and-collisions/)). The fundamental equation explains why twice the mass is accelerated only half as much by the same force.

### Third law: action and reaction

> If body A exerts a force on body B (action), B exerts a force of equal magnitude in the opposite direction on A (reaction).

$$
\vec F_\mathrm{AB} = -\vec F_\mathrm{BA}
$$

Action and reaction act on **different** bodies and therefore do not cancel. The Earth pulls an apple with its weight, and the apple pulls the Earth with the same force; because of its huge mass, however, the Earth's acceleration is immeasurably small. Rockets, recoil and walking are based on this principle.

:::caution
A common mistake: the weight and the normal force of a book on a table are **not** an action-reaction pair. Both act on the book and are only equal in magnitude in equilibrium. The counterpart to the weight is the book's pull on the Earth.
:::

### Inertial frames

Newton's laws hold in **inertial frames**, that is frames of reference that are not accelerated themselves (at rest or moving uniformly in a straight line). In a braking bus or on a merry-go-round, a body with no forces acting on it appears to accelerate. Anyone calculating in such an accelerated frame has to introduce **fictitious forces** (inertial forces), for example the outward centrifugal force $F = m\omega^2 r$ or the Coriolis force. For most engineering problems, the Earth's surface is a good approximation of an inertial frame.

## Mass and weight

| | Mass $m$ | Weight $F_\mathrm{G}$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Type | scalar, property of the body | vector, force towards the Earth's centre |
| Unit | $\mathrm{kg}$ | $\mathrm{N}$ |
| depends on location | no | yes ($g$ on the Moon is about $1.62\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$) |
| Measurement | beam balance (comparison) | spring balance, force meter |

$$
F_\mathrm{G} = m \cdot g
$$

An astronaut with a mass of $80\,\mathrm{kg}$ has the same mass on the Moon but a weight of only $80 \cdot 1.62 = 130\,\mathrm{N}$ instead of $785\,\mathrm{N}$. In everyday language, "I weigh 80 kilos" refers to mass.

## Overview of forces

### Weight

The weight $F_\mathrm{G} = mg$ acts vertically downwards (towards the Earth's centre). It is a special case of [gravitation](/en/natural-sciences/gravitation/).

### Normal force

The **normal force** $F_\mathrm{N}$ is the force with which a surface supports a body. It is perpendicular (normal) to the contact surface and exactly large enough that the body does not penetrate the surface. On level ground $F_\mathrm{N} = mg$; on an inclined plane only $mg\cos\alpha$.

### Friction

Friction arises at contact surfaces and opposes the (attempted) relative motion. Its magnitude depends on the normal force and the materials, but hardly on the size of the contact area.

| Type | Formula | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **static friction** | $F_\mathrm{H} \le \mu_\mathrm{H} F_\mathrm{N}$ | body at rest; static friction is as large as necessary, at most $\mu_\mathrm{H} F_\mathrm{N}$ |
| **kinetic (sliding) friction** | $F_\mathrm{R} = \mu_\mathrm{G} F_\mathrm{N}$ | body slides |
| **rolling resistance** | $F_\mathrm{R} = \mu_\mathrm{R} F_\mathrm{N}$ | body rolls, $\mu_\mathrm{R}$ very small |

The indices follow the German textbook notation used on this site (H for _Haftreibung_, G for _Gleitreibung_); English books often write $\mu_\mathrm{s}$ and $\mu_\mathrm{k}$. Almost always $\mu_\mathrm{R} < \mu_\mathrm{G} < \mu_\mathrm{H}$. That is why it is easier to keep a crate moving than to get it started, and why a car with ABS (wheels gripping) stops in a shorter distance than with locked wheels (wheels sliding).

| Material pair (typical values) | $\mu_\mathrm{H}$ | $\mu_\mathrm{G}$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| rubber on dry asphalt | 0.8 to 1.0 | 0.6 to 0.8 |
| rubber on wet asphalt | 0.5 to 0.7 | 0.4 to 0.6 |
| steel on steel (dry) | 0.7 | 0.6 |
| wood on wood | 0.5 | 0.3 |
| steel on ice | 0.03 | 0.01 |

### Spring force and Hooke's law

A spring is stretched by the distance $s$ when the force $F$ acts. In the elastic range, **Hooke's law** applies:

$$
F = D \cdot s
$$

The **spring constant** (stiffness) $D$ in $\mathrm{N/m}$ states how stiff the spring is; English books usually call it $k$. The restoring force of the spring is $-D s$, opposite to the displacement. A spring with $D = 200\,\mathrm{N/m}$ stretches by $2.5\,\mathrm{cm}$ under $5\,\mathrm{N}$. Force meters (spring balances) are based on this.

- In series: $\frac{1}{D} = \frac{1}{D_1} + \frac{1}{D_2}$ (softer)
- In parallel: $D = D_1 + D_2$ (stiffer)

### Centripetal force

For a body to move on a circular path, a force directed towards the centre must act, the **centripetal force**:

$$
F_\mathrm{Z} = m \frac{v^2}{r} = m \omega^2 r
$$

It is not a separate kind of force but a role taken on by an existing force: the rope tension in a chair-swing ride, the static friction of the tyres in a bend, gravitation for a satellite. If it disappears, the body flies off tangentially in a straight line (inertia).

:::tip[Example: driving round a bend]
A car drives through a bend with $r = 50\,\mathrm{m}$ at $15\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Static friction has to provide the centripetal force: $\mu_\mathrm{H} m g \ge m \frac{v^2}{r}$, so $\mu_\mathrm{H} \ge \frac{v^2}{r g} = \frac{225}{50 \cdot 9.81} = 0.46$. On dry asphalt this is enough, on ice it is not. The mass cancels: a lorry gets round the bend just as fast with the same $\mu$, as long as it does not tip over.
:::

## Free-body diagram

A **free-body diagram** is the most important tool for solving mechanics problems:

1. Mentally isolate the body from its surroundings and draw it as a point or outline.
2. Draw all forces acting **on** this body: weight, normal forces, friction, rope tensions, spring forces. No forces that the body exerts on others.
3. Choose a convenient coordinate system (for the inclined plane, parallel and perpendicular to the plane).
4. Resolve the forces into components and write $\sum F = m a$ for each axis.

## Inclined plane

A body lies on a plane with angle of inclination $\alpha$. The weight is resolved into:

$$
F_\parallel = m g \sin\alpha \quad \text{(downhill component)}, \qquad F_\perp = m g \cos\alpha \quad \text{(normal component)}
$$

![Free-body diagram on the inclined plane: weight resolved into the downhill component parallel to the plane and the normal component, plus the normal force of the plane and the friction force](/images/natural-sciences/inclined-plane_en.svg)

The normal component presses the body against the plane, so the normal force of the plane is $F_\mathrm{N} = F_\perp$. The body stays at rest as long as static friction is sufficient: $m g \sin\alpha \le \mu_\mathrm{H} m g \cos\alpha$, so

$$
\tan\alpha \le \mu_\mathrm{H}
$$

The limiting angle does not depend on the mass. This relationship is used to measure coefficients of static friction: you tilt the plane until the body starts to slide.

:::tip[Example: crate on a ramp]
A crate with $m = 10\,\mathrm{kg}$ slides down a ramp with $\alpha = 30^\circ$, coefficient of kinetic friction $\mu_\mathrm{G} = 0.2$.

- Weight: $F_\mathrm{G} = 10 \cdot 9.81 = 98.1\,\mathrm{N}$
- Downhill component: $F_\parallel = 98.1 \cdot \sin 30^\circ = 49.1\,\mathrm{N}$
- Normal force: $F_\mathrm{N} = 98.1 \cdot \cos 30^\circ = 85.0\,\mathrm{N}$
- Friction force: $F_\mathrm{R} = 0.2 \cdot 85.0 = 17.0\,\mathrm{N}$
- Resultant along the plane: $F_\mathrm{res} = 49.1 - 17.0 = 32.1\,\mathrm{N}$
- Acceleration: $a = 32.1/10 = 3.21\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$

In general: $a = g(\sin\alpha - \mu_\mathrm{G}\cos\alpha)$. Without friction it would be $g \sin 30^\circ = 4.91\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.
:::

## Further typical problems

:::tip[Example: lift]
A person with a mass of $70\,\mathrm{kg}$ stands on bathroom scales in a lift. The scales display the normal force. From $F_\mathrm{N} - m g = m a$ it follows that $F_\mathrm{N} = m(g + a)$.

| Motion | $a$ | $F_\mathrm{N}$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| at rest or constant velocity | $0$ | $687\,\mathrm{N}$ |
| starting upwards | $+2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ | $827\,\mathrm{N}$ |
| starting downwards | $-2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ | $547\,\mathrm{N}$ |
| free fall (cable breaks) | $-g$ | $0$ (weightlessness) |
:::

:::tip[Example: two bodies connected by a rope]
A trolley ($m_1 = 3\,\mathrm{kg}$) on a frictionless table is connected over a pulley to a hanging weight ($m_2 = 1\,\mathrm{kg}$). Only the weight of $m_2$ drives the system, but both masses are accelerated:

$$
a = \frac{m_2 g}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{1 \cdot 9.81}{4} = 2.45\,\mathrm{m/s^2}, \qquad F_\mathrm{rope} = m_1 a = 7.36\,\mathrm{N}
$$

The rope tension is smaller than the weight $9.81\,\mathrm{N}$ of the hanging body; otherwise it would not accelerate.
:::
