**Chemistry** studies substances, their properties and their transformations. A **substance** is a kind of matter with specific properties that stay the same under the same conditions, regardless of the shape and size of the object: a copper cable and a copper coin are made of the same substance.

## Properties of substances

Substances are identified by their **characteristic properties**. Several properties together identify a substance; a single one is rarely enough.

| Property                       | Example                                                               |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Melting and boiling point      | water: $0\,\mathrm{°C}$ and $100\,\mathrm{°C}$ at $1013.25\,\mathrm{hPa}$ |
| Density $\rho = \dfrac{m}{V}$  | water $\approx 1.00\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$, aluminium $2.70\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$, copper $8.96\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$ |
| Solubility                     | table salt dissolves well in water, oil does not                      |
| Electrical conductivity        | metals conduct, plastics insulate                                     |
| Hardness, ductility            | diamond is very hard, copper is easily shaped                         |
| Colour, odour, lustre          | sulfur is yellow, gold has a metallic lustre                          |
| Flammability                   | ethanol burns, water does not                                         |

If only the state of a substance changes (ice melts, sugar dissolves), it is a **physical change**. If a new substance with new properties forms (wood burns, iron rusts), it is a **chemical reaction**. Chemical reactions always involve a transfer of energy.

## States of matter and the particle model

According to the **particle model**, all substances consist of tiny particles (atoms, molecules or ions) that are constantly moving. The higher the temperature, the faster they move.

| State      | Arrangement of particles                  | Shape and volume                          |
| ---------- | ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| **solid**  | regular (crystal lattice), vibrate about fixed positions | fixed shape and volume          |
| **liquid** | disordered, close together, can slide past each other | fixed volume, takes the shape of the container |
| **gas**    | far apart, move freely                    | no fixed shape or volume, fills any space |

The changes of state are called:

| Change                   | Name                             |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------- |
| solid → liquid           | **melting**                      |
| liquid → solid           | **freezing** (solidification)    |
| liquid → gas             | **vaporisation** (below the boiling point: evaporation) |
| gas → liquid             | **condensation**                 |
| solid → gas              | **sublimation** (e.g. dry ice, iodine) |
| gas → solid              | **deposition** (e.g. hoar frost) |

Melting and vaporisation require energy to overcome the attractive forces between the particles. During the change the temperature therefore stays constant even though heating continues. How strong these forces are is covered on the page [Chemical Bonding](/en/natural-sciences/chemistry-chemical-bonding/#intermolecular-forces).

:::note[Density anomaly of water]
Water has its greatest density at about $4\,\mathrm{°C}$ ($0.99997\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$). Ice is less dense than liquid water and therefore floats. Lakes freeze from the top, and water of about $4\,\mathrm{°C}$ remains at the bottom, where fish can survive the winter.
:::

## Pure substances and mixtures

A **pure substance** consists of only one substance and has fixed properties, for example an exact boiling point. A **mixture** consists of several pure substances. Its properties depend on the mixing ratio, and it can be separated by physical methods.

- **Homogeneous mixtures** look uniform even under a microscope.
- **Heterogeneous mixtures** let you see the components (at least under a microscope).

| Mixture        | States of the components  | Type          | Example                       |
| -------------- | ------------------------- | ------------- | ----------------------------- |
| **Solution**   | solid, liquid or gas in liquid | homogeneous | salt water, sparkling water |
| **Alloy**      | solid in solid (metals)   | homogeneous   | brass, solder                 |
| **Gas mixture** | gas in gas               | homogeneous   | air                           |
| **Solid mixture** | solid in solid         | heterogeneous | granite, sand and iron filings |
| **Suspension** | solid in liquid           | heterogeneous | muddy water, wall paint       |
| **Emulsion**   | liquid in liquid          | heterogeneous | milk, mayonnaise, cutting fluid |
| **Foam**       | gas in liquid             | heterogeneous | soap foam                     |
| **Smoke**      | solid in gas              | heterogeneous | soot in exhaust gas           |
| **Mist** (aerosol) | liquid in gas         | heterogeneous | clouds, spray mist            |

**Air** is a gas mixture of about $78\,\%$ nitrogen, $21\,\%$ oxygen, $0.93\,\%$ argon and about $0.04\,\%$ carbon dioxide (volume fractions in dry air).

### Composition of mixtures

| Quantity             | Formula                                   | Unit            |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | --------------- |
| Mass fraction        | $w = \dfrac{m_\text{substance}}{m_\text{mixture}}$ | $1$ or $\%$ |
| Volume fraction      | $\varphi = \dfrac{V_\text{substance}}{V_\text{mixture}}$ | $1$ or $\%$ (vol) |
| Mass concentration   | $\beta = \dfrac{m_\text{substance}}{V_\text{solution}}$ | $\mathrm{g/L}$ |
| Amount concentration | $c = \dfrac{n}{V}$, see [Stoichiometry](/en/natural-sciences/chemistry-reactions-and-stoichiometry/) | $\mathrm{mol/L}$ |

:::tip[Example]
$250\,\mathrm{g}$ of solution contain $20\,\mathrm{g}$ of dissolved table salt. The mass fraction is $w = \dfrac{20\,\mathrm{g}}{250\,\mathrm{g}} = 0.08 = 8\,\%$.
:::

## Separation methods

Separation methods exploit differences in the properties of the components. The first question is therefore: how do the substances differ?

| Method                    | Property used                   | Example                                         |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| **Sieving**               | particle size                   | separating gravel from sand                     |
| **Filtration**            | particle size (solid in liquid) | coffee, water treatment                         |
| **Sedimentation, decanting** | density                      | mud settles, the liquid is poured off           |
| **Centrifugation**        | density (accelerated settling)  | separating blood into plasma and blood cells    |
| **Separating funnel**     | density, immiscible liquids     | oil from water                                  |
| **Magnetic separation**   | magnetism                       | iron scrap from waste                           |
| **Evaporation, crystallisation** | boiling point, solubility | sea salt production                            |
| **Distillation**          | boiling point                   | distilling spirits, separating crude oil into fractions |
| **Extraction**            | solubility in a solvent         | tea, decaffeinating coffee, fragrances          |
| **Adsorption**            | sticking to surfaces            | activated carbon filter, gas mask               |
| **Chromatography**        | different strength of adsorption and solubility | dyes in felt-tip pens, analysis |

### Distillation

In **distillation** a liquid mixture is heated. The component with the lower boiling point evaporates first; the vapour is condensed in a **condenser** (Liebig condenser, counter-current principle) and collected as the **distillate**. Ethanol (boiling point $78\,\mathrm{°C}$) can be concentrated this way from water ($100\,\mathrm{°C}$), but not separated completely. If the boiling points are close together, **fractional distillation** with a column is used, in which the vapour condenses and re-evaporates many times. This is how oil refineries and air separation plants (Linde process for nitrogen, oxygen and argon) work.

### Chromatography

In **chromatography** a mixture travels with a **mobile phase** (solvent or gas) over a **stationary phase** (paper, silica gel, column packing). Substances that stick more strongly to the stationary phase move more slowly. In paper chromatography the **Rf value** describes the distance travelled:

$$
R_f = \frac{\text{distance travelled by the substance}}{\text{distance travelled by the solvent front}}
$$

It lies between $0$ and $1$ and is characteristic of a substance under the same conditions. In laboratories and industry, gas chromatography (GC) and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) are used for analysis, for example in doping and food control.

### Extraction

In **extraction** a solvent selectively dissolves one component out of a mixture. When making tea, hot water extracts flavour and colour compounds from the leaves. With a separating funnel a substance is extracted from an aqueous solution into an immiscible organic solvent in which it dissolves better.

## Elements and compounds

Pure substances are either elements or compounds:

- An **element** cannot be broken down further by chemical means. It consists of atoms with the same number of protons, see [Atomic Structure](/en/natural-sciences/chemistry-atomic-structure/). There are 118 known elements, arranged in the [periodic table](/en/natural-sciences/chemistry-periodic-table/).
- A **compound** consists of at least two elements in a fixed mass ratio and can be broken down by a chemical reaction (**decomposition**, analysis). Building a compound from its elements is called **synthesis**.

Water can be decomposed into hydrogen and oxygen by electrolysis, always in the mass ratio $m(\mathrm{H}) : m(\mathrm{O}) \approx 1 : 8$. This **law of definite proportions** (Proust, 1799) was an important argument for atomic theory. The properties of a compound differ completely from those of its elements: sodium is a violently reacting metal, chlorine a toxic gas, sodium chloride is table salt.

```text
Matter
├── Pure substances
│   ├── Elements       (iron, oxygen, silicon)
│   └── Compounds      (water, table salt, silicon dioxide)
└── Mixtures
    ├── homogeneous    (solution, alloy, gas mixture)
    └── heterogeneous  (solid mixture, suspension, emulsion, ...)
```

## Laboratory safety

A few firm rules apply in the chemistry lab, because many substances are flammable, corrosive or toxic:

- Always wear **safety goggles**, and a lab coat and protective gloves where needed; tie back long hair.
- Do not eat or drink in the lab, never taste chemicals, waft odours towards you instead of sniffing directly.
- Work only with instructions and the smallest necessary amounts; never point test tubes at people while heating.
- Use the **fume hood** for toxic or foul-smelling gases.
- "Add acid to water, not water to acid": when diluting concentrated acids, slowly add the acid to the water, otherwise the heat released can make the liquid spatter.
- Dispose of chemical residues separately, not down the sink.
- Know where the fire extinguisher, fire blanket, eye wash and emergency stop are.

### GHS labelling

In the EU, hazardous substances are labelled according to the **CLP Regulation** (Regulation (EC) No 1272/2008), which implements the United Nations' worldwide **GHS** (_Globally Harmonised System_). The label shows hazard pictograms (red diamond on a white background), a **signal word** ("Danger" for severe, "Warning" for less severe hazards), **H statements** (hazard statements such as H225 "Highly flammable liquid and vapour") and **P statements** (precautionary statements such as P210 "Keep away from heat"). Detailed information is given in the **safety data sheet**, which the supplier must provide.

| Code      | Symbol                     | Meaning                                               | Example              |
| --------- | -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | -------------------- |
| **GHS01** | exploding bomb             | explosive, unstable                                   | fireworks, nitroglycerine |
| **GHS02** | flame                      | flammable (gases, liquids, solids)                    | ethanol, acetone, butane |
| **GHS03** | flame over circle          | oxidising                                             | potassium nitrate, hydrogen peroxide |
| **GHS04** | gas cylinder               | gases under pressure                                  | oxygen and nitrogen cylinders |
| **GHS05** | corrosion                  | skin corrosion, serious eye damage, corrosive to metals | hydrochloric acid, sodium hydroxide solution |
| **GHS06** | skull and crossbones       | acute toxicity (toxic to fatal)                       | methanol, mercury    |
| **GHS07** | exclamation mark           | harmful, irritant, skin sensitiser                    | isopropanol (eye irritant) |
| **GHS08** | health hazard (silhouette) | carcinogenic, mutagenic, toxic to reproduction, respiratory sensitiser | benzene, lead |
| **GHS09** | environment (dead tree and fish) | hazardous to the aquatic environment           | copper sulfate, soldering fluid containing zinc chloride |

:::caution[In the electronics workshop too]
GHS pictograms also appear on isopropanol (PCB cleaning), fluxes, contact sprays, PCB etchants (iron(III) chloride, sodium persulfate) and lithium batteries during transport. Fumes from rosin flux irritate the airways, so use fume extraction and ventilate when soldering.
:::
