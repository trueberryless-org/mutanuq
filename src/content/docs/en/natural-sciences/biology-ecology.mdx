**Ecology** (Greek _oikos_, house) studies the relationships of living things with each other and with their non-living environment. The term was coined by Ernst Haeckel in 1866. Ecological knowledge is the basis for nature conservation, agriculture and forestry, and for understanding [environmental problems and sustainability](/en/natural-sciences/biology-environment-and-sustainability/).

## Ecosystems

An **ecosystem** consists of the **biotope** (habitat with its non-living properties) and the **biocoenosis** (community of all organisms living in it). Examples are a lake, a beech forest, an alpine meadow or a bog. All individuals of a species in an area that can interbreed form a **population**.

### Abiotic factors

**Abiotic factors** are the influences of the non-living environment: light, temperature, water, humidity, wind, soil (minerals, pH), oxygen and $\mathrm{CO_2}$ content, and altitude in mountains.

Every species can tolerate a factor only within a certain range (**range of tolerance**). The **tolerance curve** shows vital activity as a function of the factor: it is highest at the **optimum**, falls off towards the **pessima** and ends at the minimum and maximum. Species with a wide range of tolerance are called **euryoecious** (generalists, such as the fox or brown rat), those with a narrow range **stenoecious** (specialists, such as the brown trout, which needs cold, oxygen-rich water). Stenoecious species are suitable as **indicator species** (bioindicators): lichens indicate clean air, certain insect larvae good water quality.

According to the **law of the minimum** (Liebig), the factor furthest from the optimum limits growth, even if all others are plentiful. If a field lacks nitrogen, extra phosphorus does not help.

### Biotic factors

**Biotic factors** are influences of other living things:

| Relationship                 | Effect for A / B  | Example                                                     |
| ---------------------------- | ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **intraspecific competition** | − / −            | spruces in a dense stand compete for light                  |
| **interspecific competition** | − / −            | foxes and birds of prey hunt mice                           |
| **predation**                | + / −             | lynx and roe deer                                           |
| **parasitism**               | + / −             | tick, tapeworm, mistletoe                                   |
| **symbiosis** (mutualism)    | + / +             | lichen (fungus and alga), mycorrhiza (fungus and tree root), flower and pollinator |
| **commensalism**             | + / 0             | scavengers feed on the remains of predators' prey           |

The **ecological niche** is the sum of all of a species' requirements of its environment and its role in the ecosystem (food, time of activity, breeding site). According to the **competitive exclusion principle**, two species with identical niches cannot coexist permanently; one displaces the other or they specialise.

## Feeding relationships and energy flow

### Food chains and food webs

Every ecosystem has three groups of organisms:

- **Producers** (plants, algae) build organic substances from inorganic ones by [photosynthesis](/en/natural-sciences/biology-biomolecules-and-metabolism/#photosynthesis).
- **Consumers** feed on other living things: herbivores are **primary consumers**, carnivores **secondary** and **tertiary consumers**.
- **Decomposers** (bacteria, fungi, soil animals such as earthworms) break down dead organic matter into minerals, which the producers take up again.

A **food chain** shows who eats whom: grass → grasshopper → shrew → tawny owl. In nature the chains are linked into **food webs**, because most animals have several sources of food. Webs with many links are more stable, because the loss of one species can be compensated.

### Trophic levels and energy flow

The steps of a food chain are called **trophic levels**. In the transition from one level to the next only a small part of the energy is built into new biomass; most is released as heat in cellular respiration, not eaten or excreted. As a rule of thumb about **10 %** of the energy reaches the next level (**ten per cent rule**).

| Trophic level            | Energy (rule of thumb) |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------- |
| producers                | $10\,000\,\mathrm{kJ}$ |
| primary consumers        | $1\,000\,\mathrm{kJ}$  |
| secondary consumers      | $100\,\mathrm{kJ}$     |
| tertiary consumers       | $10\,\mathrm{kJ}$      |

This is shown as an **energy pyramid**. It follows that food chains rarely have more than four or five links and that top predators are rare. For human nutrition it means that a kilogram of meat needs many times more plants and land than a kilogram of plant food.

**Energy flows** through the ecosystem (from the Sun to the release of heat) and must be constantly resupplied. **Matter**, by contrast, is cycled.

## Matter cycles

### Carbon cycle

| Process                         | Direction                                             |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| photosynthesis                  | $\mathrm{CO_2}$ from the air is fixed in biomass       |
| cellular respiration, decomposition | biomass becomes $\mathrm{CO_2}$                   |
| dissolution in the sea          | exchange between atmosphere and ocean; calcareous shells sink to the bottom and form limestone |
| formation of fossil fuels       | over millions of years biomass becomes coal, oil and natural gas |
| burning fossil fuels, deforestation | releases in a short time carbon that was stored for millions of years |

The large **carbon stores** are rocks, oceans, fossil deposits, soils (humus, peat bogs) and vegetation. Humans shift carbon from the long-term stores into the atmosphere and thus cause [climate change](/en/natural-sciences/biology-environment-and-sustainability/#climate-change).

### Nitrogen cycle

Nitrogen is a component of proteins and nucleic acids. Air consists of 78 % $\mathrm{N_2}$, which most living things cannot use because of its stable triple bond.

1. **Nitrogen fixation**: certain bacteria convert $\mathrm{N_2}$ into ammonium, for example root nodule bacteria (_Rhizobium_) in symbiosis with legumes (clover, beans, peas) or cyanobacteria. Lightning and the industrial Haber-Bosch process also fix nitrogen.
2. **Assimilation**: plants take up ammonium and especially nitrate and build proteins.
3. **Ammonification**: decomposers break down dead biomass and excretions into ammonium.
4. **Nitrification**: soil bacteria oxidise ammonium via nitrite to nitrate ($\mathrm{NH_4^+ \rightarrow NO_2^- \rightarrow NO_3^-}$).
5. **Denitrification**: in the absence of oxygen, bacteria reduce nitrate back to $\mathrm{N_2}$, which escapes into the air.

Excessive fertilisation leads to nitrate in groundwater and to eutrophication of lakes and rivers; it also produces the greenhouse gas nitrous oxide $\mathrm{N_2O}$.

### Water cycle

The Sun drives the water cycle: **evaporation** from seas, lakes and soils and **transpiration** by plants bring water vapour into the atmosphere; it **condenses** into clouds and falls as **precipitation**. The water runs off at the surface, seeps into the **groundwater** or is taken up by plants. Forests and unsealed soils store water and slow down floods.

## Population ecology

### Growth models

With unlimited resources a population grows **exponentially**: the growth rate is proportional to its size.

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t} = r \cdot N \quad\Rightarrow\quad N(t) = N_0 \cdot e^{r t}, \qquad T_\text{doubling} = \frac{\ln 2}{r}
$$

Bacteria that divide every 20 minutes become about $2^{30} \approx 10^9$ times as many in 10 hours ($30$ doublings). In reality food and space become scarce, and predators and diseases increase. **Logistic growth** takes into account the **carrying capacity** $K$, the largest population that the habitat can support in the long term:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t} = r \cdot N \cdot \frac{K - N}{K}
$$

The curve is S-shaped: almost exponential at first, fastest at $N = \frac{K}{2}$, then it approaches $K$. The mathematics behind it is covered on the page [Exponential and Logarithmic Functions](/en/mathematics/functions/exponential-and-logarithmic-functions/#doubling-time-and-half-life).

Factors whose effect increases with population density (competition, diseases, predators) are called **density-dependent**; weather, disasters and climate act **density-independently**. **r-strategists** (insects, mice) have many offspring without parental care and fluctuate strongly, **K-strategists** (elephants, great apes) have few offspring with intensive parental care and stay close to $K$.

### Predator-prey relationship

Alfred Lotka (1925) and Vito Volterra (1926) described the interaction between prey $x$ and predators $y$ with two coupled differential equations:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = a x - b x y \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t} = d x y - c y
$$

Here $a$ is the reproduction rate of the prey, $b$ the predation rate, $c$ the death rate of the predators and $d$ the factor by which eaten prey is converted into predator offspring. This gives the **Lotka-Volterra rules**:

1. **Periodic fluctuations**: prey and predator numbers fluctuate periodically; the predator maxima follow the prey maxima with a time lag.
2. **Constant averages**: over long periods the averages of both populations remain constant ($\bar{x} = \frac{c}{d}$, $\bar{y} = \frac{a}{b}$).
3. **Disturbance of the averages**: if both populations are reduced to the same extent (for example by an insecticide), the prey recovers faster than the predators.

The classic example is the fur purchase records of the Hudson's Bay Company for snowshoe hare and Canada lynx. In nature, however, many other factors usually play a part, such as the food supply of the prey and alternative prey species.

A simple numerical simulation with the Euler method (small time steps, changes added up) shows the offset oscillations:

<TabItem label="C#">

```csharp
using System.Globalization;

// Parameters: prey reproduction, predation rate, predator death rate, conversion
const double a = 0.1, b = 0.02, c = 0.3, d = 0.01;
const double dt = 0.001;

double prey = 40.0, predators = 9.0;

for (int t = 10; t <= 100; t += 10)
{
    // 10,000 Euler steps correspond to 10 time units
    for (int i = 0; i < 10_000; i++)
    {
        double dPrey = (a * prey - b * prey * predators) * dt;
        double dPredators = (d * prey * predators - c * predators) * dt;
        prey += dPrey;
        predators += dPredators;
    }
    Console.WriteLine(string.Create(CultureInfo.InvariantCulture,
        $"t = {t,3}: prey {prey,5:F1}, predators {predators,5:F1}"));
}
// t =  10: prey  18.0, predators   6.0
// t =  20: prey  24.0, predators   2.1
// t =  30: prey  42.9, predators   2.8
// t =  40: prey  32.1, predators  10.4
// t =  50: prey  17.8, predators   4.5
// t =  60: prey  27.6, predators   1.9
// t =  70: prey  46.2, predators   4.0
// t =  80: prey  25.1, predators  10.0
// t =  90: prey  18.7, predators   3.5
// t = 100: prey  31.8, predators   1.9
```

The predator maxima (at $t \approx 40$ and $t \approx 80$) follow the prey maxima. The simple Euler method accumulates small numerical errors, so the oscillation slowly drifts apart; more accurate methods (Runge-Kutta) avoid this.

## Biodiversity

**Biodiversity** (biological diversity) has three levels: **genetic diversity** within species, **species diversity** and **ecosystem diversity**. About two million species have been described; the actual number is many times higher. Diverse ecosystems are more stable and provide **ecosystem services**: pollination, clean water, fertile soils, protection against floods and erosion, carbon storage, raw materials and medicines.

The intergovernmental biodiversity panel **IPBES** estimated in 2019 that about one million animal and plant species are threatened with extinction. The most important direct drivers, in descending order, are: changes in land and sea use (habitat loss), direct exploitation (overfishing, hunting), climate change, pollution and **invasive species** (introduced species such as ragweed or the Asian hornet). The Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022) aims to protect 30 % of land and sea areas by 2030.

## Ecosystems in Austria

About 67,000 species live in Austria, including about 45,000 animal species and about 3,000 ferns and flowering plants. According to the environment ministry, about one third of the ferns and flowering plants and 60 % of reptiles and amphibians are endangered. There are six **national parks**: Hohe Tauern, Neusiedler See-Seewinkel, Donau-Auen, Kalkalpen, Thayatal and Gesäuse.

| Ecosystem                    | Features                                                                    |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Forest**                   | covers about half of the country's area; naturally beech and mixed forests would dominate, but spruce monocultures were often planted, which are vulnerable to bark beetles, storms and drought. Protection forests shield settlements from avalanches and debris flows |
| **Alps**                     | **altitudinal zones**: colline (deciduous forest, agriculture), montane (beech-fir-spruce forest), subalpine (spruce, larch, Swiss stone pine, mountain pine up to the tree line at about $1800$ to $2200\,\mathrm{m}$), alpine (grassland, dwarf shrubs), nival (rock, ice). The temperature falls by about $0.6\,\mathrm{°C}$ per $100\,\mathrm{m}$ of altitude. Glaciers are melting rapidly due to climate change |
| **Lake Neusiedl**            | westernmost steppe lake in Europe, very shallow (under $2\,\mathrm{m}$), large reed belt, salt pans in the Seewinkel; habitat for hundreds of bird species |
| **Danube floodplains** (Donau-Auen) | floodplain forest with regular flooding, very species-rich. The occupation of the Hainburg floodplain in 1984 against a planned power station is regarded as the birth of the Austrian environmental movement; the national park was founded in 1996 |
| **Lakes**                    | **stratified** in summer (warm surface water above cold deep water), mixing in spring and autumn. Many Alpine lakes were eutrophicated by wastewater in the 1960s and 1970s and were restored by ring sewer systems |
| **Bogs**                     | store large amounts of carbon; most of Austria's bogs have been damaged by drainage and peat extraction, and drained bogs release $\mathrm{CO_2}$ |
