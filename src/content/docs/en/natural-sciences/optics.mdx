**Optics** is the study of light. Depending on the question, light is described as a ray (geometrical optics), as a wave (wave optics) or as a stream of photons ([quantum optics](/en/natural-sciences/modern-physics/#photons)). The ray model is sufficient for mirrors, lenses and cameras; the colours of thin films, diffraction and polarisation can only be explained by the wave model.

## Light and the speed of light

Light is an electromagnetic wave with wavelengths from about $380\,\mathrm{nm}$ (violet) to $780\,\mathrm{nm}$ (red). In a vacuum it travels at the **speed of light**

$$
c = 299\,792\,458\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 3 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

Since 1983 this value has been fixed exactly and defines the metre. Light takes about $8.3\,\mathrm{min}$ from the Sun to the Earth and $1.3\,\mathrm{s}$ from the Moon. In matter, light is slower. The ratio is called the **refractive index**:

$$
n = \frac{c}{c_\mathrm{medium}} \ge 1
$$

| Medium | Refractive index $n$ (yellow light) |
| --- | --- |
| vacuum | 1 (exact) |
| air | 1.0003 (usually taken as 1) |
| water | 1.33 |
| glass (crown glass) | about 1.5 |
| core of an optical fibre | about 1.47 |
| diamond | 2.42 |

A medium with a larger $n$ is called **optically denser**.

## Reflection

When light hits a smooth surface, it is reflected. The **law of reflection** applies:

> The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. Incident ray, normal and reflected ray lie in one plane.

The angles are measured from the **normal** (perpendicular to the surface). Rough surfaces scatter light in all directions (**diffuse reflection**); that is why we can see objects that do not emit light at all.

A **plane mirror** produces a virtual, upright image of the same size, which lies as far behind the mirror as the object is in front of it.

## Refraction

When light passes into another medium, it changes direction because its speed changes. **Snell's law** states:

$$
n_1 \sin\alpha = n_2 \sin\beta
$$

$\alpha$ is the angle of incidence, $\beta$ the angle of refraction, both measured from the normal. On entering an optically denser medium, the ray bends **towards the normal**; on entering a less dense medium, it bends **away from the normal**.

:::tip[Example]
Light falls from air onto glass ($n = 1.5$) at $30^\circ$: $\sin\beta = \frac{1 \cdot \sin 30^\circ}{1.5} = 0.333$, so $\beta = 19.5^\circ$.
:::

Refraction explains why a stick in water appears bent and why a swimming pool looks shallower than it is.

### Dispersion

The refractive index depends on the wavelength: blue light is refracted more strongly than red (**dispersion**). A prism therefore splits white light into its spectral colours; a rainbow is formed by refraction and reflection in water droplets. In lenses, dispersion causes coloured fringes (chromatic aberration).

## Total internal reflection

When light passes from an optically denser into a less dense medium ($n_1 > n_2$), the angle of refraction is larger than the angle of incidence. At the **critical angle** $\alpha_\mathrm{G}$, $\beta$ would be $90^\circ$; for larger angles the light is reflected completely (**total internal reflection**):

$$
\sin\alpha_\mathrm{G} = \frac{n_2}{n_1}
$$

For glass to air, $\alpha_\mathrm{G} = \arcsin(1/1.5) = 41.8^\circ$; for water to air $48.8^\circ$. Total internal reflection is lossless, which is why binoculars use reflecting prisms instead of mirrors. The sparkle of diamonds is due to their small critical angle of $24.4^\circ$.

### Optical fibres

An **optical fibre** consists of a core with a higher refractive index and a cladding with a lower one. Light that hits the boundary at a shallow enough angle is totally reflected again and again and thus guided over kilometres. Optical fibres in networks carry data with infrared light at $850\,\mathrm{nm}$, $1310\,\mathrm{nm}$ or $1550\,\mathrm{nm}$, where the attenuation of the glass is lowest.

The **numerical aperture** states the angle $\theta_\mathrm{max}$ (from air) at which light can still be coupled in:

$$
\mathrm{NA} = \sin\theta_\mathrm{max} = \sqrt{n_\mathrm{core}^2 - n_\mathrm{cladding}^2}
$$

:::tip[Example: optical fibre]
Core $n = 1.48$, cladding $n = 1.46$: critical angle in the core $\arcsin(1.46/1.48) = 80.6^\circ$ (from the normal), $\mathrm{NA} = \sqrt{1.48^2 - 1.46^2} = 0.24$, acceptance angle $\theta_\mathrm{max} = 14.0^\circ$.
:::

| Fibre type | Core diameter | Property |
| --- | --- | --- |
| multimode | $50$ or $62.5\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ | many light paths with different travel times (modal dispersion), short distances within buildings |
| single-mode | about $9\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ | only one light path, long ranges and high bandwidths |

## Lenses

A **lens** is a transparent body with curved surfaces that converges or diverges light by refraction.

- **Converging lens** (convex, thicker in the middle): parallel incident light meets at the **focal point** $F'$. The focal length $f$ is positive.
- **Diverging lens** (concave, thinner in the middle): parallel incident light spreads out as if it came from a focal point in front of the lens. The focal length is negative.

The **optical power** $D = \frac{1}{f}$ is given in **dioptres** ($1\,\mathrm{dpt} = 1\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$). Reading glasses with $+2\,\mathrm{dpt}$ have $f = 50\,\mathrm{cm}$. For thin lenses placed close together, the optical powers add.

### Ray diagrams

For thin lenses, three principal rays are sufficient, and two of them are enough for the construction:

1. The **parallel ray** becomes a **focal ray** (passes through $F'$).
2. The **central ray** passes undeviated through the centre of the lens.
3. The **focal ray** (through $F$) becomes a **parallel ray**.

![Ray diagram for a converging lens: object G beyond twice the focal length; parallel ray, central ray and focal ray meet at the reduced, inverted image B between F' and 2F'](/images/natural-sciences/lens-image_en.svg)

### Lens equation

With object distance $g$, image distance $b$, object size $G$ and image size $B$ (the symbols used in the diagram; English books often write $d_\mathrm{o}$, $d_\mathrm{i}$, $h_\mathrm{o}$, $h_\mathrm{i}$), the **thin lens equation** and the **magnification** are:

$$
\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{g} + \frac{1}{b}, \qquad \beta = \frac{B}{G} = \frac{b}{g}
$$

A negative $b$ means a virtual image on the object side.

| Object distance | Image | Application |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $g > 2f$ | real, inverted, reduced, $f < b < 2f$ | camera, eye |
| $g = 2f$ | real, inverted, same size, $b = 2f$ | photocopier |
| $f < g < 2f$ | real, inverted, magnified, $b > 2f$ | projector, microscope objective |
| $g = f$ | no image (parallel light) | headlight, collimator |
| $g < f$ | virtual, upright, magnified | magnifying glass |

:::tip[Example: converging lens]
$f = 5\,\mathrm{cm}$, object $G = 3\,\mathrm{cm}$ at a distance of $g = 15\,\mathrm{cm}$ (as in the diagram):

$$
\frac{1}{b} = \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{15} = \frac{2}{15} \quad\Rightarrow\quad b = 7.5\,\mathrm{cm}, \qquad B = G \cdot \frac{b}{g} = 3 \cdot 0.5 = 1.5\,\mathrm{cm}
$$

The image is real, inverted and half the size. If instead the object is at $g = 3\,\mathrm{cm}$ (inside the focal length), $\frac{1}{b} = \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{3} = -\frac{2}{15}$, so $b = -7.5\,\mathrm{cm}$: a virtual, upright image magnified $2.5$ times (magnifying glass).
:::

## Concave mirrors

A **concave mirror** acts like a converging lens; for rays close to the axis its focal length is half the radius of curvature, $f = \frac{r}{2}$. The same imaging equation applies. Applications: reflecting telescopes, headlights (lamp at the focal point), shaving and make-up mirrors (face inside the focal length, magnified virtual image), solar power plants. A **convex mirror** diverges light and produces reduced, upright images with a wide field of view (rear-view mirrors, traffic mirrors).

## Optical instruments

### The eye

The cornea and the lens together form a converging lens system (around $60\,\mathrm{dpt}$, most of it from the cornea) that projects a real, inverted, reduced image onto the **retina**. The brain turns it round again. By changing the curvature of the lens, the eye adapts to different distances (**accommodation**). The **near point** (least distance of distinct vision) is $s_0 = 25\,\mathrm{cm}$.

| Defect of vision | Cause | Correction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **short-sightedness** (myopia) | eyeball too long, image forms in front of the retina | diverging lens (minus dioptres) |
| **long-sightedness** (hyperopia) | eyeball too short, image behind the retina | converging lens (plus dioptres) |
| **presbyopia** (age-related) | lens becomes stiff, near vision suffers | reading glasses, varifocals |
| **astigmatism** | cornea unevenly curved | cylindrical lens |

### Camera

A lens projects the scene onto an image sensor (CMOS). For distant objects, the image lies almost in the focal plane. Focusing means adjusting the distance between lens and sensor to the object distance. The **f-number** $k = f/D$ (focal length divided by aperture diameter) controls the amount of light: f/2.8 lets through twice as much light as f/4, because the area grows with $D^2$. A smaller aperture increases the **depth of field**.

### Magnifying glass and microscope

The **magnification** of an optical instrument is the ratio of the viewing angles with and without the instrument. A **magnifying glass** with focal length $f$ (object at the focal point) magnifies by

$$
V_\mathrm{L} = \frac{s_0}{f} = \frac{25\,\mathrm{cm}}{f}
$$

The **microscope** consists of two converging lenses: the **objective** with a short focal length produces a real, magnified intermediate image, which is viewed through the **eyepiece** as with a magnifying glass. With the optical tube length $t$ (distance between the inner focal points):

$$
V_\mathrm{M} = V_\mathrm{obj} \cdot V_\mathrm{eye} = \frac{t}{f_\mathrm{obj}} \cdot \frac{s_0}{f_\mathrm{eye}}
$$

Example: $t = 160\,\mathrm{mm}$, $f_\mathrm{obj} = 4\,\mathrm{mm}$, $f_\mathrm{eye} = 25\,\mathrm{mm}$ gives $40 \cdot 10 = 400$ times. Diffraction limits the resolution to about half the wavelength (around $0.2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ for visible light); finer structures require electron microscopes.

A **telescope** (Keplerian telescope) combines an objective with a long focal length and an eyepiece with a short one; its magnification is $V = f_\mathrm{obj}/f_\mathrm{eye}$.

## Wave optics

### Diffraction and interference at the double slit

In 1802, Thomas Young showed with the **double slit** that light is a wave: behind two narrow slits a distance $d$ apart, bright and dark fringes appear on a screen. The wavelets from both slits reaching a point at angle $\alpha$ have the path difference $\Delta s = d \sin\alpha$.

$$
\text{Maxima: } d \sin\alpha_k = k \lambda, \qquad \text{Minima: } d \sin\alpha = (2k + 1)\frac{\lambda}{2}
$$

For small angles and a screen distance $a$, the fringe spacing is $\Delta y = \frac{\lambda a}{d}$. A red laser ($\lambda = 633\,\mathrm{nm}$), $d = 0.2\,\mathrm{mm}$, $a = 2\,\mathrm{m}$ gives fringes $6.3\,\mathrm{mm}$ apart. Conversely, wavelengths are measured this way.

For a **single slit** of width $s$, the dark minima lie at $s \sin\alpha = k\lambda$ ($k = 1, 2, \dots$). The narrower the slit, the wider the diffraction pattern.

### Diffraction grating

A **grating** has very many slits a distance $d$ apart (grating constant). The maxima lie at the same positions as for the double slit but are much sharper and brighter. Gratings separate light by wavelength and are the heart of **spectrometers**.

:::tip[Example: grating]
Grating with $600$ lines per millimetre: $d = 1/600\,\mathrm{mm} = 1.667\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. Green laser light ($532\,\mathrm{nm}$):

- 1st order: $\sin\alpha_1 = 0.532/1.667 = 0.319$, $\alpha_1 = 18.6^\circ$
- 2nd order: $\sin\alpha_2 = 0.638$, $\alpha_2 = 39.7^\circ$
- 3rd order: $\sin\alpha_3 = 0.958$, $\alpha_3 = 73.3^\circ$
- 4th order: $\sin\alpha_4 > 1$, does not exist.

The tracks of a CD (spacing $1.6\,\mathrm{\mu m}$) act the same way, which is why it shimmers in rainbow colours.
:::

### Thin-film interference

In thin films (soap bubbles, oil films, anti-reflective coatings), the waves reflected at the front and back surfaces interfere. Depending on film thickness and wavelength, they reinforce or cancel each other; hence the colours. An **anti-reflective coating** of thickness $\lambda/(4n)$ cancels the reflection for one wavelength.

## Polarisation

Light is a transverse wave: the electric field oscillates perpendicular to the direction of propagation. Natural light (Sun, incandescent lamp) is **unpolarised**; the direction of oscillation changes constantly. A **polarising filter** only lets one direction of oscillation through. If linearly polarised light falls on a second filter (analyser) rotated by $\theta$, **Malus's law** applies:

$$
I = I_0 \cos^2\theta
$$

Crossed filters ($\theta = 90^\circ$) block the light completely.

Polarised light is also produced by reflection. At **Brewster's angle** $\tan\theta_\mathrm{B} = n_2/n_1$ (for glass $56.3^\circ$), the reflected light is completely polarised. Polarising sunglasses use this to filter out glare from water or roads. Further applications: LCD screens (liquid crystals rotate the direction of polarisation), 3D cinema, photoelasticity.

## The electromagnetic spectrum

Light is only a small part of the spectrum of electromagnetic waves. All of them travel at $c$ in a vacuum, $c = \lambda f$ holds for all of them, and the energy of a photon is $E = h f$.

| Range | Wavelength | Frequency | Source and application |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| radio waves | above $1\,\mathrm{m}$ | below $300\,\mathrm{MHz}$ | broadcasting, RFID |
| microwaves | $1\,\mathrm{mm}$ to $1\,\mathrm{m}$ | $300\,\mathrm{MHz}$ to $300\,\mathrm{GHz}$ | Wi-Fi ($2.4$ and $5\,\mathrm{GHz}$), mobile networks, radar, microwave oven |
| infrared | $780\,\mathrm{nm}$ to $1\,\mathrm{mm}$ | | thermal radiation, remote controls, optical fibres |
| visible light | $380$ to $780\,\mathrm{nm}$ | about $400$ to $790\,\mathrm{THz}$ | vision |
| ultraviolet | $10$ to $380\,\mathrm{nm}$ | | sunburn, disinfection, fluorescence |
| X-rays | about $10\,\mathrm{pm}$ to $10\,\mathrm{nm}$ | | medicine, materials testing |
| gamma rays | below about $10\,\mathrm{pm}$ | | radioactive decay, radiotherapy |

From the UV range upwards, the photon energy is enough to ionise atoms (**ionising radiation**); this radiation can damage genetic material. Radio, microwave and infrared radiation, by contrast, act only through heating.
