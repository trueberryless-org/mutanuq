**Genetics** studies how traits are stored, expressed and passed on to offspring. Genetic information is stored in DNA as a sequence of four bases, a code that can be described well in computing terms: four characters, words of three characters (codons), start and stop signals. The human genome comprises about $3.1$ billion base pairs per chromosome set, containing about 20,000 protein-coding genes.

## Structure of DNA

James Watson and Francis Crick published the **double helix** model in 1953, based on X-ray diffraction images by Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins.

- Two strands of **nucleotides** (deoxyribose, phosphate, base) wind around each other. Sugar and phosphate form the backbone, the bases point inwards.
- The bases pair according to **complementary base pairing**: **adenine with thymine** (two hydrogen bonds) and **guanine with cytosine** (three hydrogen bonds). If one strand is known, the other is determined.
- The strands run **antiparallel**: one runs from the 5' to the 3' end, the other the opposite way.

```text
5' - A T G C C G T A - 3'
     | | | | | | | |
3' - T A C G G C A T - 5'
```

In eukaryotic cells the DNA is wound around proteins (histones) and forms **chromatin**, which condenses into **chromosomes** before division. A **gene** is a section of DNA that contains the information for a protein or a functional RNA. Different variants of a gene are called **alleles**.

## Replication

Before each cell division the DNA is duplicated. Replication is **semi-conservative** (Meselson and Stahl, 1958): each new double strand consists of one old and one new strand.

1. The enzyme **helicase** separates the two strands (replication fork).
2. **Primase** places short RNA starter pieces (primers).
3. **DNA polymerase** reads the parent strand and adds complementary nucleotides, always in the 5'→3' direction. On the **leading strand** this happens continuously, on the **lagging strand** in pieces (Okazaki fragments).
4. Primers are replaced by DNA, and **ligase** joins the fragments.

The polymerase corrects its own errors (proofreading). In the end about one error per billion incorporated bases remains.

## Protein synthesis

The path from gene to trait goes via the protein: **DNA → mRNA → protein**.

### Transcription

In the nucleus **RNA polymerase** binds to the **promoter** of a gene, opens the double helix and makes a complementary RNA copy of the **template strand**. Uracil is incorporated instead of thymine. In eukaryotes the precursor RNA is then processed: non-coding sections (**introns**) are cut out and the coding **exons** joined (**splicing**). The finished **mRNA** (_messenger RNA_) leaves the nucleus through the nuclear pores.

### Genetic code

Every three consecutive bases of the mRNA, a **codon** (triplet), code for one amino acid. With four bases there are $4^3 = 64$ codons for 20 amino acids.

| Property                | Meaning                                                                |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **triplet code**        | three bases per amino acid                                             |
| **degenerate** (redundant) | most amino acids have several codons (leucine six, tryptophan only UGG) |
| **unambiguous**         | each codon stands for exactly one amino acid                           |
| **comma-free, non-overlapping** | read continuously without separators                           |
| **start and stop**      | AUG (methionine) starts, UAA, UAG and UGA end translation              |
| **almost universal**    | applies with few exceptions to all living things; that is why a bacterium can produce human insulin |

### Translation

At the **ribosomes** in the cytoplasm the mRNA is translated into an amino acid chain. **tRNA** molecules each carry a particular amino acid and have an **anticodon** that pairs complementarily with a codon of the mRNA. The ribosome moves on codon by codon and links the amino acids by peptide bonds until a stop codon is reached.

:::tip[Example]
| Level                         | Sequence             |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------- |
| DNA, template strand (3'→5')  | TAC CGA ACC ATT      |
| DNA, coding strand (5'→3')    | ATG GCT TGG TAA      |
| mRNA (5'→3')                  | AUG GCU UGG UAA      |
| tRNA anticodons               | UAC CGA ACC          |
| protein                       | Met – Ala – Trp – stop |

The mRNA corresponds to the coding strand, only with U instead of T.
:::

## Mutations

A **mutation** is a permanent change in the genetic information. Causes are errors in replication and cell division as well as **mutagens**: UV radiation, ionising radiation (X-rays, radioactive substances) and certain chemicals (such as benzene and substances in tobacco smoke). Mutations in body cells affect only the individual (for example skin cancer); mutations in germ cells are inherited.

| Type                        | Change                                         | Consequence, example                                |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| **Gene mutation**: point mutation (substitution) | one base is exchanged     | **silent** (same amino acid), **missense** (different amino acid, e.g. **sickle cell anaemia**: GAG → GTG, glutamic acid → valine in haemoglobin), **nonsense** (premature stop codon) |
| **Gene mutation**: frameshift mutation | insertion or deletion of bases in numbers that are not multiples of three | reading frame shifted, all following codons change; usually a non-functional protein |
| **Chromosome mutation**     | structure of a chromosome changed (loss, duplication, inversion, translocation of a segment) | e.g. cri du chat syndrome |
| **Genome mutation**         | number of chromosomes changed                  | **trisomy 21** (Down syndrome), Klinefelter syndrome (XXY); in plants often multiplication of whole sets (wheat) |

Most mutations are neutral or harmful, a few are beneficial. Together with recombination in meiosis they supply the raw material of [evolution](/en/natural-sciences/biology-evolution/).

## Mendel's laws

Gregor Mendel, an Augustinian monk in Brno, published his crossing experiments with peas in 1866. He chose traits with clear alternatives and counted the offspring, without knowing about genes or chromosomes.

**Terms**: the **genotype** is the combination of alleles (e.g. $Aa$), the **phenotype** the visible trait. **Homozygous** (pure-breeding) means two identical alleles ($AA$, $aa$), **heterozygous** two different ones ($Aa$). A **dominant** allele (capital letter) shows in the phenotype; a **recessive** one (small letter) only when it is homozygous.

### 1. Law of uniformity

If two pure-breeding parents (P generation) that differ in one trait are crossed, all offspring of the first filial generation (F1) are alike.

| P: $RR$ (red) × $ww$ (white) | $R$      | $R$      |
| ---------------------------- | -------- | -------- |
| $w$                          | $Rw$     | $Rw$     |
| $w$                          | $Rw$     | $Rw$     |

With **dominant-recessive** inheritance all F1 plants have red flowers. With **intermediate** inheritance (incomplete dominance, e.g. in the four-o'clock flower) all are pink, because both alleles act equally strongly.

### 2. Law of segregation

If the F1 hybrids are crossed with each other, the traits segregate in the F2 in a fixed numerical ratio.

| F1: $Rw$ × $Rw$ | $R$      | $w$      |
| --------------- | -------- | -------- |
| $R$             | $RR$     | $Rw$     |
| $w$             | $Rw$     | $ww$     |

- Genotypes: $RR : Rw : ww = 1 : 2 : 1$
- Phenotypes with dominant-recessive inheritance: red : white $= 3 : 1$
- Phenotypes with intermediate inheritance: red : pink : white $= 1 : 2 : 1$

### 3. Law of independent assortment

If two traits whose genes lie on different chromosomes are considered, they are inherited independently and recombined. In a **dihybrid** cross with two dominant-recessive traits (yellow $G$ dominant over green $g$, round $R$ dominant over wrinkled $r$), the cross $GgRr \times GgRr$ gives F2 phenotypes in the ratio

$$
9 : 3 : 3 : 1 \quad (\text{yellow-round} : \text{yellow-wrinkled} : \text{green-round} : \text{green-wrinkled})
$$

Genes that lie close together on the same chromosome are usually inherited together (**gene linkage**); the third law does not apply then.

:::tip[Test cross]
Whether an animal or plant with a dominant trait is $RR$ or $Rw$ is tested by crossing it with a homozygous recessive partner $ww$ (**test cross**). If white offspring appear (ratio $1 : 1$), the parent was heterozygous.
:::

## Human inheritance

Crossing experiments are impossible in humans. Inheritance is therefore studied using **pedigrees** (circle: woman, square: man, filled in: affected).

| Mode of inheritance            | Pattern in the pedigree                                     | Examples                                     |
| ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **autosomal dominant**         | appears in every generation, one affected parent is enough  | Huntington's disease, achondroplasia         |
| **autosomal recessive**        | can skip generations; healthy parents (carriers, $Aa$) can have affected children (probability $\frac{1}{4}$) | cystic fibrosis, sickle cell anaemia, phenylketonuria |
| **X-linked recessive**         | mainly affects men, because they have only one X chromosome; women are usually carriers | red-green colour vision deficiency (about 8 % of men and 0.5 % of women in Europe), haemophilia A |

### Blood groups

The **AB0 system** is determined by one gene with three alleles: $A$ and $B$ are **codominant** with each other (both are expressed), $0$ is recessive.

| Blood group | Possible genotypes  |
| ----------- | ------------------- |
| A           | $AA$, $A0$          |
| B           | $BB$, $B0$          |
| AB          | $AB$                |
| 0           | $00$                |

Parents with blood group A ($A0$) and B ($B0$) can therefore have children of all four blood groups, each with probability $\frac{1}{4}$.

## Genetic engineering

**Genetic engineering** deliberately changes the genetic material of living things. The basic tools are:

- **Restriction enzymes** cut DNA at particular base sequences; **ligases** join pieces together.
- **Plasmids** serve as vectors (vehicles) for introducing a foreign gene into bacteria. Since the 1980s bacteria have produced human **insulin** this way.
- The **polymerase chain reaction** (PCR, Kary Mullis 1983) multiplies DNA sections billions of times. It is the basis of COVID tests, paternity tests and forensic DNA analysis (**genetic fingerprinting**).
- **Gel electrophoresis** separates DNA fragments by length.
- **Sequencing** reads the base sequence. The Human Genome Project published the almost complete sequence of the human genome in 2003; the gapless sequence followed in 2022. Analysing the data is the task of **bioinformatics**.

### CRISPR/Cas9

**CRISPR/Cas9** ("gene scissors") comes from the defence system of bacteria against viruses. A short **guide RNA** leads the enzyme **Cas9** to a matching DNA site, where it cuts both strands. When repairing the cut, the cell can switch the gene off or insert a supplied template. The method is much simpler, faster and cheaper than earlier techniques. Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna received the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it. In 2023 the first CRISPR-based therapy was approved, a treatment for sickle cell anaemia and beta thalassaemia.

### Ethical questions

| Area                          | Opportunities                                   | Risks and objections                                    |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| medicine (somatic gene therapy) | curing hereditary diseases, cancer therapy, medicines | high costs, unintended changes (_off-target_ effects) |
| germline editing              | permanently preventing hereditary diseases      | changes to future generations without their consent, "designer babies"; in 2018 the birth of the first gene-edited children in China caused worldwide outrage |
| agriculture (genetically modified organisms, GMOs) | higher yields, resistance to pests and drought | outcrossing, dependence on seed companies, loss of crop diversity |
| genetic tests and genetic data | early detection, personalised medicine         | discrimination by insurers or employers, data protection |

In Austria the **Gene Technology Act** (Gentechnikgesetz, GTG) regulates work with GMOs, genetic analyses on humans and gene therapy. Austria and its federal provinces have banned the cultivation of genetically modified crops on the basis of an EU opt-out provision. In the EU, food with more than $0.9\,\%$ GMO content must be labelled. Under the GDPR, genetic data are a special category of personal data with particular protection.
