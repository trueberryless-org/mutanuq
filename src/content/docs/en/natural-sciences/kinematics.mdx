**Kinematics** describes how bodies move without asking about the cause. The causes, that is forces, are the subject of [dynamics](/en/natural-sciences/dynamics/). As long as size and rotation do not matter, the body is treated as a **point mass**.

## Position, distance and frame of reference

Motion is always relative to a **frame of reference**: a passenger sits still in the train, but for someone on the platform they move at $100\,\mathrm{km/h}$. You choose an origin and coordinate axes and describe the location of the body by its **position vector** $\vec r(t)$, or for straight-line motion by the coordinate $x(t)$.

- The **displacement** $\Delta x = x_2 - x_1$ is a vector (it can be negative).
- The **distance travelled** $s$ is the length of the actual path (always positive). If you walk 5 m forwards and 5 m back, the distance is 10 m but the displacement is 0 m.

## Velocity

The **average velocity** is displacement per time; the **instantaneous velocity** is its limit for $\Delta t \to 0$, that is the [derivative](/en/mathematics/analysis/differential-calculus/) of position with respect to time:

$$
\bar v = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}, \qquad v(t) = \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = \dot x, \qquad [v] = \mathrm{m/s}
$$

In the $s$-$t$ graph, the velocity is the **slope** of the curve. The magnitude of the velocity is called speed.

## Acceleration

**Acceleration** is the change of velocity per time:

$$
a = \frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = \ddot x, \qquad [a] = \mathrm{m/s^2}
$$

Braking is an acceleration with a negative sign (opposite to the direction of motion). Conversely, [integrating](/en/mathematics/analysis/integral-calculus/) $a(t)$ gives the velocity, and integrating $v(t)$ gives the position: the **area under the $v$-$t$ curve** is the displacement.

## Uniform motion

In **uniform straight-line motion**, $v$ is constant and $a = 0$:

$$
x(t) = x_0 + v \cdot t
$$

| Graph | Shape |
| --- | --- |
| $x$-$t$ | straight line with slope $v$ |
| $v$-$t$ | horizontal line at height $v$ |
| $a$-$t$ | on the time axis ($a = 0$) |

## Uniformly accelerated motion

For constant acceleration $a$, the **equations of motion** are:

$$
v(t) = v_0 + a t, \qquad x(t) = x_0 + v_0 t + \frac{1}{2} a t^2
$$

Eliminating time gives the **time-independent equation**:

$$
v^2 = v_0^2 + 2 a (x - x_0)
$$

| Graph | Shape |
| --- | --- |
| $x$-$t$ | parabola (opening upwards for $a > 0$) |
| $v$-$t$ | straight line with slope $a$ |
| $a$-$t$ | horizontal line at height $a$ |

:::tip[Example: accelerating from 0 to 100 km/h]
A car accelerates uniformly from $0$ to $100\,\mathrm{km/h} = 27.8\,\mathrm{m/s}$ in $8\,\mathrm{s}$. The acceleration is $a = 27.8/8 = 3.47\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, the distance covered $s = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 3.47 \cdot 8^2 = 111\,\mathrm{m}$. More simply via the area in the $v$-$t$ graph: triangle $\frac{1}{2} \cdot 8\,\mathrm{s} \cdot 27.8\,\mathrm{m/s} = 111\,\mathrm{m}$.
:::

:::tip[Example: stopping distance]
A car travels at $50\,\mathrm{km/h} = 13.9\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The reaction time is $1\,\mathrm{s}$, the braking deceleration $7\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

- Reaction distance (uniform): $s_\mathrm{R} = 13.9 \cdot 1 = 13.9\,\mathrm{m}$
- Braking distance from $0 = v_0^2 - 2 a s$: $s_\mathrm{B} = \frac{v_0^2}{2a} = \frac{13.9^2}{14} = 13.8\,\mathrm{m}$
- Stopping distance: $27.7\,\mathrm{m}$

At $100\,\mathrm{km/h}$ the reaction distance doubles and the braking distance quadruples, because it depends on $v_0$ squared.
:::

## Free fall

Without air resistance, all bodies fall equally fast with the **gravitational acceleration** $g$. Its standard value is $g_\mathrm{n} = 9.806\,65\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$; in Austria the local value is about $9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. With the axis pointing down and starting from rest:

$$
v = g t, \qquad h = \frac{1}{2} g t^2, \qquad v = \sqrt{2 g h}, \qquad t = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}
$$

:::tip[Example]
A stone falls from a height of $20\,\mathrm{m}$. Fall time $t = \sqrt{2 \cdot 20/9.81} = 2.02\,\mathrm{s}$, impact speed $v = 9.81 \cdot 2.02 = 19.8\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 71\,\mathrm{km/h}$.
:::

## Projectile motion

Projectile motion is a **superposition** of two independent motions (principle of superposition): uniform horizontally, uniformly accelerated vertically with $-g$ (axis pointing up).

### Vertical throw upwards

$$
v(t) = v_0 - g t, \qquad h(t) = v_0 t - \frac{1}{2} g t^2
$$

Rise time $t_\mathrm{S} = v_0/g$, maximum height $h_\mathrm{max} = \frac{v_0^2}{2g}$. The body returns with the same speed $v_0$ (downwards).

### Horizontal throw

Launched horizontally with $v_0$ from height $h$:

$$
x(t) = v_0 t, \qquad y(t) = h - \frac{1}{2} g t^2
$$

The path is a parabola. The flight time depends only on the height, not on $v_0$: a ball fired horizontally and one dropped at the same moment hit the ground at the same time.

:::tip[Example]
A ball rolls over the edge of a $1.25\,\mathrm{m}$ high table at $3\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Flight time $t = \sqrt{2 \cdot 1.25/9.81} = 0.505\,\mathrm{s}$, horizontal range $x = 3 \cdot 0.505 = 1.51\,\mathrm{m}$. At impact $v_y = 4.95\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $v = \sqrt{3^2 + 4.95^2} = 5.79\,\mathrm{m/s}$.
:::

### Oblique throw

Launched from the ground with $v_0$ at angle $\alpha$ to the horizontal:

$$
v_x = v_0 \cos\alpha, \qquad v_y(t) = v_0 \sin\alpha - g t
$$

$$
x(t) = v_0 \cos\alpha \cdot t, \qquad y(t) = v_0 \sin\alpha \cdot t - \frac{1}{2} g t^2
$$

| Quantity | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| time of flight | $t_\mathrm{F} = \dfrac{2 v_0 \sin\alpha}{g}$ |
| range | $w = \dfrac{v_0^2 \sin(2\alpha)}{g}$ |
| maximum height | $h_\mathrm{max} = \dfrac{v_0^2 \sin^2\alpha}{2g}$ |
| trajectory | $y = x \tan\alpha - \dfrac{g}{2 v_0^2 \cos^2\alpha} x^2$ |

The range is greatest at $\alpha = 45^\circ$, and complementary angles (such as $30^\circ$ and $60^\circ$) give the same range. With air resistance, the path is no longer a parabola and the optimum angle is smaller.

:::tip[Example]
$v_0 = 20\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $\alpha = 45^\circ$: time of flight $2.88\,\mathrm{s}$, range $\frac{400 \cdot 1}{9.81} = 40.8\,\mathrm{m}$, maximum height $\frac{400 \cdot 0.5}{19.62} = 10.2\,\mathrm{m}$.
:::

The trajectory is easy to tabulate. The program calculates time of flight, range, height and the position every $0.5\,\mathrm{s}$:

const double G = 9.81;

double v0 = 20.0;
double alpha = 45.0 * Math.PI / 180.0;
double vx = v0 * Math.Cos(alpha);
double vy = v0 * Math.Sin(alpha);

double flightTime = 2 * vy / G;
Console.WriteLine($"Time of flight: {flightTime:F2} s");
Console.WriteLine($"Range: {vx * flightTime:F2} m");
Console.WriteLine($"Maximum height: {vy * vy / (2 * G):F2} m");

for (int i = 0; i * 0.5 <= flightTime; i++)
{
    double t = i * 0.5;
    double x = vx * t;
    double y = vy * t - 0.5 * G * t * t;
    Console.WriteLine($"t = {t:F1} s: x = {x:F2} m, y = {y:F2} m");
}
// Time of flight: 2.88 s
// Range: 40.77 m
// Maximum height: 10.19 m
// t = 0.0 s: x = 0.00 m, y = 0.00 m
// t = 0.5 s: x = 7.07 m, y = 5.84 m
// t = 1.0 s: x = 14.14 m, y = 9.24 m
// t = 1.5 s: x = 21.21 m, y = 10.18 m
// t = 2.0 s: x = 28.28 m, y = 8.66 m
// t = 2.5 s: x = 35.36 m, y = 4.70 m

## Circular motion

If a body moves on a circular path of radius $r$, it is described by the angle of rotation $\varphi$ (in radians).

| Quantity | Formula | Unit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| period | $T$ | $\mathrm{s}$ |
| frequency, rotational speed | $f = \dfrac{1}{T}$ | $\mathrm{Hz} = \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ (rotational speed often in $\mathrm{min^{-1}}$, rpm) |
| angular velocity | $\omega = \dfrac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}t} = \dfrac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f$ | $\mathrm{rad/s} = \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ |
| orbital (tangential) speed | $v = \omega r = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T}$ | $\mathrm{m/s}$ |
| centripetal acceleration | $a_\mathrm{Z} = \dfrac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r$ | $\mathrm{m/s^2}$ |

In **uniform circular motion** the magnitude of the velocity stays constant, but its direction changes continuously. That is why the body is accelerated, towards the centre of the circle (**centripetal acceleration**). The force this requires is covered in [dynamics](/en/natural-sciences/dynamics/#centripetal-force).

:::tip[Example: driving round a bend]
A car drives through a bend with $r = 50\,\mathrm{m}$ at $54\,\mathrm{km/h} = 15\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The centripetal acceleration is $a_\mathrm{Z} = 15^2/50 = 4.5\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, almost half of $g$.
:::

If the magnitude of the speed changes as well, there is an additional **tangential acceleration** $a_\mathrm{T} = \alpha r$ with the angular acceleration $\alpha = \dot\omega$ in $\mathrm{rad/s^2}$ (see [Rotation](/en/natural-sciences/rotation/)).

## Relative motion and vector addition

Velocities add as vectors. If $\vec v_\mathrm{B}$ is the velocity of a boat relative to the water and $\vec v_\mathrm{W}$ the velocity of the water relative to the bank, then relative to the bank:

$$
\vec v = \vec v_\mathrm{B} + \vec v_\mathrm{W}
$$

:::tip[Example: boat on a river]
A boat travels at $4\,\mathrm{m/s}$ across the current, and the river flows at $3\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Over ground the boat moves at $v = \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = 5\,\mathrm{m/s}$, deflected downstream by $\arctan(3/4) = 36.9^\circ$. The crossing time depends only on the perpendicular component: for a width of $80\,\mathrm{m}$ it is $20\,\mathrm{s}$, however strong the current is.
:::

The **relative velocity** of B with respect to A is $\vec v_\mathrm{BA} = \vec v_\mathrm{B} - \vec v_\mathrm{A}$. Two cars travelling in the same direction at $100$ and $80\,\mathrm{km/h}$ approach each other at $20\,\mathrm{km/h}$; in opposite directions at $180\,\mathrm{km/h}$. This simple addition (Galilean transformation) only holds for $v \ll c$; close to the speed of light you need [special relativity](/en/natural-sciences/modern-physics/#special-relativity).
