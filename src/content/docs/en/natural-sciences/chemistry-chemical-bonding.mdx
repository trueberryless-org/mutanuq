Atoms bond because the bonded state is lower in energy than the separate atoms. In most cases they reach a noble gas configuration (see [valence electrons](/en/natural-sciences/chemistry-atomic-structure/#valence-electrons)). Which type of bond forms depends on the difference in [electronegativity](/en/natural-sciences/chemistry-periodic-table/#periodic-trends) $\Delta\mathrm{EN}$.

| Bonding partners         | $\Delta\mathrm{EN}$ (guide value) | Bond                          | Example               |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | --------------------- |
| non-metal + non-metal    | below 0.4                         | non-polar covalent bond       | $\mathrm{H_2}$, $\mathrm{Cl_2}$, $\mathrm{CH_4}$ |
| non-metal + non-metal    | 0.4 to 1.7                        | polar covalent bond           | $\mathrm{H_2O}$, $\mathrm{HCl}$, $\mathrm{NH_3}$ |
| metal + non-metal        | above 1.7                         | ionic bond                    | $\mathrm{NaCl}$, $\mathrm{MgO}$ |
| metal + metal            | (small)                           | metallic bond                 | Cu, Fe, brass         |

The boundaries are fluid and are given differently in different textbooks; there is a continuous range between purely covalent and purely ionic bonding.

## Ionic bonding

A metal atom gives up electrons and a non-metal atom accepts them. The resulting **cations** and **anions** attract each other electrostatically and form a regular **ionic lattice**. In the sodium chloride lattice each $\mathrm{Na^+}$ is surrounded by six $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ and vice versa (coordination number 6). There are no individual molecules; the formula $\mathrm{NaCl}$ only gives the ratio of ions.

The **empirical formula** of a salt follows from the fact that the total charge is zero:

| Ions                                    | Formula            |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------ |
| $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{Cl^-}$  | $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$  |
| $\mathrm{Al^{3+}}$ and $\mathrm{O^{2-}}$ | $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$ |
| $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and $\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}$ | $\mathrm{Na_2SO_4}$ |
| $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$ and $\mathrm{NO_3^-}$ | $\mathrm{NH_4NO_3}$ |

**Properties of salts**: high melting points (NaCl $801\,\mathrm{°C}$), because many strong attractive forces have to be overcome; hard but brittle, because shifting a lattice plane brings ions of the same charge next to each other, which then repel; non-conducting as solids, but conducting when molten or dissolved in water, because the ions can then move.

## Covalent bonding

Between non-metal atoms a **covalent bond** (electron pair bond) forms: the atoms share one or more electron pairs that belong to both nuclei. This produces **molecules**.

### Lewis structures and the octet rule

In a **Lewis structure** each line stands for an electron pair: between two atoms for a bonding pair, on one atom for a lone (non-bonding) pair. According to the **octet rule**, each atom of period 2 is surrounded by four electron pairs, hydrogen by one.

How to draw them:

1. Add up the valence electrons of all atoms (for ions take the charge into account) and divide by two: number of electron pairs.
2. Set up the skeleton; the atom with the most bonds (usually the least electronegative, never H) goes in the middle.
3. Draw single bonds and distribute the remaining pairs as lone pairs until each atom has an octet.
4. If electrons are missing for an octet, convert lone pairs into double or triple bonds.

| Molecule         | Valence electrons (pairs) | Bonds at the central atom  | Lone pairs on the central atom | Lewis structure (without lone pairs of outer atoms) |
| ---------------- | ------------------------- | -------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------- |
| $\mathrm{H_2O}$  | $6 + 2 = 8$ (4)           | 2 single bonds             | 2                              | $\mathrm{H{-}\overline{\underline{O}}{-}H}$ |
| $\mathrm{NH_3}$  | $5 + 3 = 8$ (4)           | 3 single bonds             | 1                              | $\mathrm{H{-}\overline{N}{-}H}$, third H below the N |
| $\mathrm{CH_4}$  | $4 + 4 = 8$ (4)           | 4 single bonds             | 0                              | C with four lines, each to one H |
| $\mathrm{CO_2}$  | $4 + 12 = 16$ (8)         | 2 double bonds             | 0                              | $\mathrm{O{=}C{=}O}$, 2 lone pairs on each O |
| $\mathrm{N_2}$   | $10$ (5)                  | 1 triple bond              | 1 on each N                    | $\mathrm{\lvert N{\equiv}N \rvert}$ |
| $\mathrm{HCN}$   | $1 + 4 + 5 = 10$ (5)      | 1 single, 1 triple bond    | 0 (on N: 1)                    | $\mathrm{H{-}C{\equiv}N\rvert}$ |

:::tip[Example: carbon dioxide]
C has 4 and each O has 6 valence electrons: $4 + 2 \cdot 6 = 16$ electrons, so 8 pairs. With two single bonds C would not have an octet. Only with two double bonds $\mathrm{O{=}C{=}O}$ and two lone pairs on each oxygen atom do all three atoms have an octet.
:::

There are exceptions: boron in $\mathrm{BF_3}$ has only six electrons, sulfur in $\mathrm{SF_6}$ twelve (elements from period 3 onwards can exceed the octet). In a **dative (coordinate) bond** both bonding electrons come from one partner, for example when ammonia binds a proton with its lone pair: $\mathrm{NH_3 + H^+ \rightarrow NH_4^+}$.

### Polar and non-polar bonds

With equal electronegativity (for example $\mathrm{Cl{-}Cl}$) the bonding electrons are shared evenly: a **non-polar** bond. With different EN the more electronegative atom pulls the electrons closer; it gains a **partial charge** $\delta^-$, its partner $\delta^+$. In $\mathrm{H{-}Cl}$, $\Delta\mathrm{EN} = 3.16 - 2.20 = 0.96$, so the bond is **polar**.

Whether a molecule as a whole is a **dipole** also depends on its shape: $\mathrm{CO_2}$ has two polar bonds but is linear, so the two bond dipoles cancel. $\mathrm{H_2O}$ is bent and therefore a dipole.

## Metallic bonding

In metals the atoms release their valence electrons. The positive **atomic cores** form a metal lattice; the electrons move freely between them as an **electron gas** (sea of electrons) and hold the lattice together. The model explains the typical properties of metals:

- **Electrical conductivity**: when a voltage is applied, the free electrons move in one direction. With rising temperature the cores vibrate more and hinder them, so resistance increases.
- **Thermal conductivity**: the electrons transport kinetic energy.
- **Ductility**: lattice planes can slide past each other without the bond breaking, because the electron gas binds everywhere. That is why copper wire can be drawn.
- **Metallic lustre**: the free electrons reflect light.

## Molecular geometry (VSEPR)

The **VSEPR model** (_Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion_) predicts the shape of molecules: the electron pairs around a central atom repel each other and arrange themselves as far apart as possible. Multiple bonds count as one bond; lone pairs need slightly more space than bonding pairs.

| Electron pairs (bonding + lone) | Arrangement     | Molecular shape       | Angle            | Example                 |
| ------------------------------- | --------------- | --------------------- | ---------------- | ----------------------- |
| 2 + 0                           | linear          | linear                | $180°$           | $\mathrm{CO_2}$, $\mathrm{BeCl_2}$ |
| 3 + 0                           | trigonal planar | trigonal planar       | $120°$           | $\mathrm{BF_3}$         |
| 4 + 0                           | tetrahedral     | tetrahedral           | $109.5°$         | $\mathrm{CH_4}$, $\mathrm{SiCl_4}$ |
| 3 + 1                           | tetrahedral     | trigonal pyramidal    | $\approx 107°$   | $\mathrm{NH_3}$         |
| 2 + 2                           | tetrahedral     | bent                  | $\approx 104.5°$ | $\mathrm{H_2O}$         |
| 6 + 0                           | octahedral      | octahedral            | $90°$            | $\mathrm{SF_6}$         |

## Intermolecular forces

Between molecules there are weaker attractive forces than within molecules. They determine melting and boiling points, solubility and viscosity.

| Force                          | Cause                                                         | Strength      | Example                  |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------- | ------------------------ |
| **Van der Waals forces** (London dispersion forces) | short-lived random charge shifts create induced dipoles; act between all molecules, stronger with larger surface area and more electrons | weak | alkanes, noble gases, $\mathrm{I_2}$ |
| **Dipole-dipole forces**       | attraction between permanent dipoles                          | medium        | $\mathrm{HCl}$, acetone  |
| **Hydrogen bonds**             | a strongly positive H (bound to F, O or N) attracts a lone pair of an F, O or N atom | strong (for intermolecular forces) | $\mathrm{H_2O}$, $\mathrm{NH_3}$, $\mathrm{HF}$, DNA |
| **Ion-dipole forces**          | ions attract dipole molecules (**hydration**)                 | strong        | salt dissolving in water |

All of these forces are much weaker than ionic, covalent or metallic bonds.

### Effect on boiling points

- In a homologous series the boiling point increases with molecular size, because the Van der Waals forces increase: methane $-162\,\mathrm{°C}$, propane $-42\,\mathrm{°C}$, octane $126\,\mathrm{°C}$.
- Hydrogen bonds raise the boiling point considerably. Water ($M = 18\,\mathrm{g/mol}$) boils at $100\,\mathrm{°C}$, the heavier hydrogen sulfide $\mathrm{H_2S}$ ($34\,\mathrm{g/mol}$) already at $-60\,\mathrm{°C}$. Without hydrogen bonds water would be a gas at room temperature.
- In ice the hydrogen bonds arrange the molecules in an open lattice. That is why ice is less dense than liquid water.

### Solubility

The rule of thumb is **"like dissolves like"**: polar substances and salts dissolve in polar solvents (water, **hydrophilic**), non-polar substances in non-polar solvents (petrol, oil, **lipophilic** or **hydrophobic**). Oil does not dissolve in water, because the water molecules form hydrogen bonds with each other and exclude the non-polar oil molecules. Surfactants (soaps) have a polar and a non-polar part and therefore mediate between the two. Ethanol mixes with water and with petrol because it has a polar OH group and a small non-polar part.
