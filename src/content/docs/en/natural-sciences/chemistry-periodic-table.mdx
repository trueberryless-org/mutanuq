The **periodic table of the elements** arranges all 118 known elements in order of increasing atomic number so that elements with similar properties sit below one another. It was drawn up in 1869, independently by Dmitri Mendeleev and Lothar Meyer, ordered by atomic mass. Mendeleev left gaps for elements unknown at the time and predicted their properties, for example for germanium, discovered in 1886. Today we know the order follows from [atomic structure](/en/natural-sciences/chemistry-atomic-structure/).

## Structure

- A **period** is a horizontal row. There are seven periods. The period number gives the number of occupied shells: sodium is in period 3, its electrons are spread over three shells.
- A **group** is a vertical column. According to IUPAC the 18 groups are numbered 1 to 18. Elements of a group have the same number of valence electrons and therefore similar chemical properties.
- The **main groups** (groups 1, 2 and 13 to 18, traditionally I to VIII) contain the elements in which s and p orbitals are being filled. The main group number is the number of valence electrons.
- The **subgroups** (groups 3 to 12) are the **transition metals**, in which d orbitals are being filled (iron, copper, zinc, gold).
- The **lanthanides** and **actinides** fill the f orbitals and are usually placed below the table.

| Group (IUPAC) | Main group | Name                          | Valence electrons | Examples              |
| ------------- | ---------- | ----------------------------- | ----------------- | --------------------- |
| 1             | I          | **alkali metals** (except H)  | 1                 | Li, Na, K             |
| 2             | II         | **alkaline earth metals**     | 2                 | Mg, Ca, Ba            |
| 13            | III        | boron group                   | 3                 | B, Al, Ga             |
| 14            | IV         | carbon group                  | 4                 | C, Si, Ge, Sn, Pb     |
| 15            | V          | nitrogen group (pnictogens)   | 5                 | N, P, As              |
| 16            | VI         | **chalcogens** (oxygen group) | 6                 | O, S, Se              |
| 17            | VII        | **halogens**                  | 7                 | F, Cl, Br, I          |
| 18            | VIII       | **noble gases**               | 8 (He: 2)         | He, Ne, Ar            |

A box in the periodic table contains at least the element symbol, atomic number and relative atomic mass, often also electronegativity, electron configuration and state.

### Metals, metalloids and non-metals

Around four fifths of the elements are **metals**. They are on the left and in the middle, are lustrous, conduct electricity and heat well, and are ductile. **Non-metals** are at the top right (hydrogen is a special case in group 1). Between them runs a staircase of **metalloids** such as boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony and tellurium. Silicon and germanium are semiconductors: their conductivity increases with temperature and can be changed deliberately by doping, see [Materials](/en/natural-sciences/chemistry-materials-and-plastics/#semiconductors).

## Periodic trends

Properties change regularly across a period and down a group. Two effects explain almost everything:

- **Across a period** (left to right) the nuclear charge increases while the electrons stay in the same shell. The nucleus attracts them more strongly.
- **Down a group** (top to bottom) one shell is added each time. The outer electrons are further from the nucleus and shielded by the inner electrons.

| Property                 | across period → | down group ↓ | Explanation                                 |
| ------------------------ | --------------- | ------------ | ------------------------------------------- |
| **Atomic radius**        | decreases       | increases    | stronger attraction or more shells          |
| **Ionisation energy**    | increases       | decreases    | outer electrons bound more or less tightly  |
| **Electronegativity**    | increases       | decreases    | like ionisation energy                      |
| **Metallic character**   | decreases       | increases    | metals give up electrons easily             |

The **ionisation energy** is the energy needed to remove the most weakly bound electron from a gaseous atom. It is smallest for the alkali metals (which is why they easily form $\mathrm{M^+}$ ions) and largest for the noble gases.

**Electronegativity** (EN) measures how strongly an atom in a bond attracts the bonding electrons. The common scale by Linus Pauling is dimensionless:

| Element | F    | O    | Cl   | N    | C    | H    | Si   | Al   | Na   | K    | Cs   |
| ------- | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- |
| EN      | 3.98 | 3.44 | 3.16 | 3.04 | 2.55 | 2.20 | 1.90 | 1.61 | 0.93 | 0.82 | 0.79 |

Fluorine is the most electronegative element; the alkali metals at the bottom left are the least electronegative. The EN difference decides the type of [chemical bond](/en/natural-sciences/chemistry-chemical-bonding/).

:::tip[Example]
Which atom is larger, sodium or chlorine? Both are in period 3. Chlorine has 17 protons, sodium only 11, so chlorine attracts its electrons more strongly: sodium is larger (atomic radius about $186\,\mathrm{pm}$ compared with $99\,\mathrm{pm}$).
:::

## Important groups

### Alkali metals

Lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium and francium have one valence electron, which they give up very easily. They are soft, silvery metals of low density (lithium, sodium and potassium float on water) and so reactive that they are stored under paraffin oil. With water they react to form an alkali (hydroxide solution) and hydrogen:

$$
\mathrm{2\,Na + 2\,H_2O \rightarrow 2\,NaOH + H_2}
$$

The violence of the reaction increases from lithium to caesium, because the outer electron is bound ever more loosely. Lithium is the charge carrier in [lithium-ion batteries](/en/natural-sciences/chemistry-redox-and-electrochemistry/#lithium-ion-battery); sodium occurs in table salt and sodium vapour lamps.

### Alkaline earth metals

Beryllium, magnesium, calcium, strontium, barium and radium give up two electrons and form $\mathrm{M^{2+}}$ ions. They react less violently than the alkali metals. Calcium and magnesium cause [water hardness](/en/natural-sciences/chemistry-environmental-chemistry/#water-hardness); magnesium is a light structural metal (laptop housings).

### Halogens

Fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine (and the radioactive astatine and tennessine) have seven valence electrons and readily gain one electron. As elements they exist as diatomic molecules ($\mathrm{F_2}$, $\mathrm{Cl_2}$, $\mathrm{Br_2}$, $\mathrm{I_2}$):

| Halogen  | State at room temperature | Colour           |
| -------- | ------------------------- | ---------------- |
| Fluorine | gas                       | pale yellow      |
| Chlorine | gas                       | yellow-green     |
| Bromine  | liquid                    | red-brown        |
| Iodine   | solid (sublimes)          | grey-black, violet vapour |

The name means "salt former": with metals they form salts, for example $\mathrm{2\,Na + Cl_2 \rightarrow 2\,NaCl}$. Reactivity decreases from fluorine to iodine. Chlorine disinfects drinking water and swimming pools; fluorine compounds are found in PTFE (Teflon) and toothpaste.

### Noble gases

Helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon have a full outer shell and are therefore extremely unreactive. They exist as single atoms. Argon makes up $0.93\,\%$ of air and is used as a shielding gas in welding and in light bulbs; helium cools the superconducting magnets in MRI scanners; neon glows red in neon tubes. Radon is radioactive and in some regions of Austria a relevant burden on indoor air.
