**Organic chemistry** is the chemistry of carbon compounds (excluding simple compounds such as $\mathrm{CO_2}$, carbonates and carbides). It was once believed that only living things could make such substances. In 1828 Friedrich Wöhler synthesised urea from inorganic substances and disproved this. Today more than a hundred million organic compounds are known: fuels, plastics, medicines, dyes, solvents and all biomolecules.

## Carbon

Carbon has four valence electrons and therefore forms four covalent bonds. It can form stable chains, branches and rings with itself, as well as single, double and triple bonds. This explains the enormous variety.

There are several ways of writing organic molecules:

| Notation                  | Ethanol                              |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| molecular formula         | $\mathrm{C_2H_6O}$                   |
| condensed structural formula | $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_2{-}OH}$ or $\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}$ |
| structural formula (displayed formula) | all atoms and bonds drawn |
| skeletal formula          | zigzag line, every corner and end is a C atom, H on C is omitted |

Because carbon (EN 2.55) and hydrogen (EN 2.20) have similar electronegativities, pure **hydrocarbons** are non-polar: insoluble in water but readily soluble in other non-polar substances.

## Hydrocarbons

### Alkanes

**Alkanes** contain only single bonds (**saturated**), general formula $\mathrm{C_nH_{2n+2}}$. They form a **homologous series**: each member differs from the next by a $\mathrm{CH_2}$ group. With increasing chain length, boiling point and viscosity rise because of the Van der Waals forces.

| $n$ | Name    | Formula               | Boiling point   | at room temperature |
| --- | ------- | --------------------- | --------------- | ------------------- |
| 1   | methane | $\mathrm{CH_4}$       | $-162\,\mathrm{°C}$ | gas (natural gas) |
| 2   | ethane  | $\mathrm{C_2H_6}$     | $-89\,\mathrm{°C}$  | gas             |
| 3   | propane | $\mathrm{C_3H_8}$     | $-42\,\mathrm{°C}$  | gas (LPG)       |
| 4   | butane  | $\mathrm{C_4H_{10}}$  | $-1\,\mathrm{°C}$   | gas (lighters)  |
| 5   | pentane | $\mathrm{C_5H_{12}}$  | $36\,\mathrm{°C}$   | liquid          |
| 6   | hexane  | $\mathrm{C_6H_{14}}$  | $69\,\mathrm{°C}$   | liquid          |
| 7   | heptane | $\mathrm{C_7H_{16}}$  | $98\,\mathrm{°C}$   | liquid          |
| 8   | octane  | $\mathrm{C_8H_{18}}$  | $126\,\mathrm{°C}$  | liquid (petrol) |
| 9   | nonane  | $\mathrm{C_9H_{20}}$  | $151\,\mathrm{°C}$  | liquid          |
| 10  | decane  | $\mathrm{C_{10}H_{22}}$ | $174\,\mathrm{°C}$ | liquid         |

From about 17 carbon atoms alkanes are solid (paraffin wax). Alkanes are unreactive; besides combustion, a typical reaction is **free-radical substitution** with halogens in light, in which an H atom is replaced by a halogen atom:

$$
\mathrm{CH_4 + Cl_2 \xrightarrow{\text{light}} CH_3Cl + HCl}
$$

### Alkenes and alkynes

**Alkenes** contain at least one C=C double bond ($\mathrm{C_nH_{2n}}$, ending **-ene**): ethene $\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2}$, propene. **Alkynes** contain a C≡C triple bond ($\mathrm{C_nH_{2n-2}}$, ending **-yne**): ethyne (acetylene) $\mathrm{HC{\equiv}CH}$ for welding torches. Both are **unsaturated** and more reactive than alkanes, because the multiple bond can be broken. A typical reaction is **addition**:

$$
\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2 + Br_2 \rightarrow CH_2Br{-}CH_2Br}
$$

Brown bromine water is decolourised in the process: a test for multiple bonds. Many alkenes add to themselves and form plastics, see [polymerisation](/en/natural-sciences/chemistry-materials-and-plastics/#making-plastics). The reverse, in which a small molecule is removed and a double bond forms, is called **elimination**.

### Aromatics

**Benzene** $\mathrm{C_6H_6}$ is a flat six-membered ring. The six electrons of its three double bonds are spread over the whole ring (**delocalised**), which makes the molecule particularly stable. In skeletal formulae it is therefore often drawn as a circle in a hexagon. Compounds with this ring system are called **aromatics**: toluene, phenol, naphthalene. Instead of addition they prefer substitution, because this keeps the stable ring system intact. Benzene is carcinogenic; it is present in small proportions in petrol.

## IUPAC nomenclature

Names are formed according to the rules of the **IUPAC** (_International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry_):

1. Find the longest carbon chain (for alkenes the longest chain containing the double bond): it gives the **parent name**.
2. Number the chain so that multiple bonds, functional groups and branches get the lowest possible numbers (**locants**).
3. Name side chains as **alkyl groups** (methyl, ethyl, propyl) and put them in front with their position.
4. Combine identical groups with **di-**, **tri-**, **tetra-**, and order different groups alphabetically (multiplying prefixes are ignored for this).

| Condensed formula                                         | Name                         |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH(CH_3){-}CH_2{-}CH_3}$                  | 2-methylbutane               |
| $\mathrm{CH_3{-}C(CH_3)_2{-}CH_2{-}CH(CH_3){-}CH_3}$      | 2,2,4-trimethylpentane ("isooctane", octane number 100) |
| $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH{=}CH{-}CH_3}$                          | but-2-ene                    |
| $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_2{-}CH(C_2H_5){-}CH_2{-}CH_2{-}CH_3}$  | 3-ethylhexane                |

## Isomerism

**Isomers** are compounds with the same molecular formula but a different structure and therefore different properties.

| Type                             | Description                                          | Example                                           |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| **Structural isomerism** (constitutional) | atoms connected differently                 | butane (b.p. $-1\,\mathrm{°C}$) and 2-methylpropane (b.p. $-12\,\mathrm{°C}$); ethanol and dimethyl ether |
| **cis-trans isomerism** (E/Z)    | arrangement at a double bond that cannot rotate      | cis-but-2-ene and trans-but-2-ene                  |
| **Optical isomerism** (chirality) | molecules relate to each other like an object and its mirror image, e.g. at a C with four different groups | L- and D-lactic acid; many drugs, only one form is active |

Branched isomers boil at lower temperatures because they have a smaller surface area and form weaker Van der Waals forces.

## Functional groups

A **functional group** is a group of atoms that determines the properties and reactions of a molecule. It often makes organic molecules polar and therefore soluble in water.

| Class                | Functional group                 | Suffix / prefix   | Example                                   |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------- | ----------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| **Alcohols**         | hydroxyl group $\mathrm{{-}OH}$  | -ol               | ethanol $\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}$, methanol (toxic), isopropanol (propan-2-ol) |
| **Aldehydes**        | aldehyde group $\mathrm{{-}CHO}$ | -al               | methanal (formaldehyde), ethanal          |
| **Ketones**          | carbonyl group $\mathrm{C{=}O}$ within the chain | -one | propanone (acetone), solvent           |
| **Carboxylic acids** | carboxyl group $\mathrm{{-}COOH}$ | -oic acid        | methanoic acid (formic acid), ethanoic acid (acetic acid) |
| **Esters**           | $\mathrm{{-}COO{-}}$             | -oate             | ethyl ethanoate (ethyl acetate), fats, fruit flavours |
| **Ethers**           | $\mathrm{{-}O{-}}$               | ether             | diethyl ether                             |
| **Amines**           | amino group $\mathrm{{-}NH_2}$   | -amine            | methylamine; amino acids                  |
| **Halogenoalkanes**  | $\mathrm{{-}F}$, $\mathrm{{-}Cl}$, $\mathrm{{-}Br}$ | fluoro-, chloro-, bromo- | chloromethane, CFCs     |

### Important reactions

- **Oxidation of alcohols**: primary alcohols are oxidised to aldehydes and further to carboxylic acids, secondary alcohols to ketones, tertiary alcohols not at all. This is how bacteria turn wine into vinegar in air:
  $\mathrm{C_2H_5OH \rightarrow CH_3CHO \rightarrow CH_3COOH}$
- **Esterification**: a carboxylic acid and an alcohol react with elimination of water (condensation) to form an ester. The reaction is an [equilibrium](/en/natural-sciences/chemistry-energy-and-kinetics/#law-of-mass-action):

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COOH + C_2H_5OH \rightleftharpoons CH_3COOC_2H_5 + H_2O}
$$

- The reverse reaction is called **hydrolysis**. When fats (esters of glycerol and fatty acids) are hydrolysed with sodium hydroxide, soap is formed (**saponification**).

## Fuels

**Crude oil** is a mixture of thousands of hydrocarbons. In the refinery it is separated by **fractional distillation** into fractions with different boiling ranges: gases (propane, butane), petrol, kerosene, diesel and light heating oil, heavy fuel oil and residue (bitumen). Because long-chain fractions are in less demand, they are broken into shorter molecules by **cracking**.

The **octane number** indicates the knock resistance of petrol: isooctane is defined as 100, n-heptane as 0. The usual petrol at Austrian filling stations is "Super" with at least 95 RON (research octane number).

| Fuel               | Net calorific value (guide value) | Note                                  |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| hydrogen           | $120\,\mathrm{MJ/kg}$             | burns without $\mathrm{CO_2}$         |
| natural gas (methane) | $50\,\mathrm{MJ/kg}$           | lowest $\mathrm{CO_2}$ emission per unit of energy of the fossil fuels |
| petrol, diesel     | about $42$ to $43\,\mathrm{MJ/kg}$ | about $2.3$ and $2.6\,\mathrm{kg}$ $\mathrm{CO_2}$ per litre respectively |
| ethanol            | $27\,\mathrm{MJ/kg}$              | bioethanol, petrol additive (E10)     |
| wood (air-dried)   | about $15\,\mathrm{MJ/kg}$        | renewable                             |

**Biofuels** such as bioethanol from sugar or starch and biodiesel (fatty acid methyl esters from vegetable oil) release only as much $\mathrm{CO_2}$ on combustion as the plants previously absorbed. Growing them, however, competes with food production for land.

## Combustion

In **complete combustion** a hydrocarbon reacts with sufficient oxygen to form carbon dioxide and water:

$$
\mathrm{2\,C_8H_{18} + 25\,O_2 \rightarrow 16\,CO_2 + 18\,H_2O}
$$

With a lack of oxygen, combustion is **incomplete**: the toxic, odourless **carbon monoxide** CO (it blocks haemoglobin in the blood) and soot (C) are produced. That is why gas boilers must be serviced regularly and rooms ventilated. Combustion requires three things (**fire triangle**): a fuel, oxygen and a sufficient temperature (ignition temperature). Extinguishing means removing one of them: cooling with water, smothering with $\mathrm{CO_2}$ or a fire blanket, removing the fuel. Never use water on electrical installations; use $\mathrm{CO_2}$ extinguishers.
