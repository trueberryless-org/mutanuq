Acids taste sour, are corrosive and change the colour of indicators; bases (alkalis) feel soapy and cancel the effect of acids. Acid-base theories explain these observations at the particle level. They matter for food, medicine, the environment (acid rain, lakes and rivers) and technology (lead-acid battery, etching circuit boards, cleaning agents).

## Acid-base definitions

### Arrhenius

According to **Svante Arrhenius** (1887), acids form hydrogen ions $\mathrm{H^+}$ in water and bases form hydroxide ions $\mathrm{OH^-}$:

$$
\mathrm{HCl \rightarrow H^+ + Cl^-} \qquad \mathrm{NaOH \rightarrow Na^+ + OH^-}
$$

This definition only applies to aqueous solutions and does not explain why ammonia ($\mathrm{NH_3}$, without an OH group) is basic.

### Brønsted

According to **Johannes Brønsted** and **Thomas Lowry** (1923), an acid is a **proton donor** and a base a **proton acceptor**. An acid-base reaction is a **proton transfer** (protolysis). Free protons do not exist in water; they attach to water molecules and form **oxonium ions** (hydronium ions) $\mathrm{H_3O^+}$:

$$
\mathrm{HCl + H_2O \rightarrow H_3O^+ + Cl^-}
$$

$$
\mathrm{NH_3 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons NH_4^+ + OH^-}
$$

When an acid gives up a proton, its **conjugate base** is formed. Acid and base form a **conjugate acid-base pair**: $\mathrm{HCl/Cl^-}$, $\mathrm{NH_4^+/NH_3}$, $\mathrm{H_3O^+/H_2O}$. Substances that react as an acid or a base depending on the partner are called **amphoteric** (ampholytes): water reacts with HCl as a base and with $\mathrm{NH_3}$ as an acid. $\mathrm{HCO_3^-}$ and $\mathrm{HSO_4^-}$ are also amphoteric.

## Autoionisation of water and pH

Even pure water contains ions at a low concentration, because water molecules exchange protons (**autoionisation**, autoprotolysis):

$$
\mathrm{2\,H_2O \rightleftharpoons H_3O^+ + OH^-}
$$

The **ionic product of water** at $25\,\mathrm{°C}$ is

$$
K_W = c(\mathrm{H_3O^+}) \cdot c(\mathrm{OH^-}) = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-14}\,\mathrm{mol^2/L^2}
$$

In pure water $c(\mathrm{H_3O^+}) = c(\mathrm{OH^-}) = 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{mol/L}$. Because the concentrations span many powers of ten, the negative decimal logarithm is used (Sørensen, 1909):

$$
\mathrm{pH} = -\lg \frac{c(\mathrm{H_3O^+})}{\mathrm{mol/L}} \qquad \mathrm{pOH} = -\lg \frac{c(\mathrm{OH^-})}{\mathrm{mol/L}} \qquad \mathrm{pH} + \mathrm{pOH} = 14
$$

| Solution              | pH                 |
| --------------------- | ------------------ |
| acidic                | $\mathrm{pH} < 7$  |
| neutral (at 25 °C)    | $\mathrm{pH} = 7$  |
| basic (alkaline)      | $\mathrm{pH} > 7$  |

A change of one pH unit means a tenfold change in the oxonium ion concentration.

| Substance (typical values) | pH        |
| -------------------------- | --------- |
| gastric juice              | 1 to 2    |
| lemon juice                | about 2.4 |
| cola                       | about 2.5 |
| coffee                     | about 5   |
| unpolluted rain (dissolved $\mathrm{CO_2}$) | about 5.6 |
| pure water                 | 7         |
| blood                      | 7.35 to 7.45 |
| seawater                   | about 8.1 |
| soap solution              | 9 to 10   |
| sodium hydroxide solution $c = 1\,\mathrm{mol/L}$ | 14 |

## Strong and weak acids and bases

**Strong acids** are practically completely ionised in water, **weak acids** only to a small extent; an equilibrium is established. The strength is described by the **acid dissociation constant** $K_a$ or the $\mathrm{p}K_a$ value:

$$
\mathrm{HA + H_2O \rightleftharpoons H_3O^+ + A^-} \qquad K_a = \frac{c(\mathrm{H_3O^+}) \cdot c(\mathrm{A^-})}{c(\mathrm{HA})} \qquad \mathrm{p}K_a = -\lg K_a
$$

The smaller the $\mathrm{p}K_a$ value, the stronger the acid. For a conjugate pair $\mathrm{p}K_a + \mathrm{p}K_b = 14$: a strong acid has a very weak conjugate base.

| Acid                   | Formula                 | $\mathrm{p}K_a$ | Use                                        |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------- | --------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| hydrochloric acid      | $\mathrm{HCl}$          | about $-6$      | stomach acid, metal cleaning               |
| sulfuric acid          | $\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$      | about $-3$ (1st step) | lead-acid battery, most-produced industrial chemical |
| nitric acid            | $\mathrm{HNO_3}$        | about $-1.4$    | fertilisers, explosives                    |
| phosphoric acid        | $\mathrm{H_3PO_4}$      | $2.15$ (1st step) | cola, rust converter, fertiliser         |
| acetic acid            | $\mathrm{CH_3COOH}$     | $4.76$          | vinegar, descaler                          |
| carbonic acid          | $\mathrm{H_2CO_3}$ ($\mathrm{CO_2 + H_2O}$) | $6.35$ | sparkling water, blood buffer      |
| ammonium ion           | $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$       | $9.25$          | conjugate acid of ammonia                  |

| Base                   | Formula                 | Property                                     |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| sodium hydroxide       | $\mathrm{NaOH}$         | strong, drain cleaner, soap making           |
| potassium hydroxide    | $\mathrm{KOH}$          | strong, electrolyte in alkaline batteries and NiMH cells |
| limewater              | $\mathrm{Ca(OH)_2}$     | strong but poorly soluble; mortar, test for $\mathrm{CO_2}$ |
| ammonia                | $\mathrm{NH_3}$         | weak, $\mathrm{p}K_b = 4.75$; cleaning agents, fertiliser |

Polyprotic acids release their protons step by step. This gives the names of their salts: sulfuric acid forms hydrogen sulfates ($\mathrm{HSO_4^-}$) and sulfates ($\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}$), carbonic acid hydrogen carbonates and carbonates, phosphoric acid dihydrogen phosphates, hydrogen phosphates and phosphates. Other acid anions: chloride ($\mathrm{Cl^-}$), nitrate ($\mathrm{NO_3^-}$), acetate ($\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}$).

## Calculating pH

:::tip[Example 1: strong acid]
Hydrochloric acid with $c = 0.010\,\mathrm{mol/L}$. Because HCl is completely ionised, $c(\mathrm{H_3O^+}) = 0.010\,\mathrm{mol/L} = 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{mol/L}$:

$$
\mathrm{pH} = -\lg 10^{-2} = 2
$$

If this solution is diluted tenfold with water, the pH rises to 3.
:::

:::tip[Example 2: strong base]
Sodium hydroxide solution with $c = 0.0010\,\mathrm{mol/L}$: $c(\mathrm{OH^-}) = 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol/L}$, so $\mathrm{pOH} = 3$ and

$$
\mathrm{pH} = 14 - 3 = 11
$$
:::

:::tip[Example 3: weak acid]
For weak acids (only slightly ionised), approximately

$$
\mathrm{pH} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{p}K_a - \lg \frac{c_0}{\mathrm{mol/L}}\right)
$$

Acetic acid with $c_0 = 0.10\,\mathrm{mol/L}$: $\mathrm{pH} = \frac{1}{2}(4.76 + 1) = 2.88$. Hydrochloric acid of the same concentration would have pH 1. The weak acid therefore contains about 75 times fewer oxonium ions.
:::

:::caution
pH values below 0 and above 14 are possible for very concentrated solutions, but the simple formulae no longer apply exactly there. The approximation for weak acids is only usable if less than about 5 % of the acid is ionised.
:::

## Neutralisation

When an acid is added to a base, oxonium and hydroxide ions react to form water. A salt solution is produced and heat is released (about $-57\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}$ for strong acids and bases):

$$
\mathrm{H_3O^+ + OH^- \rightarrow 2\,H_2O}
$$

$$
\mathrm{HCl + NaOH \rightarrow NaCl + H_2O} \qquad \mathrm{H_2SO_4 + 2\,NaOH \rightarrow Na_2SO_4 + 2\,H_2O}
$$

## Titration

In an **acid-base titration** the unknown concentration of a solution is determined: a **standard solution** of known concentration is added dropwise from a **burette** until the **equivalence point** is reached, at which exactly as much base as acid has been added. An indicator or a pH meter shows this point.

For monoprotic acids and bases, at the equivalence point $n(\text{acid}) = n(\text{base})$, so

$$
c_A \cdot V_A = c_B \cdot V_B
$$

:::tip[Example]
$20.0\,\mathrm{mL}$ of hydrochloric acid of unknown concentration are titrated with sodium hydroxide solution $c = 0.100\,\mathrm{mol/L}$. Up to the colour change $15.0\,\mathrm{mL}$ are used:

$$
c(\mathrm{HCl}) = \frac{0.100\,\mathrm{mol/L} \cdot 15.0\,\mathrm{mL}}{20.0\,\mathrm{mL}} = 0.0750\,\mathrm{mol/L}
$$

With sulfuric acid (diprotic) twice as much sodium hydroxide is needed: $2 \cdot n(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}) = n(\mathrm{NaOH})$.
:::

In the **titration curve** (pH against volume added), the pH rises steeply near the equivalence point. For a strong acid and strong base the equivalence point is at pH 7; for a weak acid and strong base it is in the basic range, because the salt of the weak acid is basic.

### Indicators

**Indicators** are themselves weak acids whose acid and base forms have different colours. The colour change takes place over a range of about two pH units around their $\mathrm{p}K_a$ value.

| Indicator          | Transition range | Colour acidic → basic     |
| ------------------ | ---------------- | ------------------------- |
| methyl orange      | 3.1 to 4.4       | red → yellow-orange       |
| methyl red         | 4.4 to 6.2       | red → yellow              |
| bromothymol blue   | 6.0 to 7.6       | yellow → blue             |
| phenolphthalein    | 8.2 to 10.0      | colourless → pink         |
| universal indicator | 1 to 14         | colour scale red → violet |

The indicator is chosen so that its transition range lies within the steep part of the titration curve: phenolphthalein for acetic acid with sodium hydroxide, for example.

## Buffers

A **buffer solution** keeps the pH almost constant even when small amounts of acid or base are added. It consists of a weak acid and its conjugate base in similar concentrations, for example acetic acid and sodium acetate. Added $\mathrm{H_3O^+}$ ions are taken up by $\mathrm{A^-}$, added $\mathrm{OH^-}$ ions by HA. The pH is given by the **Henderson-Hasselbalch equation**:

$$
\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a + \lg \frac{c(\mathrm{A^-})}{c(\mathrm{HA})}
$$

With equal concentrations $\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a$; an acetate buffer then has pH 4.76. **Blood** is kept within the narrow range 7.35 to 7.45 mainly by the carbonic acid-hydrogen carbonate buffer. Buffers are also used to calibrate pH meters.
