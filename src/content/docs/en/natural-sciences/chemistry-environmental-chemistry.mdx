**Environmental chemistry** studies how substances get into air, water and soil, how they are transformed there and how they affect living things. It provides the basis for environmental laws, limit values and technical solutions such as catalytic converters, sewage treatment plants and filters. The biological consequences and climate change are covered on the page [Environment and Sustainability](/en/natural-sciences/biology-environment-and-sustainability/).

## Air

Dry air consists of about $78\,\%$ nitrogen, $21\,\%$ oxygen, $0.93\,\%$ argon and about $0.04\,\%$ carbon dioxide. Trace gases in very low concentrations nevertheless have a large effect.

### Ozone layer

In the **stratosphere** (about $15$ to $35\,\mathrm{km}$ altitude) ozone $\mathrm{O_3}$ forms through high-energy UV radiation:

$$
\mathrm{O_2 \xrightarrow{\text{UV}} 2\,O} \qquad \mathrm{O + O_2 \rightarrow O_3}
$$

The **ozone layer** absorbs most of the Sun's UV-B and all of its UV-C radiation, which damages skin, eyes and genetic material. **Chlorofluorocarbons** (CFCs), formerly used as refrigerants, propellants and cleaning agents for circuit boards, rise undecomposed into the stratosphere. There UV light splits off chlorine atoms, which destroy ozone catalytically:

$$
\mathrm{Cl\cdot + O_3 \rightarrow ClO\cdot + O_2} \qquad \mathrm{ClO\cdot + O \rightarrow Cl\cdot + O_2}
$$

The chlorine atom is regenerated, so a single atom can destroy tens of thousands of ozone molecules. Every spring the **ozone hole** forms over Antarctica (discovered in 1985). The **Montreal Protocol** (1987) ended the production of CFCs worldwide. According to the scientific assessment by WMO and UNEP (2022), the ozone layer is recovering: over Antarctica a return to 1980 values is expected around 2066.

### Ground-level ozone and smog

Near the ground, ozone is a pollutant that damages the airways and plants. In strong sunlight it forms from **nitrogen oxides** ($\mathrm{NO_x}$, mainly from traffic) and volatile organic compounds (**summer smog**, photochemical smog):

$$
\mathrm{NO_2 \xrightarrow{\text{UV}} NO + O} \qquad \mathrm{O + O_2 \rightarrow O_3}
$$

In the EU and in Austria (Ozone Act) there is an information threshold of $180\,\mathrm{\mu g/m^3}$ and an alert threshold of $240\,\mathrm{\mu g/m^3}$ (one-hour mean). **Winter smog** (London smog), by contrast, forms during temperature inversions from sulfur dioxide and soot from heating. **Particulate matter** (PM10 and PM2.5, particles below $10$ and $2.5\,\mathrm{\mu m}$) from traffic, heating and industry penetrates deep into the lungs.

### Acid rain

Sulfur dioxide from burning sulfur-containing coal and oil, and nitrogen oxides react in the atmosphere to form acids:

$$
\mathrm{2\,SO_2 + O_2 \rightarrow 2\,SO_3} \qquad \mathrm{SO_3 + H_2O \rightarrow H_2SO_4}
$$

$$
\mathrm{4\,NO_2 + O_2 + 2\,H_2O \rightarrow 4\,HNO_3}
$$

In the 1980s **acid rain** damaged forests (forest dieback), acidified lakes and attacked limestone on buildings. Flue gas desulfurisation, low-sulfur fuels and catalytic converters have greatly reduced emissions in Europe.

### Greenhouse gases

The Earth's surface radiates the absorbed solar energy as infrared radiation. **Greenhouse gases** absorb part of it and also radiate it back to Earth. Without this **natural greenhouse effect** the mean surface temperature would be about $-18\,\mathrm{°C}$ instead of about $+15\,\mathrm{°C}$. Humans intensify the effect through additional emissions (**anthropogenic greenhouse effect**).

Greenhouse gases are molecules made of at least three atoms or of different atoms ($\mathrm{H_2O}$, $\mathrm{CO_2}$, $\mathrm{CH_4}$), because their dipole moment changes when they vibrate, which lets them absorb infrared radiation. $\mathrm{N_2}$ and $\mathrm{O_2}$ cannot do this.

| Gas                     | Main sources                                        | Global warming potential (GWP, 100 years) |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| carbon dioxide $\mathrm{CO_2}$ | burning fossil fuels, cement, deforestation  | $1$ (reference)                           |
| methane $\mathrm{CH_4}$ | cattle farming, rice cultivation, landfills, natural gas leaks | about $27$ to $30$             |
| nitrous oxide $\mathrm{N_2O}$ | nitrogen fertilisers, industry                | about $270$                               |
| fluorinated gases (F-gases) | refrigerants, sulfur hexafluoride $\mathrm{SF_6}$ as insulating gas in high-voltage switchgear | up to over $20\,000$ ($\mathrm{SF_6}$) |

The **global warming potential** states how strongly a gas acts over 100 years compared with the same mass of $\mathrm{CO_2}$. Emissions are therefore given in **$\mathrm{CO_2}$ equivalents**. The $\mathrm{CO_2}$ concentration of the atmosphere has risen from about $280\,\mathrm{ppm}$ before industrialisation to $410\,\mathrm{ppm}$ in 2019 (IPCC AR6) and is now above $420\,\mathrm{ppm}$.

Part of the $\mathrm{CO_2}$ dissolves in the sea and forms carbonic acid. The pH of surface water has fallen by about $0.1$ since industrialisation (**ocean acidification**); this makes it harder for mussels and corals to build their calcium carbonate shells and skeletons.

## Water

### Water hardness

**Water hardness** indicates the content of calcium and magnesium ions. It arises when rainwater containing dissolved $\mathrm{CO_2}$ seeps through limestone:

$$
\mathrm{CaCO_3 + CO_2 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons Ca^{2+} + 2\,HCO_3^-}
$$

On heating the equilibrium shifts to the left, $\mathrm{CO_2}$ escapes and lime precipitates: **limescale** in kettles, boilers and coffee machines. This part is called **carbonate hardness** (temporary hardness). **Non-carbonate hardness** (mainly sulfates) remains after boiling.

In Austria hardness is usually given in **German degrees of hardness** (°dH): $1\,\mathrm{°dH}$ corresponds to $10\,\mathrm{mg}$ of calcium oxide per litre or $0.179\,\mathrm{mmol/L}$ of alkaline earth ions.

| Hardness range | mmol/L calcium carbonate | °dH          |
| -------------- | ------------------------ | ------------ |
| soft           | below $1.5$              | below $8.4$  |
| medium         | $1.5$ to $2.5$           | $8.4$ to $14$ |
| hard           | above $2.5$              | above $14$   |

Hard water is harmless to health. Limescale is removed with acids: $\mathrm{CaCO_3 + 2\,CH_3COOH \rightarrow Ca^{2+} + 2\,CH_3COO^- + H_2O + CO_2}$. Water is softened with **ion exchangers**, which exchange calcium and magnesium ions for sodium ions (dishwashers with regeneration salt).

### Drinking water and wastewater

Austria obtains almost all of its drinking water from groundwater and springs; Vienna, for example, through its two mountain spring pipelines from the Alps. Quality is regulated by the **Drinking Water Ordinance**. Typical treatment steps are filtration, removal of iron and manganese by aeration, deacidification, activated carbon filters where needed and disinfection with UV light, chlorine or ozone.

A **municipal sewage treatment plant** cleans wastewater in three stages:

1. **Mechanical stage**: screens, grit chamber and primary settling tank remove coarse material, sand and settleable solids.
2. **Biological stage**: in the aerated **activated sludge tank** microorganisms break down organic substances into $\mathrm{CO_2}$ and water. Ammonium is oxidised to nitrate (**nitrification**) and reduced to nitrogen $\mathrm{N_2}$ in low-oxygen zones (**denitrification**).
3. **Chemical stage**: phosphate is precipitated as an insoluble salt with iron or aluminium salts.

If too many nutrients (phosphate, nitrate) from wastewater or fertiliser reach lakes and rivers, algae grow in masses. Their decomposition uses up the oxygen and fish die: **eutrophication**, see [Ecology](/en/natural-sciences/biology-ecology/).

## Soil

Soil consists of mineral particles, humus (dead organic matter), water, air and countless organisms. It filters water, stores carbon and nutrients and is the basis of agriculture. It is burdened by over-fertilisation, heavy metals (lead, cadmium, mercury), pesticides, erosion and above all by **soil sealing**: built-over areas lose all soil functions, and rainwater cannot seep away. The high land take in Austria is surveyed annually by the Environment Agency Austria and is a constant political issue.

## Toxicology

**Toxicology** studies the harmful effects of substances on living things. Its principle goes back to Paracelsus (16th century): "All things are poison, and nothing is without poison; only the dose makes a thing not a poison."

- The **dose** is given in milligrams per kilogram of body mass.
- **Acute toxicity**: effect shortly after a single intake. It is measured by the **median lethal dose** $\mathrm{LD_{50}}$, at which half of the test animals die. The smaller the value, the more toxic the substance.
- **Chronic toxicity**: effect of repeated intake of small amounts over a long period, for example lead poisoning or carcinogenic effects.
- Routes of exposure: swallowing (oral), inhalation, through the skin (dermal).

| Substance              | $\mathrm{LD_{50}}$ (rat, oral, guide value) |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| ethanol                | about $7000\,\mathrm{mg/kg}$                |
| table salt             | about $3000\,\mathrm{mg/kg}$                |
| caffeine               | about $190\,\mathrm{mg/kg}$                 |
| sodium cyanide         | about $6\,\mathrm{mg/kg}$                   |

Substances that are poorly broken down and accumulate in fatty tissue (mercury compounds, PCBs, DDT, some PFAS) become ever more concentrated along the food chain (**bioaccumulation**, **biomagnification**). Predatory fish and birds of prey are therefore particularly affected. For workplaces, Austria's **Limit Values Ordinance** sets maximum permissible concentrations in the air (occupational exposure limits).

## Green chemistry

**Green chemistry** aims to design chemicals and processes so that hazardous substances and waste are not produced in the first place. Paul Anastas and John Warner formulated twelve principles in 1998, including:

1. Preventing waste is better than treating it.
2. **Atom economy**: incorporate as many atoms of the reactants as possible into the desired product.
3. Design less hazardous syntheses and safer products.
4. Use safer solvents or avoid them (water, supercritical $\mathrm{CO_2}$).
5. Work energy-efficiently, ideally at room temperature and normal pressure.
6. Use renewable raw materials.
7. Use **catalysts** instead of stoichiometric reagents.
8. Design products so that they break down into harmless substances after use.
9. Monitor processes in real time to prevent the formation of pollutants, and minimise the risk of accidents.

In the EU the **REACH Regulation** (2007) requires chemicals to be registered and assessed before they are placed on the market: "no data, no market".
