Living things consist of about $60$ to $70\,\%$ water. The rest is mainly four classes of organic **biomolecules**: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids. They are built from a few elements (C, H, O, N, P, S) and are built up and broken down in **metabolism**. The chemical basics, such as functional groups and condensation, are covered on the page [Organic Chemistry](/en/natural-sciences/chemistry-organic-chemistry/#functional-groups).

## Carbohydrates

**Carbohydrates** (saccharides) mostly have the formula $\mathrm{C_n(H_2O)_m}$. They serve as an energy source and store, and as building material.

| Group                 | Examples                                            | Occurrence, function                                 |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **Monosaccharides** (simple sugars) | glucose (grape sugar), fructose (fruit sugar), both $\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}$; ribose, deoxyribose ($\mathrm{C_5}$) | fuel for cellular respiration, building blocks of nucleic acids |
| **Disaccharides** (double sugars) | sucrose (table sugar, from glucose and fructose), lactose (milk sugar), maltose (malt sugar) | transport and dietary sugars |
| **Polysaccharides** | **starch** (amylose, amylopectin), **glycogen**, **cellulose**, chitin | storage in plants or in liver and muscles; plant cell walls; insect exoskeletons |

Two monosaccharides join with elimination of water (**condensation**) through a **glycosidic bond**. Starch and cellulose both consist only of glucose but differ in the type of linkage ($\alpha$- or $\beta$-1,4). That is why humans can digest starch but not cellulose; it is nevertheless important as dietary fibre. Carbohydrates provide about $17\,\mathrm{kJ/g}$.

## Lipids

**Lipids** are non-polar and therefore insoluble in water.

- **Fats and oils** (triglycerides) are esters of **glycerol** and three **fatty acids**. **Saturated** fatty acids (without C=C double bonds) give solid fats such as butter, **unsaturated** ones (with double bonds) liquid oils, because the kinks at the double bonds prevent close packing. Some polyunsaturated fatty acids (omega-3, omega-6) are **essential**: the body cannot make them itself. Fats provide about $37\,\mathrm{kJ/g}$ and serve as energy store, thermal insulation and padding.
- **Phospholipids** have a polar phosphate group in place of one fatty acid and form [biomembranes](/en/natural-sciences/biology-cell/#biomembranes-and-transport).
- **Steroids** such as cholesterol (a membrane component) and the sex hormones testosterone and oestrogen.

## Proteins

**Proteins** are chains of **amino acids**. Each amino acid has an amino group ($\mathrm{{-}NH_2}$), a carboxyl group ($\mathrm{{-}COOH}$) and one of 20 different side chains. Two amino acids join with elimination of water through a **peptide bond** $\mathrm{{-}CO{-}NH{-}}$. Chains of up to about 100 amino acids are called peptides, longer ones proteins. Nine amino acids are essential for adults.

| Structure         | Description                                                                  |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Primary structure**   | order (sequence) of the amino acids, encoded in the DNA                |
| **Secondary structure** | regular folding through hydrogen bonds: $\alpha$-helix, $\beta$-pleated sheet |
| **Tertiary structure**  | overall three-dimensional shape of one chain through interactions of the side chains (disulfide bridges, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions) |
| **Quaternary structure** | assembly of several chains, e.g. haemoglobin from four subunits       |

The function of a protein depends on its three-dimensional shape. Heat, strong acids or bases and heavy metals destroy this structure (**denaturation**), for example when an egg is cooked. This is usually irreversible.

| Function          | Example                                           |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| catalysis         | enzymes such as amylase, pepsin                   |
| transport         | haemoglobin (oxygen), membrane transporters       |
| structure         | keratin (hair, nails), collagen (connective tissue) |
| movement          | actin and myosin in muscle                        |
| defence           | antibodies                                        |
| signalling        | hormones such as insulin, receptors               |

Proteins provide about $17\,\mathrm{kJ/g}$, but serve mainly as building material.

## Nucleic acids

**Nucleic acids** store and transmit genetic information. Their building blocks, the **nucleotides**, consist of a sugar (deoxyribose or ribose), a phosphate group and one of four **bases**.

| Feature       | DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid)        | RNA (ribonucleic acid)                  |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| sugar         | deoxyribose                        | ribose                                  |
| bases         | adenine, thymine, guanine, cytosine | adenine, **uracil**, guanine, cytosine |
| structure     | double strand (double helix)       | usually single strand                   |
| role          | permanent storage of genetic information | copy (mRNA), transport of amino acids (tRNA), ribosomes (rRNA) |

The structure and function of DNA are covered on the page [Genetics](/en/natural-sciences/biology-genetics/).

## Enzymes

**Enzymes** are proteins that act as **biocatalysts** and speed up reactions in the cell, often by many orders of magnitude. Like any [catalyst](/en/natural-sciences/chemistry-energy-and-kinetics/#activation-energy-and-catalysts) they lower the activation energy without being used up themselves. This allows reactions to take place at body temperature that would otherwise need high temperatures. Enzyme names mostly end in **-ase** (amylase breaks down starch, lipase fats, protease proteins).

The **substrate** binds in the **active site** of the enzyme. According to the **lock-and-key model** (Emil Fischer, 1894) only a particular substrate fits; according to the more accurate **induced-fit model** the active site adapts to the substrate when it binds.

$$
\text{enzyme} + \text{substrate} \rightleftharpoons \text{enzyme-substrate complex} \rightarrow \text{enzyme} + \text{product}
$$

Enzymes are **substrate-specific** (they recognise only certain substrates) and **reaction-specific** (they catalyse only one particular reaction).

| Factor                  | Effect                                                                  |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Temperature**         | activity first increases (van 't Hoff rule); above the optimum (about $37\,\mathrm{°C}$ in humans) the enzyme denatures and activity collapses |
| **pH**                  | every enzyme has a pH optimum: pepsin in the stomach at about 2, trypsin in the small intestine at about 8 |
| **Substrate concentration** | the rate increases until all enzymes are working at full capacity (saturation, $v_\text{max}$) |
| **Inhibitors**          | **competitive** inhibition: the inhibitor occupies the active site; **non-competitive** (allosteric) inhibition: the inhibitor changes the shape of the enzyme at another site |

Many poisons and drugs act as enzyme inhibitors: penicillin blocks an enzyme that bacteria need to build their cell wall; heavy metals such as lead and mercury block many enzymes non-specifically.

## ATP: the energy carrier of the cell

Cells store energy from food or light in the short term in **adenosine triphosphate** (ATP). When a phosphate group is split off, energy is released that drives muscle movement, active transport and syntheses:

$$
\mathrm{ATP + H_2O \rightarrow ADP + P_i} \qquad \Delta G^{\circ\prime} \approx -30.5\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}
$$

ATP is constantly regenerated from ADP and phosphate. A person turns over roughly their own body weight in ATP every day.

## Photosynthesis

Green plants, algae and cyanobacteria use light energy to build glucose from carbon dioxide and water. They are **autotrophic** and the basis of almost all food chains; the oxygen they release shaped the Earth's atmosphere.

$$
\mathrm{6\,CO_2 + 12\,H_2O \xrightarrow{\text{light, chlorophyll}} C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6\,O_2 + 6\,H_2O}
$$

Simplified: $\mathrm{6\,CO_2 + 6\,H_2O \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6\,O_2}$, $\Delta G \approx +2870\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}$ (endergonic).

Photosynthesis takes place in the **chloroplasts** in two coupled parts:

| Part                                     | Location         | Process                                                                 |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Light-dependent reactions**            | thylakoid membrane | chlorophyll absorbs light (mainly red and blue, green is reflected). Water is split (**photolysis**), $\mathrm{O_2}$ is released, ATP and NADPH are formed |
| **Light-independent reactions** (Calvin cycle) | stroma     | using ATP and NADPH, $\mathrm{CO_2}$ is fixed (enzyme RuBisCO) and reduced to glucose |

The oxygen therefore comes from the water, not from the $\mathrm{CO_2}$. The rate of photosynthesis depends on light intensity, $\mathrm{CO_2}$ concentration and temperature; the factor in shortest supply limits it.

## Cellular respiration

In **cellular respiration** glucose is completely broken down with oxygen to carbon dioxide and water. The energy released is stored in ATP. The equation is the reverse of photosynthesis:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6\,O_2 \rightarrow 6\,CO_2 + 6\,H_2O} \qquad \Delta G \approx -2870\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}
$$

| Step                           | Location                    | Result per glucose                                |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| **Glycolysis**                 | cytoplasm                   | 2 pyruvate, 2 ATP (net), 2 NADH                   |
| **Oxidative decarboxylation** (link reaction) | mitochondrial matrix | 2 acetyl-CoA, 2 $\mathrm{CO_2}$, 2 NADH  |
| **Citric acid cycle** (Krebs cycle) | mitochondrial matrix   | 4 $\mathrm{CO_2}$, 2 ATP (as GTP), 6 NADH, 2 $\mathrm{FADH_2}$ |
| **Respiratory chain** (oxidative phosphorylation) | inner mitochondrial membrane | electrons from NADH and $\mathrm{FADH_2}$ are transferred to $\mathrm{O_2}$, forming water; a proton gradient drives ATP synthase |

According to current knowledge about **30 to 32 ATP** are formed per glucose in total (older textbooks give 36 to 38). This corresponds to an efficiency of about 30 to 40 %; the rest is released as body heat.

### Fermentation

Without oxygen some cells can break down glucose only incompletely (**fermentation**). Only the 2 ATP from glycolysis are produced.

- **Alcoholic fermentation** (yeasts): $\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 \rightarrow 2\,C_2H_5OH + 2\,CO_2}$. Used for beer, wine, bread and bioethanol.
- **Lactic acid fermentation** (lactic acid bacteria, muscle cells short of oxygen): $\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 \rightarrow 2\,C_3H_6O_3}$. Used for yoghurt, sauerkraut and sourdough.

| Comparison        | Photosynthesis                     | Cellular respiration                |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| organisms         | plants, algae, cyanobacteria       | almost all living things, plants too |
| organelle         | chloroplast                        | mitochondrion                       |
| energy            | light energy → chemical energy     | chemical energy → ATP and heat      |
| substances        | uses $\mathrm{CO_2}$, releases $\mathrm{O_2}$ | uses $\mathrm{O_2}$, releases $\mathrm{CO_2}$ |
