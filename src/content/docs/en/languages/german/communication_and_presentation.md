Besides writing, the HTL German curriculum focuses on **listening and speaking**: you should be able to hold conversations, take part in discussions, give presentations and document meetings. Communication models help you understand and avoid misunderstandings. They are also typical material for the oral part of the Matura.

## Communication models

### Sender-receiver model

The **sender-receiver model** goes back to Claude Shannon and Warren Weaver (1949) and originally described the technical transmission of signals.

1.  A **sender** has a message and **encodes** it into signs (speech, writing, gestures).
2.  The message is transmitted over a **channel** (air, telephone, e-mail).
3.  **Noise** can occur (background noise, a poor connection, distraction).
4.  The **receiver** **decodes** the message and responds with **feedback**, thereby becoming a sender.

Communication only succeeds if sender and receiver share a **common set of signs** (code), such as the same language and the same technical vocabulary. A system administrator who tells a layperson „Der DNS-Resolver cacht noch den alten A-Record“ (the DNS resolver is still caching the old A record) uses a code the receiver cannot decode. The model explains technical disturbances well, but hardly explains interpersonal misunderstandings; the following models do that.

### Four-sides model (Schulz von Thun)

The Hamburg psychologist **Friedemann Schulz von Thun** published the **four-sides model** (communication square, four-ears model) in 1981. Every message contains four messages at once:

| Side                | Sender's question (mouth)           | Receiver's question (ear)                    |
| ------------------- | ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| factual content (Sachinhalt) | What am I informing about? | How should the facts be understood?         |
| self-disclosure (Selbstkundgabe) | What do I reveal about myself? | What kind of person is this? What is up with them? |
| relationship (Beziehung) | What do I think of you, how do we stand? | How is this person talking to me? Who do they think I am? |
| appeal (Appell)     | What do I want you to do?           | What should I do, think, feel?               |

The classic example: a passenger says: „Du, die Ampel ist grün.“ (Hey, the light is green.)

-   **Factual content:** the traffic light is green.
-   **Self-disclosure:** I am in a hurry. I am paying attention.
-   **Relationship:** you need my help with driving.
-   **Appeal:** drive on!

Misunderstandings arise when sender and receiver emphasise different sides. If the driver listens mainly with the relationship ear ("she thinks I am a bad driver"), she may reply irritably: „Fährst du oder fahre ich?“ (Are you driving or am I?) For conversations this means: word things deliberately and ask if a reaction does not match your intention.

### Watzlawick's five axioms

**Paul Watzlawick**, an Austrian communication theorist and psychotherapist, formulated five **axioms** (principles) of communication in 1967 together with Janet Beavin and Don Jackson:

| No. | Axiom                                                                    | Explanation and example                                                   |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | One cannot **not** communicate.                                          | All behaviour is communication. Someone who puts on headphones on the train and looks out of the window is saying: "I do not want to talk." |
| 2   | Every communication has a **content** and a **relationship** aspect, such that the latter determines the former. | Whether „Kannst du das bitte nochmal erklären?“ (could you explain that again, please?) comes across as a request or a reproach depends on the relationship and tone. |
| 3   | The nature of a relationship depends on the **punctuation** of the communication sequences. | Each person sets the start of a sequence differently: "I nag because you withdraw." and "I withdraw because you nag." |
| 4   | Human communication uses **digital** and **analogue** modalities.        | Digital: words with a fixed meaning (content). Analogue: tone of voice, facial expressions, gestures (often relationship). If they contradict each other, people usually believe the analogue. |
| 5   | Communication is either **symmetrical** or **complementary**.            | Symmetrical: partners of equal rank (two classmates). Complementary: unequal positions that complement each other (teacher and pupil, doctor and patient). |

## Conducting conversations

### Active listening

**Active listening** means showing the other person that you have understood, so that misunderstandings are recognised early:

-   show attention (eye contact, nodding, an open posture)
-   let the other person finish
-   **paraphrase:** repeat what you heard in your own words („Du meinst also, dass ...“, so you mean that ...)
-   **verbalise:** address feelings („Das hat dich offenbar geärgert.“, that obviously annoyed you)
-   ask instead of interpreting

### I-messages

**I-messages** describe your own perception and its effect instead of accusing the other person. They avoid defensiveness and justification.

| You-message                          | I-message                                                                     |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Du bist immer unpünktlich! (You are always late!) | Ich habe heute 20 Minuten gewartet. Das ärgert mich, weil wir dadurch die Präsentation nicht proben konnten. (I waited 20 minutes today. That annoys me because it meant we could not rehearse the presentation.) |
| Du hörst mir nie zu. (You never listen to me.) | Ich habe den Eindruck, dass mein Vorschlag untergegangen ist. (I have the impression my suggestion got lost.) |

A complete I-message contains the **observation**, the **effect** or feeling, and a **wish**.

### Questioning techniques

| Question type       | Feature                                         | Example                                      |
| ------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| open question       | wh-question, allows detailed answers            | Wie bist du bei der Fehlersuche vorgegangen? (How did you go about debugging?) |
| closed question     | yes/no or short facts                           | Hast du die Logdatei geprüft? (Did you check the log file?) |
| alternative question | offers a choice                                | Treffen wir uns Montag oder Dienstag?        |
| leading question    | suggests the answer, manipulative               | Sie sind doch sicher auch der Meinung, dass ...? (Surely you agree that ...?) |
| follow-up question  | clarifies what was not understood               | Was genau meinst du mit „instabil“? (What exactly do you mean by "unstable"?) |

## Feedback

**Feedback** tells someone how a behaviour or a piece of work affects others. It helps to recognise blind spots (see the **Johari window** by Luft and Ingham: areas you do not know about yourself but others perceive).

### Giving feedback

-   **describe rather than judge:** „Du hast bei den Folien 3 bis 6 vom Blatt gelesen“ (you read from your notes on slides 3 to 6) instead of „Dein Vortrag war langweilig.“ (your talk was boring)
-   **be specific:** refer to particular situations, not "always" or "never"
-   use **I-messages**
-   name **positive points** as well as suggestions for improvement
-   only address behaviour that the person can **change**
-   give it **promptly** and only when it is wanted

### Receiving feedback

-   listen, let the other person finish, do not justify yourself immediately
-   ask if something is unclear
-   say thank you and decide for yourself what you will act on

## Presentation technique

### Preparation

1.  **Clarify the aim and audience:** what should the audience know or do afterwards? What do they already know?
2.  Formulate the **key message** in one sentence.
3.  **Select content:** a few points covered thoroughly are better than everything superficially.
4.  **Structure** the talk, create slides or media, write cue cards.
5.  **Rehearse** aloud and with a timer, ideally in front of an audience.

### Structure

| Part         | Share (guideline)  | Content                                                               |
| ------------ | ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| introduction | approx. 10 to 15 % | greeting, hook (question, figure, quotation, experience), topic, overview of the talk |
| main part    | approx. 75 to 80 % | content in a logical order, announce transitions, examples            |
| conclusion   | approx. 10 %       | summary of the key points, outlook or appeal, thanks, invitation to ask questions |

The introduction decides whether you gain attention; the conclusion is what people remember best. Prepare both particularly well. For persuasive presentations, the [speech structure models of the opinion speech](/en/languages/german_text_types/opinion_speech/#speech-structure-models) are useful.

### Slides

-   **one message per slide**, with a meaningful heading
-   **little text:** keywords instead of full sentences, no walls of text
-   **large font** (body text at least about 24 pt), sans-serif font, strong contrast
-   **pictures, diagrams and schematics** instead of tables full of figures; only show what you explain
-   **consistent design**, few animations
-   give **sources** for other people's images and data
-   slides support the talk, they do not replace it: never read from your slides

### Body language and voice

| Area         | Recommendation                                                         |
| ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| stance       | stand firmly on both feet, hip-width apart, facing the audience        |
| eye contact  | look around the whole room, look briefly at individuals, do not talk to the screen |
| gestures     | hands visible above the waist, gestures underline statements; not in your pockets, not crossed |
| facial expression | friendly, matching the content                                    |
| voice        | loud and clear, vary the pace, pause deliberately                      |
| language     | short sentences, speak freely with cue cards, avoid fillers („ähm“, „sozusagen“) |

### Stage fright

Stage fright is a normal stress reaction that can even improve concentration. These things help:

-   **thorough preparation** and rehearsing several times, especially the first sentences
-   **check the technology beforehand** (projector, adapter, file available offline)
-   **breathe calmly**, pause briefly before the first sentence
-   remind yourself that the audience hardly notices your nervousness
-   if your mind goes blank: look at your cue card, briefly summarise what you last said, carry on

### Answering questions

Listen to the whole question, repeat it for everyone if necessary, and answer briefly and honestly. If you do not know something, admit it and offer to provide the answer later.

## Discussion and debate

### Discussion

In a **discussion**, several people exchange opinions and arguments on a topic in order to clarify a problem or find a solution.

**Rules:** let others finish, stay factual, respond to previous speakers, stay on topic, share speaking time fairly, use arguments instead of personal attacks.

**Moderation:** the moderator opens the discussion, introduces the topic and participants, gives people the floor, watches the time and the rules, summarises interim results and closes the discussion with a conclusion.

You build **arguments** using the pattern **claim, reason, evidence (example)**. Avoid **killer phrases** that stifle discussion without arguing („Das haben wir immer schon so gemacht.“, we have always done it this way; „Das funktioniert nie.“, that will never work).

| Discussion format      | Feature                                                                  |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| panel discussion       | experts discuss in front of an audience, which can ask questions at the end |
| pro and contra discussion | two groups represent opposing positions                               |
| fishbowl               | an inner circle discusses; an empty chair allows listeners to join briefly |
| round-table discussion | participants of equal rank without an audience                          |

### Debate

A **debate** is a strictly regulated form of argument about a yes/no question („Soll ...?“, should ...?). The pro and contra sides have fixed speaking times. A common procedure (for example in the competition „Jugend debattiert“) is: **opening round** (each person states their position), **open debate** (direct exchange) and **closing round** (each person sums up; changing one's mind is allowed). A jury usually assesses subject knowledge, expressiveness, ability to engage in dialogue and persuasiveness.

## Minutes

**Minutes** record the course and results of a meeting, session or experiment in writing. They are factual and objective, written in the **present tense**, and contributions are reproduced in **reported speech** (subjunctive) (see [Verb forms](/en/languages/german/verb_forms/#reported-speech-indirekte-rede)).

### Header and closing

| Part        | Content                                                                       |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| header      | type of meeting, date, start and end time, place, those present, those absent (excused/unexcused), chair or moderator, minute-taker |
| agenda      | numbered agenda items (TOP 1, TOP 2, ...; TOP = Tagesordnungspunkt)           |
| main part   | course or results depending on the type of minutes, structured by agenda item |
| closing     | place, date, signatures of the chair and the minute-taker, attachments if any |

### Minutes of proceedings and minutes of results

| Feature          | Minutes of proceedings (Verlaufsprotokoll)                 | Minutes of results (Ergebnisprotokoll)                 |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| content          | course of the meeting with important contributions and arguments | only results, decisions and tasks              |
| order            | chronological                                              | by agenda item                                         |
| contributions    | in reported speech, with names                             | omitted                                                |
| length           | detailed                                                   | brief                                                  |
| use              | committees, negotiations, when the path to a decision matters | team meetings, project meetings                    |

Example of an agenda item:

> **Verlaufsprotokoll, TOP 2: Termin der Projektpräsentation**
> Frau Huber schlägt den 12. Mai vor, da die Firma an diesem Tag teilnehmen könne. Herr Novak wendet ein, dass die Testphase bis dahin noch nicht abgeschlossen sei. Nach kurzer Diskussion einigt sich das Team auf den 19. Mai (einstimmig).
> (Ms Huber suggests 12 May, as the company could attend on that day. Mr Novak objects that the test phase would not be finished by then. After a short discussion, the team agrees on 19 May (unanimously).)

> **Ergebnisprotokoll, TOP 2: Termin der Projektpräsentation**
> Die Präsentation findet am 19. Mai statt (einstimmig beschlossen).
> Aufgabe: Herr Novak schließt die Testphase bis 12. Mai ab.
> (The presentation takes place on 19 May (decided unanimously). Task: Mr Novak completes the test phase by 12 May.)

In minutes of results from project meetings, tasks are often recorded as a table: **what** has to be done, **who** is responsible, **by when**.
