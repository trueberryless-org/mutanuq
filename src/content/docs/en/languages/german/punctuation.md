Punctuation marks structure a text and make its organisation visible to the reader. Unlike English, German punctuation follows mainly the **grammatical structure** of the sentence, not pauses in speech. This is why you need the terms from [Sentence structure](/en/languages/german/sentence_structure/) for the comma rules: main clause, subordinate clause, infinitive group, insertion.

The basis is the **Official Rules** (Amtliches Regelwerk) in the 2024 version. The punctuation chapter was restructured and simplified. The most important change in content: infinitive groups must now **always** be separated by a comma.

## Comma

### Lists

Elements of equal rank (words, phrases and clauses) are separated by commas if they are not joined by `und`, `oder`, `sowie`, `bzw.`, `sowohl ... als auch`, `weder ... noch`, `entweder ... oder`. Unlike English, German has no comma before `und` in a list (no Oxford comma).

> Wir brauchen einen Router, zwei Switches**,** ein Patchpanel **und** Kabel. (We need a router, two switches, a patch panel and cables.)
> Sie spricht **weder** Englisch **noch** Französisch.

With adjectives: use a comma only if they are of **equal rank**. Test: if you can insert `und` without changing the meaning, use a comma.

> ein spannender**,** lehrreicher Vortrag (spannend und lehrreich: an exciting, instructive talk)
> ein neues mobiles Endgerät (no comma: `mobiles Endgerät` is a fixed term further specified by `neues`)

### Contrasting conjunctions

There is always a comma before `aber`, `doch`, `jedoch`, `sondern`, `allein` (but, however).

> Das Programm ist langsam**,** aber zuverlässig. (The program is slow but reliable.)
> Nicht er**,** sondern sie hat den Fehler gefunden. (Not he but she found the bug.)

### Main clauses

You separate main clauses of equal rank (compound sentence) with a comma. If they are joined by `und` or `oder`, the comma is **optional**; you can use it to make the structure clear.

> Der Server startet neu**,** die Dienste werden geladen.
> Der Server startet neu(**,**) und die Dienste werden geladen.

### Subordinate clauses

Subordinate clauses are **always** separated from the superordinate clause by a comma. If the subordinate clause is embedded, there is a comma before and after it. This is a major difference from English, where a restrictive relative clause ("the program that you wrote") has no commas.

> Ich weiß nicht**,** ob das Update schon installiert ist.
> Weil der Akku leer war**,** ging das Handy aus.
> Das Programm**,** das du geschrieben hast**,** läuft fehlerfrei. (The program that you wrote runs without errors.)

If a subordinating conjunction is combined with another word (`sodass`, `ohne dass`, `als ob`, `anstatt dass`, `je nachdem ob`), the comma goes before the whole combination: Er tut so**,** als ob er nichts wüsste.

Several subordinate clauses joined by `und` or `oder` are not separated by a comma: Ich hoffe, dass du kommst **und** dass du Zeit mitbringst.

### Infinitive groups

An **infinitive group** consists of an infinitive with `zu` and at least one other word. Since 2024:

1.  **Infinitive groups are always separated by a comma.**

    > Sie plant**,** mit dem Zug nach Wien zu fahren. (She plans to go to Vienna by train.)
    > Um die Prüfung zu bestehen**,** lernt er jeden Tag. (To pass the exam, he studies every day.)
    > Er ging**,** ohne zu grüßen. (He left without saying hello.)
    > Sie hat vergessen**,** die Datei zu speichern. (She forgot to save the file.)

2.  With a **bare infinitive** with `zu` (no other word), the comma is optional.

    > Sie plant(**,**) zu verreisen.

3.  **No comma** is used if the infinitive is part of the predicate, i.e. with verbs such as `scheinen`, `pflegen`, `brauchen`, `haben`, `sein`, and in interlaced constructions:

    > Er scheint zu schlafen. Sie hat viel zu tun. Du brauchst nicht zu kommen.
    > Diesen Fehler wollen wir zu beheben versuchen.

:::note[What changed in 2024?]
Under the 2006 rules, the comma was optional with many infinitive groups and compulsory only in certain cases (after `um`, `ohne`, `statt`, `anstatt`, `außer`, `als`, when dependent on a noun, or with a reference word such as `daran`, `es`). Since the 2024 version, the comma is compulsory with all infinitive groups. Older textbooks still give the old rule. With a comma before every infinitive group, you are always right.
:::

### Participle groups

A **participle group** is a participle with at least one other word. The comma is usually optional. If the group is an addition or afterthought in the middle or at the end of the sentence, it is separated by commas.

> Vor Freude strahlend(**,**) kam sie aus dem Prüfungsraum. (Beaming with joy, she came out of the exam room.)
> Sie kam**,** vor Freude strahlend**,** aus dem Prüfungsraum.

### Additions, afterthoughts and appositions

Additions and afterthoughts that interrupt or supplement the sentence are separated by commas, with insertions on both sides.

-   **Apposition** (a following noun phrase in the same case): Wien**,** die Hauptstadt Österreichs**,** liegt an der Donau. A close apposition without commas is used with names and titles: Kaiser Franz Joseph, die Stadt Wien.
-   **Afterthoughts** with `und zwar`, `das heißt (d. h.)`, `nämlich`, `insbesondere`, `vor allem`, `zum Beispiel (z. B.)`: Er spielt viele Instrumente**,** z. B. Gitarre und Klavier.
-   **Dates, places and addresses**: Am Montag**,** dem 4. Mai**,** beginnt die Matura. Sie wohnt in Linz**,** Landstraße 12.

### Forms of address, exclamations and comments

Forms of address, exclamations and affirming words are separated by commas because they stand outside the actual sentence.

> Lisa**,** kommst du mit? Kommst du mit**,** Lisa?
> Ach**,** das habe ich vergessen. Oh**,** wie schön!
> Ja**,** ich komme. Nein**,** danke.
> Sehr geehrte Damen und Herren**,** ...

### Comparisons with als and wie

There is a comma before `als` and `wie` only if a complete subordinate clause (with a finite verb) follows.

> Sie ist größer als ich. (no comma: she is taller than me)
> Sie ist größer**,** als ich dachte. (subordinate clause: she is taller than I thought)

### Direct speech

> „Ich komme morgen“**,** sagte sie.
> „Kommst du morgen?“**,** fragte er.
> Sie sagte: „Ich komme morgen.“
> „Ich komme“**,** sagte sie**,** „aber erst am Abend.“

The comma after the closing quotation mark is used even if the direct speech ends with a question or exclamation mark. A full stop at the end of direct speech is dropped if the reporting clause follows.

## Full stop (Punkt)

The full stop ends a declarative sentence. It is also used:

-   after many **abbreviations**: z. B., usw., bzw., Dr., Nr. (initialisms spoken letter by letter and short forms have no full stop: HTL, EU, PDF, km)
-   after **ordinal numbers** in digits: am 3. März (on 3 March), der 2. Jahrgang (the second year)

If a sentence ends with an abbreviation that has a full stop, you do not add a second one. There is no full stop after **headings**, titles and lines that stand alone (closing formula, signature).

**Ellipsis dots** (three dots) show that something has been left out. If a whole word or part of a sentence is missing, there is a space before them: Er murmelte: „Das ist doch ...“

## Colon (Doppelpunkt)

The colon announces something:

-   **direct speech** and quotations: Sie sagte: „Wir fangen an.“
-   **lists**: Mitzubringen sind: Laptop, Netzteil, Ausweis.
-   **summaries**, conclusions and explanations: Haus, Auto, Job: Alles war weg.

After the colon, you capitalise if a complete sentence follows; otherwise lowercase (except nouns).

## Semicolon (Semikolon)

The **semicolon** (Strichpunkt) separates more strongly than a comma and less strongly than a full stop. It joins main clauses of equal rank that are closely related in content, and structures long lists with subgroups.

> Das Projekt ist abgeschlossen; die Dokumentation fehlt aber noch.
> Wir brauchen Hardware: Router, Switches; Software: Betriebssystem, Treiber; Zubehör: Kabel, Dübel.

## Quotation marks (Anführungszeichen)

Quotation marks indicate direct speech, quotations, titles of works and words that are being talked about or from which the writer distances themself.

| Form                         | Use                                                                 |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| „...“ (low, high)            | standard form in German and in Austria                              |
| »...« (points facing inwards) | in books, typographic alternative                                  |
| ‚...‘ (single quotation marks) | quotation within a quotation                                      |

> Der Autor schreibt: „Schon Kant forderte: ‚Habe Mut, dich deines eigenen Verstandes zu bedienen!‘“

Use quotation marks for the **titles** of books, films, newspapers and articles: der Roman „Die Verwandlung“, ein Artikel im „Standard“. Punctuation that belongs to the quotation goes inside the quotation marks, everything else outside.

:::caution
The English character `"` (straight, high) or the English form with both marks high is not correct in German running text. Word processors set the German marks automatically if the language is set to German (Austria).
:::

## Dash (Gedankenstrich)

The **dash** (Gedankenstrich) is a horizontal line clearly longer than the hyphen (typographically an en rule) and, unlike the hyphen, has **a space before and after it** in German. It announces something unexpected or encloses an insertion that is to be emphasised more strongly than with commas. With an insertion, one dash goes before and one after it; a comma that would be needed without the insertion follows the second dash.

There is no dedicated key for it on the keyboard; word processors usually replace a hyphen between two spaces automatically. Many style guides recommend using it sparingly. In non-fiction texts, brackets, commas or a colon often do the same job. The line between two numbers or places (pages 12 to 15, the route from Vienna to Graz) is read as „bis“ (to) and has no spaces.

## Brackets (Klammern)

Round brackets enclose **additions and explanations** that are not necessary for the sentence.

> Die HTL (Höhere Technische Lehranstalt) dauert fünf Jahre.

Punctuation of the surrounding sentence goes after the closing bracket: Sie kam spät (wie immer)**.** If a whole sentence is in brackets, its full stop belongs inside the bracket. Square brackets are used for your own additions and omissions in quotations: „Er [der Autor] kritisiert [...] die Schulpolitik.“

## Apostrophe (Apostroph)

The apostrophe (`'`) shows that letters have been left out.

-   **Omission** in colloquial forms: Wie geht's? So 'n Unsinn! In `geht's`, `gibt's` the apostrophe may also be left out: gehts, gibts.
-   **Genitive** of names ending in s, ss, ß, x, z, ce: Klaus**'** Laptop, Max**'** Fahrrad, Grass**'** Romane.
-   To clarify the base form of a name, an apostrophe before the genitive s is permitted: Andrea**'**s Blumenladen (Andrea, not Andreas). Normally, however, Andreas Laptop has no apostrophe.

:::danger[Common mistake]
There is no apostrophe in the **plural** or in the normal **genitive**: die Autos (not `*Auto's`), die PCs (not `*PC's`), Peters Handy (not `*Peter's Handy` in running text). English learners in particular tend to carry over the English possessive apostrophe.
:::

## Hyphen (Bindestrich)

The hyphen is a word mark, not a punctuation mark: it stands without spaces inside compounds (IT-Abteilung, Open-Source-Software) and as a suspension hyphen (Ein- und Ausgabe). You will find the rules under [Spelling](/en/languages/german/spelling/#hyphen-bindestrich).

## Question mark and exclamation mark

-   The **question mark** follows direct questions: Wann beginnt die Prüfung? After an **indirect** question (subordinate clause), there is no question mark but a full stop: Sie fragt, wann die Prüfung beginnt.
-   The **exclamation mark** (in Austria: Rufzeichen) follows commands, exclamations, wishes and emphatic statements: Komm sofort! Das ist ja großartig! In letters, it can replace the comma after the salutation, in which case the following text starts with a capital: Sehr geehrte Frau Huber!

In non-fiction texts, never put several question or exclamation marks in a row (`?!`, `!!!`).
