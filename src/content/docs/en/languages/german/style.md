Good style means that a text is worded appropriately for **its readers** and **its purpose**. Minutes need different language from an opinion speech. In the SRDP in German, style is assessed in several criteria: in "style and expression" (Stil und Ausdruck: word choice, sentence structure, register, suitability for the text type) and in "text structure" (Textstruktur: organisation, coherence). The following rules are not rigid laws, but they help you write clearly and precisely.

## Registers

| Register                 | Features                                             | Example                                    | Use                                    |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------- |
| formal, literary         | refined words, often loanwords, long sentences       | Er verschied im Kreise seiner Lieben. (He passed away surrounded by his loved ones.) | speeches on formal occasions, literature |
| standard (neutral)       | generally understandable, factual                    | Er starb im Kreis seiner Familie. (He died surrounded by his family.) | non-fiction, Matura text types |
| colloquial               | relaxed, shortened forms, regional words       | Er ist gestorben, die ganze Familie war da. | conversations, chat, informal e-mail  |
| slang to vulgar          | flippant, coarse, sometimes offensive     | Er hat den Löffel abgegeben. (He kicked the bucket.) | avoid in school texts         |

**Standard German** applies to the Matura text types. Colloquial language only makes sense as a deliberate stylistic device (for example in an opinion speech to people your own age). It is important to keep to one register and not to switch between registers without a reason (**break in style**).

## Nominal style and verbal style

In **nominal style** (Nominalstil), the action is expressed by nouns (often ending in -ung, -heit, -keit); in **verbal style** (Verbalstil), by verbs.

| Nominal style                                                       | Verbal style                                                |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Nach erfolgter Durchführung der Überprüfung der Anlage erfolgt die Freigabe. (Following completion of the inspection of the system, release takes place.) | Nachdem wir die Anlage überprüft haben, geben wir sie frei. (After we have checked the system, we release it.) |
| Die Verbesserung der Lesbarkeit ist durch die Vermeidung von Nominalisierungen möglich. | Texte werden lesbarer, wenn man Nominalisierungen vermeidet. |
| zur Kenntnis nehmen (take note of)                                  | beachten (note)                                             |
| in Erwägung ziehen (take into consideration)                        | erwägen (consider)                                          |

Nominal style is **concise** and suits headings, tables, notes and technical texts (Datensicherung, Inbetriebnahme). In running text, however, it quickly sounds **heavy and bureaucratic** because the verbs become empty light verbs such as `erfolgen`, `durchführen`, `vornehmen`. Verbal style is more vivid and easier to understand. A good aim: nominal style in headings and lists, verbal style in running text.

## Sentence length and structure

-   **Variety:** alternate short and medium-length sentences. Only short sentences sound choppy; only long sentences are tiring.
-   **One piece of information per sentence:** if a sentence has more than two subordinate clauses or more than about 25 words, check whether you can split it.
-   **Important things in the main clause:** the key statement belongs in the main clause, secondary details in the subordinate clause.
-   **Do not overstretch the sentence bracket:** do not separate the parts of the verb too far. Instead of „Wir **werden** die Ergebnisse der Umfrage unter den Schülerinnen und Schülern aller fünf Jahrgänge nächste Woche im Rahmen einer Präsentation **vorstellen**“ write „Nächste Woche **stellen** wir die Ergebnisse der Umfrage **vor**. Befragt wurden Schülerinnen und Schüler aller fünf Jahrgänge.“ (Next week we will present the survey results. Pupils from all five years were surveyed.)
-   **Vary sentence openings:** do not start every sentence with the subject („Der Autor ... Der Autor ... Er ...“). An adverbial or object at the start shifts the emphasis.

## Avoiding repetition

Words repeated in quick succession sound monotonous. Use:

-   **synonyms:** Autor, Verfasser, Journalist; Text, Artikel, Beitrag
-   **pronouns:** er, dieser, sie
-   **generic terms:** instead of saying „Smartphone“ three times, also „das Gerät“ (the device)

Note: in **technical texts** and definitions, repetition is often necessary. If you change the term there, the reader will think something else is meant (server, host and computer do not always mean the same thing). Deliberate repetition as a stylistic device (anaphora) is allowed.

## Filler words

Filler words carry no information and weaken statements. Delete them if the sentence means the same without them.

| Typical filler words                                 | Example                                            | Better                                   |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| eigentlich, irgendwie, quasi, halt, eben, ja, wohl   | Das ist eigentlich irgendwie ein Problem.          | Das ist ein Problem.                     |
| an und für sich, im Grunde genommen, sozusagen       | Im Grunde genommen ist das sozusagen richtig.      | Das ist richtig.                         |
| durchaus, gewissermaßen, ziemlich, relativ           | Der Vorschlag ist durchaus relativ gut.            | Der Vorschlag ist gut.                   |
| duplications (pleonasms)                             | weißer Schimmel (white white-horse), neu renoviert, bereits schon | Schimmel, renoviert, bereits |

## Using the passive sparingly

The [passive](/en/languages/german/verb_forms/#active-and-passive) hides who is acting. That makes sense in instructions and minutes, but in argumentative texts it often sounds evasive: „Es wurden Fehler gemacht“ (mistakes were made) does not reveal who is responsible. Use the active when the doer matters: „Die Regierung hat Fehler gemacht.“ (The government made mistakes.) An accumulation of passive sentences also makes the text impersonal and hard to read.

## Anglicisms

**Anglicisms** are words and phrases from English. In IT, many of them are technical terms without a common German equivalent (Software, Server, Cloud, Update, Debugging) and are therefore correct. Anglicisms are problematic when

-   an equivalent German word exists and the target group does not understand the anglicism (Meeting instead of Besprechung in a text for older readers),
-   they are only meant to sound fashionable (das macht Sinn instead of das ergibt Sinn, Deadline instead of Frist in an official letter),
-   they are **pseudo-anglicisms** that do not exist in English with that meaning (Handy for mobile phone, Beamer for projector, Public Viewing, Oldtimer for vintage car).

Write English terms according to German rules: nouns capitalised, compounds as one word or with a hyphen (das Update, die Open-Source-Software), verbs conjugated the German way (sie hat die App upgedatet).

## Gender-inclusive language

**Gender-inclusive language** is meant to make all genders visible or to address people neutrally. Many people find the generic masculine („die Schüler“ for everyone) ambiguous because it is unclear whether women are included. In Austria there is no uniform legal requirement for all texts, but there are guidelines, for example from the Ministry of Education, and the requirement of linguistic equal treatment in the federal civil service. Many schools and companies have their own rules.

| Option                               | Example                                        | Note                                                       |
| ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| pair form (naming both)              | Schülerinnen und Schüler                       | unambiguous, compliant with the rules, but longer          |
| slash with suspension hyphen         | Schüler/-innen                                 | compliant, space-saving for forms                          |
| neutral forms                        | Lehrkräfte (teaching staff), Studierende (students), das Team, die Person | compliant, reads fluently        |
| rewording                            | alle, die teilnehmen; wer teilnimmt (everyone who takes part) | compliant, often elegant                    |
| special characters inside the word  | Schüler\*innen, Schüler:innen, Schüler_innen   | not part of the Official Rules                             |
| internal capital I                   | SchülerInnen                                   | not part of the Official Rules                             |

In 2024 the Council for German Orthography stated that special characters inside words (asterisk, colon, underscore) are **not part of the core** of German orthography. It describes them in a supplementary passage as an observed writing practice without regulating them. Pair forms, the slash with a suspension hyphen, neutral forms and rewording, on the other hand, fully comply with the rules.

For your texts:

-   Follow the requirements of your school or client.
-   Be **consistent** within a text.
-   Watch the **grammar**: pronouns and articles must match the form you choose (jede Schülerin und jeder Schüler hat **ihr** oder **sein** Heft, every pupil has her or his exercise book).
-   Prefer forms that are **easy to read** and **easy to read aloud**, especially in speeches.

## Text coherence

A text is **coherent** if its sentences and paragraphs are recognisably connected. Connectives, references and a clear paragraph structure achieve this.

### Connectives

Connectives show the logical relationship between statements.

| Relationship     | Connectives                                                         |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| addition         | erstens, zweitens, außerdem, zudem, darüber hinaus, ferner (firstly, secondly, moreover, furthermore) |
| reason           | weil, da, denn, deshalb, daher, aus diesem Grund (because, therefore) |
| consequence      | sodass, folglich, somit, demzufolge, infolgedessen (so that, consequently) |
| contrast         | aber, jedoch, dagegen, hingegen, während, im Gegensatz dazu (but, however, whereas) |
| concession       | obwohl, zwar ... aber, trotzdem, dennoch, allerdings (although, nevertheless) |
| condition        | wenn, falls, sofern, im Fall, dass (if, provided that)              |
| example          | zum Beispiel, beispielsweise, etwa, so (for example)                |
| summary          | zusammenfassend, insgesamt, letztlich, kurz gesagt (in short, overall) |
| sequence in time | zuerst, danach, anschließend, schließlich, zuletzt (first, then, finally) |

### References

Pronouns and reference words (dieser, jener, dabei, deshalb, diese Entwicklung) link back to what came before. Make sure the reference is unambiguous: in „Der Lehrer sprach mit dem Schüler, weil er unzufrieden war“ (the teacher spoke to the pupil because he was dissatisfied), it is unclear who was dissatisfied.

### Paragraphs

A **paragraph** deals with one idea, for example one argument. A good paragraph begins with a topic sentence that names the subject of the paragraph and then develops it with a reason and an example. Mark paragraphs with a line break and a blank line. A text without paragraphs is hard to read and loses marks in the SRDP criterion "text structure"; too many one-sentence paragraphs break up the line of thought.

## Reader orientation

Always write with the target group in mind:

-   **Who is reading?** Experts, laypeople, people your age, superiors? This determines technical vocabulary, register and form of address.
-   **What does the target group already know?** Explain technical terms for laypeople the first time they appear, write out abbreviations.
-   **What should they do or know afterwards?** The most important information belongs at the beginning; requests must be clearly worded.
-   **Concrete rather than abstract:** examples, figures and comparisons make statements vivid.
-   **Make the structure visible:** headings, paragraphs and, in non-fiction texts, lists and tables.

The four dimensions of comprehensibility of the **Hamburg comprehensibility model** (Langer, Schulz von Thun, Tausch) sum this up: simplicity, structure and order, brevity and conciseness, and stimulating additions.
