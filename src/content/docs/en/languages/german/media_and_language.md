Almost all source texts in the SRDP come from newspapers, magazines or online media. If you know how the media work and which text type you are looking at, you can describe the intention and effect of a text more precisely in a [text analysis](/en/languages/german_text_types/text_analysis/). The second part of this page deals with the language itself: where German comes from and in which varieties it is spoken today.

## Media studies

### Types of media

| Type                      | Examples                                            | Features                                                     |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| print media               | daily paper, weekly paper, magazine, book           | permanent, readers set the pace, publication cycle           |
| broadcasting (audiovisual) | radio, television                                  | linear (schedule), wide reach, picture and sound             |
| online and digital media  | news portals, social media, podcasts, streaming     | available at any time, interactive, fast, sorted by algorithms |

In Austrian broadcasting, there is the **public service** broadcaster ORF, which the ORF Act obliges to provide information, education, entertainment and balance and which is financed mainly by the ORF levy, and **private** broadcasters financed by advertising. Newspapers are roughly divided into **quality newspapers** (factual language, background reports, clear separation of news and opinion) and **tabloids** (large pictures, short texts, emotionalisation, personalisation, headlines with superlatives).

The **functions** of the media in a democracy are information, opinion-forming, criticism and scrutiny of those in power (the "fourth estate") and entertainment.

### Press freedom

**Press freedom** is the right to publish information and opinions without state censorship. In Austria it is protected by the constitution, among other things by Article 13 of the Basic Law of 1867 (Staatsgrundgesetz) and Article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR), which has constitutional status in Austria. It is not unlimited: defamation, slander, incitement to hatred and National Socialist activities are criminal offences, and the Media Act (Mediengesetz) protects personal rights.

| Institution             | Task                                                                       |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| KommAustria             | state media authority: licensing and supervision of broadcasting           |
| Austrian Press Council (Presserat) | voluntary self-regulation of the print media, examines breaches of the code of ethics (e.g. diligence, protection of privacy, separation of advertising and editorial content) |
| Media Act               | governs, among other things, the imprint, disclosure of owners, counter-statements and labelling of advertising |

International organisations such as **Reporters Without Borders** publish an annual press freedom index comparing the situation in individual countries.

### Journalistic text types

Journalistic text types can be divided into **informative** and **opinion-based** ones. Good newspapers separate the two clearly, for example through separate sections or the label „Kommentar“ (commentary).

| Text type      | Kind           | Features                                                                  |
| -------------- | -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| short news item (Meldung) | informative | very short, answers the most important wh-questions             |
| news report (Nachricht) | informative | factual, concise, structured according to the inverted pyramid     |
| report (Bericht) | informative  | more detailed than the news report, with background, context and quotations, factual |
| feature report (Reportage) | informative | vivid, close to people and places, scenic opening, present tense, alternates description and facts |
| interview      | informative    | question-and-answer form, verbatim reproduction                           |
| commentary (Kommentar) | opinion-based | the author's opinion on a current topic, argumentative, signed with the author's name (see [Commentary](/en/languages/german_text_types/commentary/)) |
| editorial (Leitartikel) | opinion-based | a commentary representing the newspaper's line, usually prominently placed |
| gloss (Glosse) | opinion-based  | short, ironic or satirical, pointed, often with a surprising punchline    |
| column (Kolumne) | opinion-based | regular contribution by the same person in a fixed place, personal style |
| review (Rezension, Kritik) | opinion-based | assessment of a book, film, concert or product                  |
| letter to the editor (Leserbrief) | opinion-based | readers' reaction to an article (see [Letter to the editor](/en/languages/german_text_types/letter_to_the_editor/)) |

The **news report** follows the **inverted pyramid**: the most important information comes first, details follow in decreasing order of importance. This means the text can be cut from the end without losing anything essential. The first paragraph (**lead**) answers the wh-questions: who? what? when? where? how? why? where does the information come from (source)?

### Fake news and checking sources

**Fake news** is false or misleading information that looks like news. A distinction is made between:

-   **disinformation:** deliberately false information, for example to influence politics or to make money
-   **misinformation:** false information spread without intent to deceive
-   **manipulated content:** quotations taken out of context, edited images, AI-generated images, voices and videos (**deepfakes**)

It spreads quickly because it often appeals to emotions such as fear or outrage and is favoured by social media algorithms. **Filter bubbles** and **echo chambers** reinforce this: if you mainly see content that confirms your own opinion, you can easily mistake it for the majority opinion.

This is how you check a piece of information:

| Step                       | Questions and tools                                                        |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| stop                       | Does the post trigger strong emotions? Check first, then share.            |
| check the source           | Who is behind it? Is there an imprint? Is the medium known and reputable?  |
| look for other sources     | Do several independent, reputable media report on it?                      |
| trace it back to the origin | Where does the quotation, the figure, the image come from? When was it created? |
| check images               | reverse image search, metadata, conspicuous details (with AI images, for example hands, lettering, shadows) |
| use fact checks            | fact-checking teams of news agencies and media, in Austria for example the APA-Faktencheck |

### Advertising and its language

**Advertising** aims to attract attention and to persuade people to buy something or take an action. A classic model is the **AIDA formula**: attention, interest, desire, action.

| Linguistic device               | Example                                           | Effect                                   |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| superlatives, high-value words  | das Beste (the best), extra, premium, einzigartig (unique) | enhances the product            |
| neologisms                      | unkaputtbar (unbreakable)                         | attention, memorability                  |
| anglicisms                      | Feel the difference, Smart Home                   | modern, international, young             |
| imperative, direct address      | Hol dir jetzt ...! (Get ... now!) Gönn dir ...! (Treat yourself to ...!) | call to action, closeness |
| rhetorical questions            | Schon gewusst? (Did you know?)                    | involves the target group                |
| alliteration, rhyme, rhythm     | Milch macht müde Männer munter. (Milk perks up tired men.) | memorability                    |
| wordplay, ambiguity             | slogans with a double meaning                     | wit, attention                           |
| ellipses                        | Einfach. Schnell. Sicher. (Simple. Fast. Secure.) | conciseness, dynamism                    |
| technical terms, pseudoscience  | klinisch getestet (clinically tested), mit Hyaluron-Komplex | apparent competence            |
| testimonials                    | well-known people recommend a product             | trust, role model effect                 |

Advertising must be recognisable as such. Paid content in the media is labelled, for example, with „Anzeige“, „Werbung“ or „entgeltliche Einschaltung“ (paid insertion), and influencers must disclose paid partnerships.

## History of the German language

German belongs to the **Germanic** branch of the **Indo-European** language family, which also includes the Romance, Slavic, Celtic, Baltic and Indo-Iranian languages as well as Greek. Proto-Indo-European is not attested in writing but has been reconstructed by comparing languages.

| Stage                       | Period (approx.)     | Features and records                                                       |
| --------------------------- | -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Germanic                    | from the 1st millennium BC | the **First (Germanic) Sound Shift** separates Germanic from other Indo-European languages: Latin pater, English father; Latin tres, English three |
| Old High German (Althochdeutsch) | 750 to 1050     | the **Second (High German) Sound Shift** separates High German from Low German and English: English apple, German Apfel; English ten, German zehn; English make, German machen. Records: Merseburg Charms, Hildebrandslied, glosses in monasteries |
| Middle High German (Mittelhochdeutsch) | 1050 to 1350 | courtly poetic language: Nibelungenlied, Minnesang, Walther von der Vogelweide; unstressed final vowels weakened to e |
| Early New High German (Frühneuhochdeutsch) | 1350 to 1650 | **printing** (Gutenberg, around 1450), chancery languages, **Luther's Bible translation** (New Testament 1522, complete 1534); **New High German diphthongisation**: MHG mîn niuwez hûs became mein neues Haus (my new house) |
| New High German (Neuhochdeutsch) | from 1650       | language societies, standardisation of grammar and spelling, Konrad Duden's dictionary (1880), uniform spelling (1901/1902), spelling reform (1996, revised 2006), 2024 version |

The literature of the individual periods can be found under [Literature](/en/languages/literature/).

## Language varieties

A language is not a uniform system. Depending on region, social group and situation, people speak different **varieties**.

| Variety               | Determined by           | Description and examples                                                     |
| --------------------- | ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| standard language (Standardsprache, Hochsprache) | supra-regional norm | laid down in dictionaries and grammars; language of school, authorities, news |
| colloquial language (Umgangssprache) | situation | everyday language between standard and dialect, regionally coloured         |
| dialect (Dialekt, Mundart) | region             | own sounds, words and grammar; in Austria mainly **Bavarian** dialects, in Vorarlberg and parts of Tyrol **Alemannic** |
| sociolect             | social group            | the language of a group, such as youth language or professional jargon       |
| technical language (Fachsprache) | field        | precise technical terms, often loanwords and abbreviations (IT: Router, Commit, Deployment) |
| ethnolect             | origin                  | a way of speaking shaped by contact with other languages                     |
| idiolect              | person                  | an individual's personal way of speaking                                     |

In Austria, many people move along a **continuum** between dialect and standard language and choose a level in between depending on the situation. In German-speaking Switzerland, by contrast, there is **diglossia**: dialect is spoken almost always in everyday life, while writing is mostly in the standard language.

### Austrian German as a pluricentric variety

German is a **pluricentric language**: it has several centres with their own, equally valid standard varieties, above all Germany, Austria and Switzerland. **Austrian German** is therefore neither a dialect nor a deviation from "correct" German but a standard variety in its own right, with distinctive features in vocabulary, grammar, pronunciation and spelling variants.

-   It is **codified** in the **Österreichisches Wörterbuch** (ÖWB), which has been published on behalf of the Ministry of Education since 1951.
-   When Austria joined the EU in 1995, **Protocol No. 10** laid down that 23 Austrian terms (mainly food names such as Erdäpfel, Paradeiser, Topfen, Marillen, Schlagobers) are used in EU legal texts on an equal footing with the terms used in Germany.
-   Examples of Austrianisms can be found under [Spelling](/en/languages/german/spelling/#austrian-features).

Variants used only in Germany are called **Teutonisms** (Deutschlandismen), Swiss variants **Helvetisms**.
