When the National Socialists seized power in 1933, freedom of art in Germany came to an end. Books were burned, and authors were persecuted, arrested or forced to flee. German-language literature split into the state-controlled literature in the German Reich, the literature of **inner emigration** and exile literature.

## Key facts

- 1933 – 1945
- 10 May 1933: book burning in Berlin and many other university towns
- forced coordination of culture through the Reich Chamber of Literature
- more than 2000 writers in exile
- 1938: "Anschluss" of Austria: Austrian authors also have to flee
- themes of exile: loss of home, resistance, informing the world about the Nazi regime

## Historical background

On 30 January 1933, Adolf Hitler became Chancellor of the German Reich. Within a few months, the National Socialists established a dictatorship. Anyone who wanted to publish had to be a member of the **Reich Chamber of Literature**; Jews and political opponents were excluded. With the "Anschluss" of Austria in March 1938, these rules applied there too. The Second World War (1939 – 1945) and the Holocaust, in which around six million Jews were murdered, represent the greatest break with civilisation in history.

## Book burning

On **10 May 1933**, students, professors and SA men burned tens of thousands of books by "un-German" authors on Berlin's Opernplatz and in many other towns, including works by Heinrich Heine, Karl Marx, Sigmund Freud, Erich Kästner, Erich Maria Remarque, Kurt Tucholsky, Stefan Zweig and Bertolt Brecht. Erich Kästner was present himself and watched his books being burned.

Heinrich Heine had already written in his tragedy "Almansor" in 1821:

> Das war ein Vorspiel nur, dort wo man Bücher verbrennt, verbrennt man auch am Ende Menschen.

(That was only a prelude; where they burn books, they will ultimately burn people as well.)

## Literature in the German Reich

The officially promoted literature was meant to spread Nazi ideology: **"blood and soil" poetry**, hero worship, glorification of war and the cult of the Führer. Today it has no literary value.

Some authors remained in the country but rejected the regime. This is called **inner emigration**: they withdrew into non-political subjects or expressed hidden criticism, for example in historical material. Examples are Werner Bergengruen, Reinhold Schneider and Erich Kästner, who continued to write under a pseudonym.

Literature was also written in the **resistance**. The leaflets of the White Rose around the siblings Hans and Sophie Scholl called for resistance in 1942/43. The members were executed.

## Exile literature

Exiled authors first fled to neighbouring countries such as Czechoslovakia, Austria, Switzerland, France or the Netherlands, later mainly to the USA, Great Britain, Mexico or Palestine. They faced great difficulties: the loss of their homeland, their language, their readers and their financial livelihood. Some broke down under the strain.

### Thomas Mann (1875 – 1955)

Thomas Mann had received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1929, mainly for his novel "**Buddenbrooks**" (1901) about the decline of a Lübeck merchant family. In 1933, he did not return to Germany from a lecture tour and lived in Switzerland and later in the USA. In his radio addresses "Deutsche Hörer!" (German Listeners!), he spoke to the people in Germany via the BBC. In exile, he wrote "Doktor Faustus" (1947), among other works. "Der Zauberberg" (The Magic Mountain, 1924) and the novella "Der Tod in Venedig" (Death in Venice, 1912) are also well known.

### Anna Seghers (1900 – 1983)

"**Das siebte Kreuz**" (The Seventh Cross, 1942) tells of the escape of seven prisoners from the Westhofen concentration camp. Six are caught; only Georg Heisler escapes, thanks to the help of many people. The seventh cross, prepared for him, remains empty. The novel shows that humanity was possible even in the Nazi state. In "Transit" (1944), she describes the desperate situation of refugees in Marseille.

### Klaus Mann (1906 – 1949)

Thomas Mann's son wrote the novel "**Mephisto**" (1936) in exile, about an actor who advances his career in the Nazi state through conformity and betrayal. The character is a portrait of the actor Gustaf Gründgens.

### Other representatives

- **Bertolt Brecht:** wrote his most important plays in exile in Denmark, Sweden and the USA, including "Mother Courage and Her Children" and "Life of Galileo", as well as poems such as "An die Nachgeborenen" (To Those Born Later)
- **Stefan Zweig:** fled Salzburg for London in 1934 and later Brazil, where he took his own life in 1942. Shortly before, he completed the "Schachnovelle".
- **Joseph Roth:** died impoverished in exile in Paris in 1939
- **Ödön von Horváth:** died in exile in Paris in 1938 when a falling branch struck him
- **Nelly Sachs** (1891 – 1970): fled to Sweden in 1940 and dealt with the Holocaust in her poems; in 1966 she received the Nobel Prize in Literature
- Lion Feuchtwanger, Heinrich Mann, Franz Werfel and Carl Zuckmayer, who had lived in Austria since 1926 and had to flee in 1938

## Literature about the Holocaust

Among the most important testimonies are diaries and reports by persecuted people, such as "**The Diary of Anne Frank**", which the Jewish girl kept in her hiding place in Amsterdam before she was deported and died in the Bergen-Belsen concentration camp in 1945. The literary reckoning with the Holocaust shaped post-war literature, for example in Paul Celan's poem "[Todesfuge](/en/languages/literature/austrian_literature_after_1945/#paul-celan-1920--1970)".

## Genres

- **Epic:** exile novel, historical novel, contemporary novel
- **Poetry:** political poetry, laments
- **Drama:** parable plays, epic theatre
- **Non-fiction:** radio addresses, leaflets, diaries, autobiographies
