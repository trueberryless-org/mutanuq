Conditional sentences (_if-clauses_) consist of a subordinate clause with the condition (_if-clause_) and a main clause with the result (_main clause_). Which tenses you use depends on how likely the condition is and whether it refers to the present, the future or the past.

## Overview

| Type             | if-clause            | Main clause                 | Meaning                                    |
| ---------------- | -------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Zero             | present simple       | present simple              | always true, fact                          |
| First (type I)   | present simple       | `will` + base form          | realistic, possible                        |
| Second (type II) | past simple          | `would` + base form         | unlikely or unreal (present)               |
| Third (type III) | past perfect         | `would have` + participle   | unreal (past), can no longer be changed    |

:::caution[No will and no would in the if-clause]
The if-clause never contains `will` or `would` (for exceptions see below). Wrong: ~~If I would have time, I would help you.~~ Correct: `If I had time, I would help you.`
:::

**Punctuation:** If the if-clause comes first, it is followed by a comma. If the main clause comes first, there is no comma.

-   `If it rains, we will stay at home.`
-   `We will stay at home if it rains.`

## Zero conditional

General truths, laws of nature, technical processes: whenever the condition is met, the result follows. `if` can be replaced by `when` here.

-   `If you heat ice, it melts.`
-   `If the battery is low, the laptop switches to power-saving mode.`

## First conditional

A realistic condition in the present or future with a likely result.

-   `If you study regularly, you will pass the exam.`
-   `If the server goes down, we will lose customers.`

Instead of `will`, the main clause can contain a modal verb (`can`, `may`, `might`, `should`) or an imperative: `If you see Tom, tell him to call me.`

## Second conditional

An unlikely or imaginary (unreal) condition in the present or future. The past simple in the if-clause does not express past time but distance from reality.

-   `If I had more money, I would buy a new graphics card.` (I do not have enough money.)
-   `If I won the lottery, I would travel around the world.`

With `be`, formal English uses `were` for all persons, especially in the fixed phrase `If I were you, ...` for advice. In informal English, `was` is also common.

-   `If I were you, I would apply for that internship.`

`could` and `might` are also possible in the main clause: `If we had more time, we could test the app more thoroughly.`

## Third conditional

An unreal condition in the past: the situation is over and cannot be changed. The sentence often expresses regret or criticism.

-   `If I had backed up my files, I wouldn't have lost my project.` (I did not make a backup.)
-   `If she had left earlier, she would have caught the train.`

Instead of `would have`, `could have` and `might have` are also possible: `If you had asked me, I could have helped you.`

:::tip[Remember]
The third conditional contains **`have` twice**: `had` + participle in the if-clause, `would have` + participle in the main clause. In the short form both become `'d`: `If I'd known, I'd have told you.`
:::

## Mixed conditionals

If the condition and the result belong to different times, you mix the second and the third conditional.

| Condition           | Result             | Form                                                   | Example                                                                  |
| ------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| past                | present            | `if` + past perfect, `would` + base form              | `If I had studied IT, I would work as a developer now.`                 |
| present (state)     | past               | `if` + past simple, `would have` + participle         | `If I were better at maths, I would have passed the test last week.`     |

## Inversion instead of if

In formal texts, `if` can be dropped; the auxiliary then moves to the beginning of the sentence.

| With if                                       | Inversion                                   |
| --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| `If you should need help, ...`                | `Should you need help, ...`                 |
| `If I were in your position, ...`             | `Were I in your position, ...`              |
| `If I had known about the problem, ...`       | `Had I known about the problem, ...`        |

`Should you have any questions, please do not hesitate to contact me.` is a typical closing sentence in formal emails and letters.

## Other conjunctions

| Conjunction                          | Meaning                                   | Example                                                        |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `unless`                             | if not, except if                         | `You won't pass unless you study.` (= if you don't study)      |
| `in case`                            | as a precaution against something         | `Take an umbrella in case it rains.`                           |
| `as long as`, `provided (that)`, `providing (that)` | on condition that          | `You can use my laptop as long as you are careful.`            |
| `suppose`, `supposing`, `what if`    | imagine that                              | `Suppose you lost your job, what would you do?`                |
| `otherwise`                          | if not, or else                           | `Save your work, otherwise you might lose it.`                 |
| `even if`                            | it does not matter whether                | `Even if it rains, the event will take place.`                 |

:::caution[in case is not if]
`Take an umbrella if it rains.` means: take it only when it is raining. `Take an umbrella in case it rains.` means: take it to be on the safe side, it might rain. `in case` is not followed by `will`. Likewise, `unless` is not followed by a negative: ~~unless you don't study~~.
:::

### will and would in the if-clause

In two cases `will` or `would` is correct in the if-clause, because it is not a time marker but expresses willingness or politeness:

-   polite request: `If you would follow me, I'll show you the lab.`
-   willingness or refusal: `If you will not listen, I can't help you.`

## Wishes with wish and if only

With `wish` and `if only` (more emphatic), you express that you would like reality to be different. As with conditionals, the tense moves one step back.

| Reference                              | Form                                 | Example                                           |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- |
| present (regret about now)             | `wish` + past simple                 | `I wish I had more free time.`                    |
| present with `be`                      | `wish` + `were`                      | `I wish I were taller.`                           |
| ability                                | `wish` + `could`                     | `I wish I could speak Japanese.`                  |
| past (regret about the past)           | `wish` + past perfect                | `I wish I hadn't deleted that file.`              |
| annoyance, wanting others to change    | `wish` + `would` + base form         | `I wish you would stop interrupting me.`          |

-   `If only I had listened to my teacher!`
-   `If only it would stop raining.`

:::caution
Use `wish ... would` only for the behaviour of other people or things, not for yourself: ~~I wish I would have a car.~~ Correct: `I wish I had a car.` For wishes that can come true, use `hope`: `I hope you pass the exam.`
:::

### Similar structures

-   `It's (high) time` + past simple: `It's time we started the presentation.`
-   `would rather` + subject + past simple: `I'd rather you didn't use my account.`
