When one verb is followed by another, the second verb is either a **gerund** (`-ing` form: `I enjoy coding.`) or an **infinitive** with or without `to` (`I want to code.`, `I can code.`). Which form follows depends on the first verb and partly has to be learnt by heart. There is a rough tendency, though: the gerund often describes something real or already done, the `to`-infinitive something in the future or intended.

## The gerund

The gerund is the `-ing` form of a verb used like a **noun**.

-   as the subject: `Swimming is healthy.`, `Learning a language takes time.`
-   as the object after certain verbs: `I enjoy reading.`
-   after prepositions: `She is good at solving problems.`

Negative: `not` + `-ing`: `I regret not asking for help.` Passive: `being` + participle: `I hate being interrupted.` Earlier action: `having` + participle: `He denied having taken the money.`

## Verbs followed by the gerund

| Verbs                                                                                      | Example                                      |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- |
| `admit`, `avoid`, `consider`, `delay`, `deny`, `dislike`, `enjoy`, `finish`, `imagine`, `involve`, `keep`, `mind`, `miss`, `postpone`, `practise`, `recommend`, `risk`, `suggest` | `I've finished writing the report.`  |
| `can't help`, `can't stand`, `give up`, `carry on`, `put off`, `feel like`                 | `I can't help laughing.`                     |
| `it's worth`, `it's no use`, `there's no point (in)`                                       | `It's worth trying.`                         |
| `look forward to`, `be used to`, `get used to`, `object to`                                | `I look forward to hearing from you.`        |

## Verbs followed by the to-infinitive

| Verbs                                                                                      | Example                                      |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- |
| `afford`, `agree`, `appear`, `arrange`, `attempt`, `choose`, `decide`, `deserve`, `expect`, `fail`, `hope`, `learn`, `manage`, `offer`, `plan`, `prepare`, `pretend`, `promise`, `refuse`, `seem`, `tend`, `threaten`, `want`, `would like` | `We decided to use PostgreSQL.` |

### Verb + object + to-infinitive

| Verbs                                                                                      | Example                                      |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- |
| `advise`, `allow`, `ask`, `encourage`, `expect`, `force`, `invite`, `order`, `persuade`, `remind`, `teach`, `tell`, `want`, `warn`, `would like` | `My teacher encouraged me to apply.` |

`want` is never followed by a `that`-clause: ~~I want that you help me.~~ Correct: `I want you to help me.`

### to-infinitive after adjectives and question words

-   adjective + `to`: `It's easy to learn.`, `I'm happy to help.`
-   `too` + adjective + `to`, adjective + `enough` + `to`: `It's too late to change it.`, `He's old enough to drive.`
-   question word + `to`: `I don't know what to do.`, `Can you show me how to install it?`
-   purpose (`in order to`, `so as to`): `I came here to learn English.` (not ~~for to learn~~, and not ~~for learning~~ for a person's purpose)

## Bare infinitive

Use the infinitive without `to` (_bare infinitive_) after:

-   modal verbs: `You must go.`, `I can help.`
-   `let` and `make` (active): `Let me explain.`, `She made me laugh.` (in the passive with `to`: `I was made to wait.`)
-   `had better`, `would rather`: `You'd better hurry.`, `I'd rather stay.`
-   `help` (with or without `to`): `Can you help me (to) fix this?`
-   verbs of perception (see below)

## Gerund or infinitive with the same meaning

After `begin`, `start`, `continue`, `intend` the difference is small: `It started raining.` = `It started to rain.`

With `like`, `love`, `hate`, `prefer` there is a tendency: in British English the gerund emphasises the experience (`I like swimming.` = I enjoy it), the infinitive a habit or something you think is right (`I like to back up my files every Friday.`). After `would like`, `would love`, `would prefer` the `to`-infinitive is always used: `I would like to apply.`

## Changes of meaning

With some verbs the meaning changes depending on whether a gerund or an infinitive follows.

| Verb        | + gerund                                                         | + to-infinitive                                                    |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `stop`      | end an activity: `He stopped smoking.` (he no longer smokes)     | stop in order to do something: `He stopped to smoke.` (he stopped so that he could smoke) |
| `remember`  | remember something in the past: `I remember locking the door.`   | not forget to do something: `Remember to lock the door.`           |
| `forget`    | forget having done something (usually negative): `I'll never forget meeting her.` | not remember to do something: `I forgot to save the file.` |
| `try`       | experiment: `Try restarting the router.`                         | make an effort: `I tried to fix it, but I couldn't.`               |
| `regret`    | be sorry about something done: `I regret saying that.`          | be sorry to announce something: `We regret to inform you that ...` |
| `mean`      | involve: `A new job means moving to Vienna.`                     | intend: `I didn't mean to delete it.`                              |
| `go on`     | continue the same activity: `She went on talking.`               | move on to something new: `She went on to explain the next step.` |
| `need`      | passive meaning (BrE): `The car needs washing.` (= needs to be washed) | have to: `I need to wash the car.`                            |

### Verbs of perception

After `see`, `hear`, `watch`, `feel`, `notice` + object:

-   base form: the whole, completed action: `I saw him cross the street.`
-   `-ing`: an action in progress, only part of it: `I saw him crossing the street.`

## Preposition + gerund

A **preposition** is always followed by the gerund, never by the infinitive.

| Expression                                              | Example                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| adjective + preposition                                 | `interested in`, `good at`, `afraid of`, `tired of`, `responsible for`, `capable of`, `keen on`: `She is interested in learning Rust.` |
| verb + preposition                                      | `apologise for`, `insist on`, `succeed in`, `think of/about`, `believe in`, `depend on`: `He succeeded in fixing the bug.` |
| noun + preposition                                      | `the idea of`, `the advantage of`, `the chance of`, `a reason for`: `What's the advantage of using Docker?` |
| prepositions and conjunctions                           | `before`, `after`, `without`, `instead of`, `by`, `despite`, `besides`: `Read the manual before installing.`, `You can save time by using shortcuts.` |

### to as a preposition

In some expressions `to` is not the infinitive marker but a **preposition**. That is why the gerund follows: `look forward to`, `be used to`, `get used to`, `object to`, `be committed to`, `when it comes to`, `in addition to`.

-   `I look forward to hearing from you.` (not ~~to hear~~)
-   `I'm used to working at night.` (= it is normal for me)

Do not confuse this with `used to` + base form for past habits: `I used to work at night.` (= I worked at night in the past).

A test: if a noun can follow `to` (`I look forward to the holidays.`), `to` is a preposition and the gerund follows.
