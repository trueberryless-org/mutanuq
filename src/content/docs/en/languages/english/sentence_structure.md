English has hardly any case endings. Who does what to whom is therefore shown almost entirely by **word order**. It is stricter than in German, and many mistakes made by German speakers come from transferring German word order (verb in second position, verb at the end of the subordinate clause).

## Word order

### Subject, verb, object

Statements almost always follow **S-V-O**: subject, verb, object. This also applies in subordinate clauses and when another part of the sentence comes first.

| German                                           | English                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Gestern **habe ich** einen Fehler gefunden.      | Yesterday **I found** a bug.                   |
| ..., weil ich keine Zeit **hatte**.              | ..., because **I had** no time.                |
| Das Buch **habe ich** schon gelesen.             | **I have** already read the book.              |

### Place before time

Adverbials at the end of the sentence follow the order **manner, place, time**, which is usually the reverse of German.

-   `She worked hard (manner) at the office (place) yesterday (time).`
-   `We are going to Vienna on Friday.` (not ~~on Friday to Vienna~~)

The time expression can also go at the beginning: `On Friday we are going to Vienna.`

### Verb and object stay together

No adverb goes between the verb and its direct object: ~~I like very much football.~~ Correct: `I like football very much.` For the position of adverbs see [Adjectives and Adverbs](/en/languages/english/adjectives_and_adverbs/#adverb-position).

## Questions

### Questions with an auxiliary

Questions are formed with **auxiliary + subject + verb**. If there is no auxiliary, you need `do/does/did`.

| Statement                        | Question                                |
| -------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| She is working.                  | **Is she** working?                     |
| They have finished.              | **Have they** finished?                 |
| He can swim.                     | **Can he** swim?                        |
| You like Python.                 | **Do you** like Python?                 |
| She lives in Linz.               | Where **does she** live?                |
| They left early.                 | Why **did they** leave early?           |

### Subject questions

If the question word asks about the **subject**, there is no `do` and the word order stays as in a statement.

-   `Who called you?` (Somebody called you. Who?) versus `Who did you call?` (You called somebody. Whom?)
-   `What happened?` (not ~~What did happen?~~)

### Indirect questions

Polite indirect questions begin with an introduction such as `Could you tell me ...`, `Do you know ...` or `I would like to know ...`. This is followed by **statement word order**, without `do/does/did`.

| Direct question                  | Indirect question                                      |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Where is the station?            | Could you tell me where **the station is**?            |
| When does the course start?      | Do you know when **the course starts**?                |
| Is breakfast included?           | I would like to know **if/whether breakfast is** included. |

The question mark depends on the introduction: after `Could you tell me ...?` there is one, after `I would like to know ...` there is not.

### Question tags

A question tag is a short question at the end of a sentence. It is formed with the auxiliary of the sentence and a pronoun.

-   **positive sentence, negative tag:** `You're coming, aren't you?`
-   **negative sentence, positive tag:** `She doesn't like tea, does she?`
-   no auxiliary in the sentence: `do/does/did`: `He works here, doesn't he?`

| Special case                                | Example                                             |
| ------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| `I am`                                      | `I'm late, aren't I?`                               |
| `Let's`                                     | `Let's start, shall we?`                            |
| imperative                                  | `Close the door, will you?` / `could you?`          |
| negative words (`never`, `nobody`, `hardly`) | `You never call, do you?`                          |
| `nobody`, `everybody`, `someone`            | `Nobody saw it, did they?`                          |
| `there is`                                  | `There's a problem, isn't there?`                   |

With falling intonation you expect agreement; with rising intonation it is a real question.

## Relative clauses

Relative clauses give additional information about a noun.

| Relative pronoun | Refers to                    | Example                                                   |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| `who`            | people                       | `The woman who called is our client.`                     |
| `which`          | things, animals              | `The laptop which I bought is fast.`                      |
| `that`           | people and things (defining clauses only) | `The app that we developed won a prize.`     |
| `whose`          | possession (people and things) | `The student whose project won is from Krems.`          |
| `whom`           | people as object (formal)    | `The manager to whom I spoke was helpful.`                |
| `where`          | place                        | `This is the room where the servers are.`                 |
| `when`           | time                         | `I remember the day when we met.`                         |
| `why`            | reason (after `the reason`)  | `That's the reason why I left.`                           |

### Defining and non-defining relative clauses

| Feature                       | Defining                                                | Non-defining                                                  |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| function                      | identifies who or what is meant                         | adds extra information                                        |
| commas                        | **none**                                                | **with** commas                                               |
| `that` possible               | yes                                                     | no                                                            |
| pronoun can be left out       | yes, if it is the object                                | no                                                            |
| example                       | `Students who study regularly pass the exam.`           | `My brother, who studies in Graz, is a developer.`            |

If the relative pronoun is the **object** of the relative clause, it can be left out in a defining clause (_contact clause_): `The book (that) I'm reading is great.` As the subject it must stay: `The man who lives next door ...`

`which` can refer to a **whole clause**; then there is always a comma: `He passed the exam, which surprised everyone.`

:::caution[Commas]
In German, every relative clause is preceded by a comma. In English, only non-defining ones are. A comma changes the meaning: `My sister who lives in London` (I have several sisters) versus `My sister, who lives in London,` (I have only one).
:::

### Prepositions in relative clauses

-   formal: preposition before `whom/which`: `the company for which I work`
-   neutral: preposition at the end: `the company (that) I work for`

## Linking words and connectors

Linking words connect ideas and show the logic of a text. They matter for essays, articles and reports in the criterion _Organisation and Layout_.

| Function               | Linking words                                                                    |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| listing, sequence      | `firstly`, `secondly`, `first of all`, `to begin with`, `then`, `finally`, `lastly` |
| addition               | `and`, `also`, `in addition (to)`, `furthermore`, `moreover`, `besides`, `as well as`, `not only ... but also` |
| contrast               | `but`, `however`, `nevertheless`, `although`, `even though`, `though`, `whereas`, `while`, `on the other hand`, `in contrast`, `despite`, `in spite of` |
| reason                 | `because`, `as`, `since`, `because of`, `due to`, `owing to`                     |
| result                 | `so`, `therefore`, `consequently`, `as a result`, `thus`, `that is why`          |
| purpose                | `to`, `in order to`, `so as to`, `so that`                                       |
| example                | `for example`, `for instance`, `such as`, `e.g.`, `in particular`, `especially`   |
| conclusion             | `in conclusion`, `to sum up`, `all in all`, `on balance`, `overall`              |

### Sentence structure with linking words

Linking words belong to different word classes, which determines what follows them.

| Type                          | Examples                                     | Example sentence                                                     |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| conjunction + clause          | `although`, `because`, `whereas`, `while`    | `Although the app is free, few people use it.`                       |
| preposition + noun/`-ing`     | `despite`, `in spite of`, `because of`, `due to` | `Despite being free, the app is rarely used.`                    |
| adverb (new sentence, comma)  | `however`, `therefore`, `moreover`, `nevertheless` | `The app is free. However, few people use it.`                 |

:::caution[Common mistakes]
-   ~~despite of~~ is wrong: `despite` or `in spite of`.
-   ~~Although ..., but ...~~: English does not use a second `but`.
-   `however` does not join two sentences with a comma: ~~It is free, however few people use it.~~ Correct: `It is free. However, few people use it.` or `It is free; however, few people use it.`
:::

## Participle clauses

Participle clauses shorten subordinate clauses and make texts more compact. The subject of the participle clause must be the same as that of the main clause.

| Form                      | Meaning                          | Example                                                     | Full form                                       |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| present participle (`-ing`) | active, simultaneous or reason | `Feeling tired, I went to bed early.`                       | Because I felt tired, ...                       |
| past participle           | passive                          | `Built in 1900, the school needs renovation.`               | Because it was built in 1900, ...               |
| perfect participle        | earlier action                   | `Having finished the report, she went home.`                | After she had finished the report, ...          |

Participles also shorten relative clauses:

-   `The man standing at the door is my teacher.` (= who is standing)
-   `The software developed by our team is open source.` (= which was developed)

:::caution[Dangling participle]
If the subjects do not match, the result is a mistake that often sounds funny: ~~Walking to school, the rain started.~~ (The rain is walking to school.) Correct: `Walking to school, I got caught in the rain.`
:::

## Inversion for emphasis

When certain negative or limiting expressions come at the beginning of a sentence, **question word order** follows (auxiliary before subject). This sounds formal and emphatic and suits essays and speeches.

| Expression                         | Example                                                               |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `Never (before)`                   | `Never have I seen such a fast computer.`                             |
| `Rarely`, `Seldom`                 | `Rarely do we get such good feedback.`                                |
| `Not only ... but also`            | `Not only is the app free, but it is also open source.`               |
| `Hardly/Scarcely ... when`         | `Hardly had I logged in when the system crashed.`                     |
| `No sooner ... than`               | `No sooner had we started than the power went off.`                   |
| `Only then`, `Only after`, `Only when` | `Only after the update did the problem disappear.`                |
| `Under no circumstances`           | `Under no circumstances should you share your password.`              |
| `Little`                           | `Little did I know that the email was a phishing attempt.`            |
| `Not until`                        | `Not until the next day did we notice the error.`                     |

### Cleft sentences

A **cleft sentence** emphasises one part of the sentence by splitting it:

-   `It was Anna who found the bug.` (not someone else)
-   `What we need is a better backup strategy.`
