The **early modern period** (c. 1500 to 1789) was a time of upheaval: a new image of humanity, the printing press, the discovery of other continents and the division of the Church changed Europe fundamentally. At the same time, the modern, centrally administered state emerged with civil servants, a standing army and taxes. For Austria, it was the time in which the Habsburgs built a large empire in the Danube region and defended it against the Ottoman Empire.

## Renaissance and humanism

The **Renaissance** ("rebirth") began in the 14th and 15th centuries in the wealthy cities of Italy, especially in Florence under the Medici. Artists and scholars took antiquity as their model and placed the **human being** at the centre as a free, creative individual. Instead of focusing only on the afterlife, people turned to the world here and now, which they wanted to observe and explore.

- **Art**: central perspective, lifelike depiction of the body (Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo, Raphael, Albrecht Dürer).
- **Humanism**: scholars studied ancient texts in the original (_ad fontes_, "to the sources"). Erasmus of Rotterdam published the New Testament in Greek. Conrad Celtis worked in Vienna.
- **Science**: Nicolaus Copernicus presented the **heliocentric world view** in 1543; Galileo Galilei confirmed it with telescope observations and was forced to recant by the Inquisition in 1633. Johannes Kepler, who also taught in Graz and Linz, described planetary orbits as ellipses. The method of **observation, experiment and mathematics** founded modern natural science (scientific revolution, Isaac Newton 1687).

### The printing press

Around 1450 in Mainz, **Johannes Gutenberg** developed printing with **movable type** made of metal, a casting device for the type, a suitable ink and the printing press. The Gutenberg Bible was produced around 1454/55. Before that, books were copied by hand and were correspondingly expensive and rare.

The printing press was a media revolution comparable to the internet: by 1500, an estimated 30,000 titles had been printed (**incunabula**). Knowledge spread faster, became standardised and was harder to control. Without pamphlets, the Reformation would not have spread so quickly. The authorities responded with **censorship** (papal Index of Prohibited Books from 1559).

## Discoveries and colonialism

In the 15th century, Portugal and Spain looked for a sea route to Asia in order to trade in spices, silk and gold without Ottoman and Venetian middlemen. This was made possible by new ships (the caravel), the compass, the astrolabe, better maps and knowledge that the Earth is a sphere. Motives were trade and wealth, glory, competition between the powers and the spread of Christianity.

- In **1492**, **Christopher Columbus**, sailing for Spain, reached the Caribbean; he believed he was in India (hence "Indians", "West Indies"). The continent was named after Amerigo Vespucci.
- In **1498**, **Vasco da Gama** reached India by sailing around Africa.
- From **1519 to 1522**, Ferdinand Magellan's expedition completed the first circumnavigation of the globe; Magellan himself died on the way.

### Consequences

- **Conquest of America**: Hernán Cortés destroyed the Aztec Empire from 1519 to 1521, Francisco Pizarro the Inca Empire from 1532. Violence, forced labour and above all introduced diseases such as smallpox reduced the indigenous population of the Americas in the 16th century by a large proportion, according to many estimates by up to 90 %.
- **Colonialism**: European states established colonies from which they obtained raw materials (silver from Potosí, sugar, tobacco, cotton). Spain and Portugal divided the world between them in the Treaty of Tordesillas in 1494; later the Netherlands, England and France followed.
- **Transatlantic slave trade**: between the 16th and 19th centuries, around 12 million people were abducted from Africa to the Americas to work on plantations (**triangular trade** between Europe, Africa and America). Millions died during the crossing.
- **Columbian exchange**: plants and animals changed continents: potatoes, maize, tomatoes, cocoa and tobacco came to Europe; horses, cattle, wheat and sugar cane to the Americas.
- **Economy**: the focus of trade shifted from the Mediterranean to the Atlantic. Large trading companies (East India Companies) and banking houses such as the **Fuggers** in Augsburg, who also financed the Habsburgs, gained power. The influx of silver contributed to price inflation in Europe.

## Reformation and Counter-Reformation

Around 1500, the Church was under criticism: the worldliness and wealth of many clergymen, the buying of offices and above all the **sale of indulgences**, in which the remission of punishment for sins was promised in exchange for money. Among other things, the proceeds financed the new St. Peter's Basilica in Rome.

### Martin Luther

The Augustinian monk and professor of theology **Martin Luther** published **95 Theses** against indulgences in Wittenberg on **31 October 1517**. His key statements:

- People are not made righteous by good works or indulgences, but by faith and the grace of God alone (_sola fide_, _sola gratia_).
- The only standard is the Bible (_sola scriptura_), not tradition or the pope.
- **Priesthood of all believers**: every Christian has direct access to God.

In 1521, at the **Diet of Worms**, Luther refused to recant before Emperor **Charles V** and was outlawed. At the Wartburg he translated the New Testament into German, which strongly shaped the German written language. Many territorial princes joined the Reformation, partly because it allowed them to seize church property and strengthen their power. Their protest against a resolution of the Diet in 1529 gave the **Protestants** their name.

Other reformers were **Huldrych Zwingli** in Zurich and **John Calvin** in Geneva (doctrine of predestination, influence in Switzerland, France, the Netherlands and Scotland). In England, Henry VIII broke with Rome in 1534 (Anglican Church). Radical groups such as the Anabaptists were persecuted by all sides.

In the **Peasants' War** of 1525, peasants in southern Germany, Tyrol (Michael Gaismair) and Salzburg invoked the Reformation and in the **Twelve Articles** demanded the abolition of serfdom and lower dues. Luther opposed them; the uprisings were bloodily suppressed.

The **Peace of Augsburg** of 1555 recognised the Lutheran confession. The principle _cuius regio, eius religio_ ("whose realm, his religion") let the territorial ruler decide the confession of his subjects; those of other faiths could emigrate. This did not mean religious freedom for individuals.

### Counter-Reformation and Catholic reform

The Catholic Church responded with internal reforms and the recovery of lost territories. The **Council of Trent** (1545 to 1563) confirmed Catholic doctrine, banned the buying of offices and improved the training of priests. The **Jesuit order**, confirmed in 1540, worked through schools and universities. **Baroque** became the style of the Counter-Reformation: magnificent churches such as Melk or the Karlskirche were intended to make faith a sensory experience.

In the Habsburg lands, a large part of the nobility and population had become Protestant in the 16th century. From around 1600, the Habsburgs enforced **re-Catholicisation**: Protestants had to convert or emigrate. In Salzburg, Prince-Archbishop Firmian expelled more than 20,000 Protestants as late as 1731/32. Groups of **crypto-Protestants** survived in secret until Joseph II granted toleration in 1781.

## The Thirty Years' War

The **Defenestration of Prague** on 23 May 1618, in which Protestant nobles threw two imperial governors out of a window of Prague Castle, triggered an uprising of the Bohemian estates against the Habsburgs. It developed into a European war (1618 to 1648) about confession, the power of the emperor over the imperial princes and supremacy in Europe.

| Phase | Content |
| ----- | ------- |
| Bohemian-Palatinate War (1618 to 1623) | imperial victory at the White Mountain 1620; re-Catholicisation of Bohemia |
| Danish-Lower Saxon War (1625 to 1629) | successes of imperial armies under Wallenstein and Tilly |
| Swedish War (1630 to 1635) | intervention of Gustavus Adolphus of Sweden on the side of the Protestants |
| Swedish-French War (1635 to 1648) | Catholic France fights the Catholic Habsburgs: power politics instead of confession |

The war devastated large parts of Central Europe. Mercenary armies lived off the land ("war feeds war"), followed by epidemics and famine. In some regions of Germany half the population or more died.

The **Peace of Westphalia** of Münster and Osnabrück in 1648:

- equal rights for Catholics, Lutherans and Reformed in the empire
- the imperial princes gained far-reaching sovereignty, imperial power was weakened
- the Netherlands and Switzerland finally left the empire
- it is regarded as the beginning of a system of **sovereign, equal states** in Europe that settle their conflicts through diplomacy and treaties (**Westphalian system**)

## Absolutism

Under **absolutism**, the monarch claimed sole power "released" (_legibus absolutus_) from laws and estates, justified by the **divine right of kings**. The model example is **Louis XIV** of France (1643 to 1715), the "Sun King", to whom the sentence "I am the state" is attributed.

Pillars of absolutist rule:

- a **standing army** instead of hired mercenaries
- a **bureaucracy** dependent on the king
- the **court**: the Palace of Versailles bound the nobility to the court and made it dependent on the king; it became the model for Schönbrunn.
- **Mercantilism**: economic policy (finance minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert) aimed to bring as much money as possible into the country: promote exports of finished goods, limit imports with tariffs, found manufactories, obtain raw materials from colonies, build roads and canals.
- **State church**: the Church was placed in the service of the state.

In England, by contrast, the power of the king was limited by Parliament: after civil war and the **Glorious Revolution** (1688/89), the **Bill of Rights** of 1689 guaranteed the rights of Parliament (taxes and laws only with its consent). England developed into a **constitutional** and later **parliamentary monarchy**.

## The Ottoman sieges of Vienna

The **Ottoman Empire** had conquered Constantinople in 1453 and expanded into the Balkans. After the victory at **Mohács** in 1526 over King Louis II of Bohemia and Hungary, who died in the battle, both crowns passed to the Habsburg Ferdinand I on the basis of an inheritance treaty. This began the **Habsburg Monarchy** as a union of lands in the Danube region, but most of Hungary remained Ottoman for 150 years.

| | First siege 1529 | Second siege 1683 |
| - | ---------------- | ----------------- |
| Ottoman leadership | Sultan Suleiman I (the Magnificent) | Grand Vizier Kara Mustafa |
| Duration | c. 27 September to 14 October 1529 | 14 July to 12 September 1683 |
| Defence | Count Niklas Salm | Count Ernst Rüdiger Starhemberg |
| End | siege abandoned because of the onset of winter, epidemics and supply problems | relief battle at the **Kahlenberg** by an army under the Polish king **Jan III Sobieski** and Duke Charles of Lorraine |

After 1683 the Habsburgs went on the offensive (**Great Turkish War**). In the Peace of Karlowitz in 1699 they gained almost all of Hungary and Transylvania. **Prince Eugene of Savoy** became famous as a general (victory at Zenta 1697, capture of Belgrade 1717) and built the Belvedere in Vienna. Austria rose to become a **great power**.

In the culture of remembrance, the sieges were long interpreted as a defensive struggle of the "Christian West" and were also used politically. Today research equally emphasises the many cultural and economic contacts between Habsburgs and Ottomans.

## Maria Theresa and Joseph II

### Maria Theresa (1740 to 1780)

Because Emperor Charles VI had no son, he laid down in the **Pragmatic Sanction** of 1713 that the Habsburg lands were indivisible and could also be inherited by a daughter. When **Maria Theresa** began her rule in 1740, several powers did not recognise this. In the **War of the Austrian Succession** (1740 to 1748) she held her ground but lost Silesia to Prussia under Frederick II. Nor could she win Silesia back in the **Seven Years' War** (1756 to 1763). The **dualism** between Austria and Prussia shaped German history until 1866. Her husband Francis I Stephen became emperor; Maria Theresa herself held the titles Queen of Hungary and Bohemia and Archduchess of Austria, but was generally called "Empress".

Her reforms were intended to strengthen the state and belong to **enlightened absolutism**:

- **Administrative reform**: central authorities in Vienna, separation of justice and administration, district offices; restriction of the power of the provincial estates
- **Tax reform**: taxation of nobility and clergy as well, land tax register
- **Army reform**: standing army, Military Academy in Wiener Neustadt (1751), conscription
- **Education**: in 1774 the **General School Ordinance** by Johann Ignaz von Felbiger with **six years of compulsory education** and elementary, secondary and teacher-training schools
- **Justice**: _Constitutio Criminalis Theresiana_ (1768) as a uniform criminal law, abolition of torture in 1776
- **Protection of peasants**: limits on the robot (labour service) through robot patents

### Joseph II (1780 to 1790)

Her son **Joseph II** was co-regent and emperor from 1765 and ruled alone after his mother's death. He implemented reforms more radically ("Everything for the people, nothing by the people"):

- **Patent of Toleration** 1781: free private worship for Lutherans, Reformed and Orthodox Christians; further patents of toleration from 1782 gave Jews more rights.
- **Abolition of serfdom** (1781 for Bohemia, Moravia and Silesia, then in other lands): peasants were allowed to marry, move and choose an occupation. Manorialism and the robot remained.
- **Dissolution of monasteries**: around 700 monasteries that were not active in pastoral care, teaching or nursing were dissolved; their assets formed the Religious Fund, which financed new parishes (**Josephinism**: state control of the church).
- **Health and welfare**: General Hospital in Vienna (1784), the Narrenturm (asylum tower), institute for the deaf
- **Law**: Josephine Code as a forerunner of the ABGB, abolition of the death penalty in ordinary proceedings (1787)
- **German as the official language** in all lands, including Hungary

Many reforms met resistance from the nobility, the Church and non-German-speaking lands (uprisings in Hungary and the Austrian Netherlands). Shortly before his death, Joseph withdrew some of them. Toleration, the emancipation of the peasants and modern administration nevertheless remained in effect. The intellectual foundations of these reforms are covered on the page [The Age of Revolutions](/en/history-and-politics/age-of-revolutions/).
