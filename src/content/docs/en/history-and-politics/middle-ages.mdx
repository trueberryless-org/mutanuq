The **Middle Ages** stretch roughly from 500 to 1500. The term comes from humanists of the 15th and 16th centuries, who saw their own time as a rebirth of antiquity and disparagingly called the time in between a "middle age". In fact, many foundations of present-day Europe emerged in the Middle Ages: the division into kingdoms and territories, towns with their own rights, universities, the separation of spiritual and secular power, and Austria as a political unit.

The Middle Ages are usually divided into the **Early Middle Ages** (c. 500 to 1050), **High Middle Ages** (c. 1050 to 1250) and **Late Middle Ages** (c. 1250 to 1500).

## The Migration Period

From 375, the **Huns** advanced westwards from Central Asia and triggered movements of Germanic groups (Goths, Vandals, Franks, Lombards and others). The term "migration of peoples" (_Völkerwanderung_) is disputed today: it was not closed "peoples" that moved, but bands of warriors with their families who regrouped along the way. Many of them were admitted into the Roman Empire as allies (_foederati_). As a result, the Western Roman Empire gradually lost control of its provinces. Germanic kingdoms emerged on its territory, such as Theodoric's Ostrogothic kingdom in Italy and the Visigothic kingdom in Spain. Most of them soon disappeared again; the kingdom of the **Franks** endured.

In the Eastern Alps, **Bavarians** settled in the 6th century (in the west and along the Danube) and **Slavs** (in the east and south, principality of Carantania in present-day Carinthia), joined by the **Avars** in the Pannonian region.

## The Frankish Empire

The Merovingian **Clovis I** united the Frankish sub-tribes and had himself baptised as a Catholic around 500. This won him the support of the Romanised population and the Church. Later, the mayors of the palace from the **Carolingian** family took power. Charles Martel stopped an Arab advance from Spain at Tours and Poitiers in 732.

**Charlemagne** (king from 768, emperor 800 to 814) extended the empire over large parts of Western and Central Europe, subjugated the Saxons, defeated the Avars and set up border marches in the east, including a march in the area of present-day Lower Austria. On Christmas Day 800, Pope Leo III crowned him emperor in Rome, so that the Frankish Empire claimed to succeed Western Rome. Charlemagne promoted education and writing (**Carolingian Renaissance**, the Carolingian minuscule as the forerunner of our lower-case letters) and ruled as a **travelling king** from changing palaces, advised by counts and royal envoys.

In the **Treaty of Verdun** in 843, Charlemagne's grandsons divided the empire. The Holy Roman Empire later emerged from the East Frankish kingdom, France from the West Frankish kingdom. **Otto I** defeated the Hungarians at the Lechfeld near Augsburg in 955 and was crowned emperor in Rome in 962. The **Holy Roman Empire** (from the 15th century with the addition "of the German Nation") lasted until 1806.

## Feudalism and manorialism

Medieval society was a **society of estates**. According to a widespread idea, there were three estates willed by God: some pray (clergy), others fight (nobility), the third work (peasants). You were born into your estate; social advancement was rare and most likely through the Church.

### Feudalism

Because there was hardly any money and no civil servants, the king bound the powerful to himself through **feudalism** (the fief system):

- The **lord** (king) gave a **vassal** (tenant-in-chief: duke, count, bishop, abbot) a **fief**: land, rights or offices.
- In return the vassal owed **loyalty**, counsel and military service; the lord owed **protection**.
- Tenants-in-chief passed parts of their fiefs on to **sub-vassals** (knights). This created a **feudal pyramid**.
- Over time, fiefs became **hereditary**. As a result the princes became more independent and royal power in the empire weaker. This fragmentation shaped Germany and Italy until the 19th century.

### Manorialism

The great majority of people (around 90 %) lived in the countryside. Under **manorialism** (_Grundherrschaft_), the land belonged to a **lord of the manor** (nobleman, monastery, bishop). The peasants farmed it, had to pay **dues** (in kind, later in money, such as the tithe to the Church) and perform **labour services** (unpaid work on the lord's fields), and were often **unfree** (serfs). The lord held the lower jurisdiction and owed protection. The three-field system, the heavy plough and the horse collar increased yields in the High Middle Ages. Manorialism was only abolished in Austria in 1848 (see [The Age of Revolutions](/en/history-and-politics/age-of-revolutions/)).

## Church and state

The Church was the central institution of the Middle Ages. Monasteries preserved ancient knowledge and ran schools, hospitals and farms. Bishops were often powerful territorial lords at the same time. Important orders were the Benedictines (_ora et labora_), later the Cistercians and the mendicant orders of the Franciscans and Dominicans. Major monasteries were founded in Austria, such as Melk, Kremsmünster, St. Florian, Admont, Heiligenkreuz and Klosterneuburg.

### The Investiture Controversy

The kings appointed bishops and abbots themselves (**investiture**), because these served as a support against the nobility and could not pass on their offices to heirs (**imperial church system**). The church reform movement (starting from the monastery of Cluny), by contrast, demanded the freedom of the Church from secular power.

- In 1075, Pope **Gregory VII** banned lay investiture and claimed precedence over all secular rulers in the _Dictatus Papae_.
- King **Henry IV** declared the pope deposed, whereupon Gregory excommunicated him. The princes threatened to stop recognising Henry.
- In January 1077, Henry went to **Canossa** as a penitent and obtained release from the ban. "Going to Canossa" has since meant a humiliating submission.
- The **Concordat of Worms** in 1122 separated the spiritual office (ring and staff, from the pope) from the secular rights (sceptre, from the king).

The dispute had long-term consequences: it called into question the sacred status of kingship and thus contributed to the distinction between the spiritual and secular spheres, a precondition for the later secular state.

### The Crusades

In 1095, Pope **Urban II** called in Clermont for the liberation of Jerusalem, which had been under Muslim rule since the 7th century. Motives were religious zeal, the prospect of forgiveness of sins, land and booty, love of adventure, the trading interests of Italian maritime cities and the wish to direct the feuds of the nobility outwards. The First Crusade conquered Jerusalem in 1099 with a massacre of Muslims and Jews; on the way, Jewish communities in the Rhineland had already been murdered. **Crusader states** were established and lasted until 1291 (fall of Acre).

Important for Austria: the English king **Richard the Lionheart** was captured at Erdberg near Vienna in 1192 on his return from the Third Crusade and held at Dürnstein Castle because he had insulted Duke Leopold V. The ransom financed, among other things, the Vienna city wall and the founding of Wiener Neustadt.

Consequences of the Crusades: lasting hostility and mutual prejudice between the Christian and Islamic worlds, the sack of Constantinople in 1204 in the Fourth Crusade, a revival of long-distance trade across the Mediterranean, contact with Arab science (medicine, mathematics, Arabic numerals), but also growing anti-Judaism in Europe.

## Towns and trade

From the 11th century, the population grew and many towns were founded. They arose on trade routes, river crossings, bishops' seats and castles. One principle was "**town air makes you free**": anyone who lived in a town for a year and a day without being reclaimed by their lord became free.

- Towns received **town charters** from the town lord: market rights, the right to fortify, their own court and self-government by a **council** with a mayor. Vienna received a town charter in 1221 and the **staple right**: merchants passing through had to offer their goods for sale in Vienna.
- Urban society was stratified: **patricians** (rich merchants), **craftsmen** in **guilds**, which regulated prices, quality and training, and a lower class of day labourers, servants and the poor. Jews often lived in separate quarters, were excluded from guild crafts and frequently persecuted, for example in the **Vienna Gesera** of 1420/21, when the Jewish community of Vienna was expelled and more than 200 people were burned.
- Long-distance trade connected Europe: the **Hanseatic League** dominated trade on the North Sea and the Baltic (Lübeck, Hamburg, Bruges, Novgorod), northern Italian cities such as Venice and Genoa trade with the Orient. Merchants from Augsburg and Nuremberg mediated between the two.
- Trade brought a **money economy**, banking, cashless payments (bills of exchange) and double-entry bookkeeping (Luca Pacioli 1494, see [Bookkeeping](/en/economy_and_law/accounting/bookkeeping/)).

**Universities** emerged from the 12th century: Bologna, Paris, Oxford, and in the empire Prague (1348) and Vienna (1365, founded by Duke Rudolf IV).

## Babenbergs and early Habsburgs

### The Babenbergs (976 to 1246)

The march on the Danube, which Otto II granted to the Babenberg **Luitpold** (Leopold I) in **976**, was a narrow border strip facing the Hungarians. The Babenbergs gradually extended it to the March and Leitha rivers. In a charter of Emperor Otto III dated 1 November **996**, the area is called **"Ostarrîchi"** for the first time, the oldest known form of the name Austria (Österreich).

| Babenberg | Significance |
| --------- | ------------ |
| Leopold III (the Saint) | founded Klosterneuburg and Heiligenkreuz; patron saint of Lower Austria and Vienna |
| Henry II Jasomirgott | 1156 Privilegium minus: Austria becomes a duchy; residence in Vienna |
| Leopold V | capture of Richard the Lionheart; acquisition of Styria in 1192 (Georgenberg Pact 1186) |
| Leopold VI | heyday, promotion of towns and of courtly love poetry (Walther von der Vogelweide) |
| Frederick II (the Quarrelsome) | fell in 1246 at the Battle of the Leitha; with him the dynasty died out in the male line |

The **Privilegium minus** of 1156 raised Austria to a duchy that could also be inherited through the female line and limited its obligations towards the empire. It compensated Henry Jasomirgott for returning the Duchy of Bavaria to Henry the Lion.

### Ottokar and Rudolf of Habsburg

After the Babenbergs died out, the Bohemian king **Přemysl Ottokar II** took over Austria and other territories. In the empire, after the end of the Hohenstaufen dynasty, there was a period without a generally recognised king (**interregnum**). In 1273 the prince-electors chose the Swabian count **Rudolf of Habsburg** as king, partly because they thought him weak. Rudolf demanded the return of imperial fiefs, defeated Ottokar in **1278** at the Battle of **Dürnkrut and Jedenspeigen** on the Marchfeld and in 1282 enfeoffed his sons with Austria and Styria. This began Habsburg rule in Austria, which lasted until 1918.

The Habsburgs extended their possessions with Carinthia and Carniola (1335), Tyrol (1363) and areas in Vorarlberg. **Rudolf IV the Founder** (1358 to 1365) had the **Privilegium maius** forged to put Austria on a par with the prince-electors (title "archduke"), founded the University of Vienna and began the Gothic rebuilding of St. Stephen's Cathedral. In 1452, **Frederick III** became the first Habsburg to be crowned emperor in Rome; from 1438, with a short interruption in the 18th century, the Habsburgs provided all Roman-German kings and emperors.

## The plague

In 1347, merchant ships from the Black Sea region brought the **plague** to Sicily. By 1351 it had spread across the whole of Europe (**Black Death**). The disease is caused by the bacterium _Yersinia pestis_ and transmitted by fleas and from person to person. Estimates suggest that around a third of the European population died, in some cities half. It reached Vienna in 1349.

Consequences:

- **Pogroms against Jews**: Jews were accused of poisoning wells and were murdered in many towns.
- **Flagellant processions**: people moved through the country and publicly whipped themselves to avert God's punishment.
- **Economic upheaval**: labour became scarce, wages rose, many villages were abandoned (deserted settlements). Lords tried to bind peasants more tightly, which contributed to peasant revolts.
- The plague returned in waves. The **Plague Column** on the Graben in Vienna commemorates the epidemic of 1679, the Karlskirche that of 1713.

The Church lost prestige in these crises, especially as it was itself divided in the 14th century (popes in Avignon, **Western Schism** 1378 to 1417 with at times two and three popes). Critics such as John Wycliffe and **Jan Hus** (burned at the Council of Constance in 1415) prepared the way for the Reformation (see [The Early Modern Period](/en/history-and-politics/early-modern-period/)).
