The **First World War** (1914 to 1918) is regarded as the "seminal catastrophe of the 20th century" (George F. Kennan). It was the first industrialised mass war, cost around 17 million lives, ended the Habsburg Monarchy and three other empires and, with the peace treaties, created an order whose tensions led into the Second World War. For Austria it marks the transition from monarchy to republic.

## Causes

Historians distinguish long-term causes and the trigger.

- **Imperialism**: rivalry for colonies, markets and influence, especially between Germany and Britain.
- **Arms race**: the German naval programme from 1898 challenged Britain; all great powers enlarged their armies.
- **Alliance systems**: two blocs faced each other, so a local conflict could quickly become a major war.
- **Nationalism**: French revanchism (Alsace-Lorraine), Pan-Slavism in Russia, Greater Serbian nationalism, conflicts between nationalities in Austria-Hungary.
- **Balkan crises**: the annexation of Bosnia in 1908 and the Balkan Wars of 1912/13 made the Balkans the "powder keg of Europe". Serbia was strengthened, and Austria-Hungary saw this as an existential threat.
- **Militarism and war planning**: the military had great influence; plans such as the German **Schlieffen Plan** (quick victory in the west through neutral Belgium, then turning east) relied on early mobilisation and narrowed the room for diplomacy.

Despite the Triple Alliance, Italy stayed neutral in 1914 and entered the war on the side of the Entente in 1915, after being promised South Tyrol, Trentino, Trieste and parts of Dalmatia in the Treaty of London.

The question of **war guilt** is still a subject of research. In the 1960s, Fritz Fischer emphasised Germany's responsibility; in 2012, Christopher Clark described a shared responsibility of many actors in "The Sleepwalkers". There is broad consensus that Austria-Hungary and Germany deliberately risked a war in July 1914, but other governments also contributed to the escalation.

## The July Crisis

On **28 June 1914**, the Bosnian Serb grammar school pupil **Gavrilo Princip**, a member of the nationalist group "Young Bosnia", which was supported by the Serbian secret organisation "Black Hand", shot the Austrian heir to the throne **Archduke Franz Ferdinand** and his wife Sophie in **Sarajevo**.

1. The government in Vienna saw the opportunity to eliminate Serbia militarily. On 5/6 July it obtained Germany's unconditional support in Berlin (the **"blank cheque"**).
2. On **23 July**, Austria-Hungary presented Serbia with a 48-hour **ultimatum** that was deliberately worded to be hard to accept (participation of Austrian officials in investigations in Serbia).
3. Serbia accepted most points but rejected the involvement of Austrian officials. Vienna broke off relations and declared war on **28 July 1914**.
4. Russia supported Serbia and mobilised. Germany declared war on Russia (1 August) and France (3 August). When German troops marched into neutral **Belgium** in line with the Schlieffen Plan, **Britain** declared war on 4 August.

Within a week, a regional conflict had become a European war. In all countries, large parts of the urban population initially welcomed the war, and many expected it to be short ("home by Christmas"). In the countryside and among workers the mood was often more subdued.

## Course of the war

### Western Front

The German advance through Belgium and northern France was halted on the **Marne** in September 1914. After that the front, from the North Sea to Switzerland, froze into **trench warfare**: trenches, barbed wire and machine guns made attacks extremely costly. In the **battles of attrition** at **Verdun** (February to December 1916) and on the **Somme** (July to November 1916), hundreds of thousands died without the front shifting significantly.

### Eastern Front and the Balkans

In 1914 Austria-Hungary suffered heavy defeats against Russia in **Galicia** and initially failed in Serbia. Germany won at Tannenberg. In 1915 the Central Powers broke through the Russian front at Gorlice-Tarnów, and Serbia was occupied. The Russian Brusilov Offensive of 1916 brought the Austro-Hungarian army to the brink of collapse; from then on it was heavily dependent on Germany.

### Italian Front

From May 1915, Austria-Hungary and Italy fought along the **Isonzo** and in the **Alps** (Dolomites, Ortler, Carnic Alps). The **twelve Battles of the Isonzo** (1915 to 1917) caused enormous losses; the mountain war claimed many additional victims through avalanches and cold. In October 1917, Austria-Hungary and Germany broke through at **Caporetto** as far as the Piave. An Austrian offensive on the Piave failed in June 1918; at the end of October 1918, the Austro-Hungarian front collapsed at **Vittorio Veneto**.

### New weapons and total war

New features were the mass use of machine guns and artillery, **poison gas** (first used on a large scale at Ypres in 1915), **tanks** (1916), **aircraft**, submarines and airships. The First World War was a **total war**: the whole economy and society were placed in the service of the war effort, and the boundary between front and home blurred. In the Ottoman Empire, the **Armenian genocide** was committed in 1915/16, with an estimated 1 to 1.5 million victims.

### Turning point 1917/18

- Germany's **unrestricted submarine warfare** and its offer to Mexico to fight the USA (Zimmermann Telegram) led the **USA** to enter the war on 6 April 1917.
- In **Russia**, the February Revolution of 1917 overthrew the tsar, and the October Revolution brought the **Bolsheviks** under Lenin to power (see [The Interwar Period](/en/history-and-politics/interwar-period/)). With the **Treaty of Brest-Litovsk** (3 March 1918), Russia left the war.
- The German spring offensive of 1918 failed. With American troops and tanks, the Allies pushed the German army back from August 1918. At the end of September, the German Supreme Army Command demanded an armistice.

## The home front

The war lasted much longer than expected. In Austria-Hungary, the situation of the civilian population deteriorated dramatically:

- **Supply crisis**: the British naval blockade, the loss of workers and Hungary's reluctance to deliver food led to hunger. From 1915, bread, later flour, milk, fat, sugar, potatoes and coal were **rationed** (ration cards). People queued for hours, and substitute products became part of everyday life.
- **Women's work**: women replaced conscripted men in factories (munitions production), on trams and in offices. This strengthened their demand for political equality.
- **War economy and repression**: Parliament (the Reichsrat) was not convened from March 1914 to May 1917; the government ruled by emergency decree. Military courts, censorship and the War Services Act determined life. Members of non-German nationalities, especially Serbs and Ruthenians, were often suspected of disloyalty across the board; thousands were interned in camps such as Thalerhof near Graz.
- **Propaganda**: posters, postcards, war bonds and patriotic celebrations were intended to boost morale. Karl Kraus sharply criticised war propaganda in "The Last Days of Mankind".
- **Strikes and longing for peace**: in the **January Strike** of 1918, hundreds of thousands of workers in Wiener Neustadt, Vienna and other industrial areas stopped work to demand peace and better supplies. In February 1918, sailors mutinied in Cattaro (Kotor).
- The **Spanish flu** claimed tens of millions of lives worldwide in 1918/19, many in Austria too, including the painter Egon Schiele.

## The end of the monarchy

Emperor **Charles I**, in office from November 1916, tried in vain through his brother-in-law Prince Sixtus of Bourbon-Parma to obtain a separate peace with France (**Sixtus Affair**, made public in 1918). In January 1918, US President **Woodrow Wilson** announced his **Fourteen Points**, including the self-determination of peoples and, for Austria-Hungary, "the freest opportunity to autonomous development".

In autumn 1918 the monarchy disintegrated:

- **16 October 1918**: in the **People's Manifesto**, Charles announced a federal state of national sub-states. It came too late and accelerated the dissolution.
- At the end of October 1918, **Czechoslovakia** (28 October), the State of **Slovenes, Croats and Serbs** (29 October) and Hungary declared independence; Galicia joined Poland.
- On **21 October 1918**, the German-speaking members of the Reichsrat met in Vienna as the **Provisional National Assembly** and on 30 October founded the state of **German-Austria**. Karl Renner (Social Democrat) became State Chancellor.
- On **3 November 1918**, Austria-Hungary signed the armistice with Italy at the **Villa Giusti** near Padua. Because of an error in communicating when it took effect, Austro-Hungarian troops stopped fighting earlier than the Italians, who were thus still able to take more than 300,000 soldiers prisoner.
- On **11 November 1918**, Emperor Charles renounced "all participation in affairs of state", but did not formally abdicate. On the same day the armistice of Compiègne ended the war in the west.
- On **12 November 1918**, the Provisional National Assembly resolved that German-Austria was a **democratic republic** and "part of the German Republic". The republic was proclaimed in front of the Parliament building.

The German Empire (November Revolution, abdication of William II on 9 November 1918), the Russian Empire and the Ottoman Empire also came to an end.

## The Paris peace treaties

In 1919/20 the victors negotiated the peace treaties in suburbs of Paris; the defeated were not involved on an equal footing. At the same time the **League of Nations** was founded, an idea of Wilson's to secure peace, which the USA, however, never joined.

| ------ | ---- | -------------- |
| Versailles | 28 June 1919 | Germany |
| **Saint-Germain-en-Laye** | **10 September 1919** | **Austria** |
| Neuilly-sur-Seine | 27 November 1919 | Bulgaria |
| Trianon | 4 June 1920 | Hungary |
| Sèvres | 10 August 1920 (replaced by Lausanne in 1923) | Ottoman Empire, Turkey |

**Versailles**: Germany lost Alsace-Lorraine, West Prussia and Posen and all its colonies; the Rhineland was demilitarised and occupied, and the army was limited to 100,000 men. Article 231 made Germany and its allies responsible for war damage (basis of **reparations**). In Germany the treaty was perceived as a "dictated peace" and fought by nationalists.

### The Treaty of Saint-Germain

The treaty with Austria was signed on 10 September 1919 and came into force on 16 July 1920. The Austrian delegation was led by State Chancellor Karl Renner.

- Austria was regarded as one of the legal successors of the monarchy and had to share its responsibility for the war (reparations, which were never paid on a large scale).
- The name of the state had to be **"Republic of Austria"**; **union with Germany** was prohibited (Article 88) except with the consent of the Council of the League of Nations.
- **Territorial losses**: South Tyrol and the Canal Valley went to Italy, Lower Styria and the Meža Valley to the SHS state (Yugoslavia), Bohemia, Moravia and Austrian Silesia with more than three million German speakers (Sudeten areas) to Czechoslovakia, and the Valtice area and parts near Gmünd also to Czechoslovakia.
- **Territorial gains**: German West Hungary came to Austria as **Burgenland** (1921). After a disputed plebiscite in December 1921, **Sopron** remained with Hungary.
- **Carinthia**: after fighting against Yugoslav troops (**Carinthian defensive struggle** 1918/19), a **plebiscite** in southern Carinthia on **10 October 1920** decided with around 59 % of the votes to remain with Austria, including the votes of many Carinthian Slovenes.
- The Austrian army was limited to 30,000 professional soldiers, and general conscription was banned.

## Consequences

- **Victims**: around 17 million dead, of whom about 10 million were soldiers; plus more than 20 million wounded. Austria-Hungary mobilised around 8 million men, of whom more than a million died.
- **New states**: on the territory of the Habsburg Monarchy, **successor states** emerged (Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Poland, SHS state). Because the population lived in mixed settlements, almost all new states contained large **minorities**, which created new conflicts.
- **Austria**: a small state with 6.5 million inhabitants, almost a third of them in Vienna, lost its markets, raw materials and food sources. Many doubted that it was **viable** and wanted union with Germany.
- **Politics**: **women's suffrage** was introduced in many states (Austria November 1918, first exercised in the election of 16 February 1919). Monarchies gave way to republics, which were often unstable.
- **Society**: returning soldiers, invalids, widows and orphans, a "lost generation", brutalisation through the experience of violence and the formation of paramilitary groups.
- **World politics**: the USA became the leading economic power and Europe lost weight; in Russia the first communist state emerged. The mandate territories in the Middle East (British: Palestine, Iraq; French: Syria, Lebanon) and contradictory promises to Arabs and Jews (Balfour Declaration 1917) still shape the Middle East conflict today.
