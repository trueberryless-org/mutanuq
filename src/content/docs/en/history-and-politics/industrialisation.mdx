**Industrialisation** changed people's lives more than any event since the transition to settled farming. Within a few generations, an agrarian society in which most people worked on the land became an industrial society with factories, large cities, railways and wage labour. It brought new social problems, new political movements and parties, and the race of the industrial states for colonies. How technology itself developed is covered on the page [History of Technology and Computing](/en/history-and-politics/history-of-technology-and-computing/).

## The Industrial Revolution

Industrialisation began around 1760 in **Great Britain**. Several favourable conditions came together there:

- **Raw materials**: rich deposits of coal and iron ore close to each other
- **Capital** from trade and colonies, a developed banking system
- **Agricultural revolution**: new farming methods and the enclosure of common land increased yields and released labour
- **Population growth**, and with it demand and labour
- **Markets** in the colonies, cotton as a raw material
- **Political stability**, protection of property through patents, freedom of trade
- Transport routes: rivers, canals, coastal shipping

At its core was **mechanisation** with **steam power**. Textile production was mechanised first (spinning machines, looms). The steam engine, which James Watt decisively improved from 1769, made production independent of water power and location. Coal and iron (coke smelting) became key industries. The **railway** connected markets, lowered transport costs and itself created enormous demand for coal, iron and machines.

### Phases

| Phase | Time | Leading sectors and features |
| ----- | ---- | ---------------------------- |
| **Early industrialisation** | from c. 1760 (GB), from c. 1830 (continent) | textiles, steam engine, coal and iron |
| **High industrialisation** | c. 1850 to 1873 | railway construction, heavy industry, mechanical engineering |
| **Second Industrial Revolution** | from c. 1870 | electrical engineering, chemicals, combustion engine, steel; large corporations, research laboratories |

On the continent, Belgium, northern France and parts of Germany (Ruhr, Saxony, Silesia) industrialised first; the USA followed from around 1840 and overtook Britain around 1900.

### Industrialisation in Austria

In the Habsburg Monarchy, industrialisation began later and very unevenly across regions. Centres were **Bohemia and Moravia** (textiles, coal, machinery, Škoda in Pilsen), **Vienna and the southern Vienna Basin** (textiles, mechanical engineering, locomotives), **Styria** (Erzberg, iron and steel) and **Vorarlberg** (textiles). Large parts, especially Galicia and Hungary, remained agrarian.

- From 1837 a railway network developed; the **Semmering Railway** (1854, Carl von Ghega) was Europe's first mountain railway for standard-gauge steam trains and is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- The **emancipation of the peasants** in 1848 and the **Trade Regulation Act** of 1859 (freedom of trade, end of compulsory guild membership) created free labour and entrepreneurship.
- The **Gründerzeit** (founders' period) until 1873 brought a building boom, for example on the Vienna Ringstrasse, and many new companies. The **stock market crash** of 9 May 1873, shortly after the opening of the World Exhibition, triggered a long economic crisis.
- By 1910 Vienna had grown to around 2 million inhabitants, making it one of the largest cities in the world. Growth came mainly from immigration from Bohemia and Moravia.

## The social question

The **social question** was the name given to the problems created by industrialisation:

- **Working conditions**: working hours of 12 to 16 hours a day, six days a week, accidents on unguarded machines, no protection against illness, disability or old age.
- **Child labour** and women's work for particularly low wages.
- **Housing misery**: overcrowded tenement blocks without water or toilets in the flats; in Vienna, families rented out sleeping places by the hour to **bed lodgers** (_Bettgeher_).
- **Pauperism**: mass poverty, diseases such as tuberculosis and cholera.
- Emergence of the **proletariat**: wage labourers who owned nothing but their labour.

### Responses to the social question

| Actor | Response |
| ----- | -------- |
| **Labour movement** | self-help through workers' associations, trade unions, cooperatives and parties; strikes |
| **Employers** | some built company housing, company health insurance funds and schools (paternalistic welfare, e.g. Krupp) |
| **Churches** | charity (Caritas), journeymen's associations (Adolph Kolping); the encyclical **Rerum novarum** (1891) by Pope Leo XIII called for fair wages and recognised workers' associations but rejected socialism: the basis of **Catholic social teaching** |
| **State** | worker protection laws, ban on child labour, **social insurance** |

In the German Empire, **Bismarck** created the first state social insurance: health (1883), accident (1884) and invalidity and old-age insurance (1889). He wanted to bind workers to the state and undermine Social Democracy, which he simultaneously persecuted with the Anti-Socialist Law (1878 to 1890) ("carrot and stick"). Austria followed under Prime Minister Taaffe with the 1885 amendment to the Trade Regulation Act (eleven-hour day in factories, Sunday rest, labour inspectors), accident insurance (1887) and health insurance (1888).

### The labour movement in Austria

From 1867 workers' associations could be founded, and in 1870 the right of association was recognised. The movement was initially split into moderates and radicals. The physician **Victor Adler** united them at the **Hainfeld party congress** (30 December 1888 to 1 January 1889) into the **Social Democratic Workers' Party** (SDAP). Its goals were universal suffrage, the eight-hour day and, in the long term, a socialist society. On **1 May 1890**, tens of thousands of workers demonstrated in the Prater in Vienna for the first time. In 1907, after mass demonstrations, universal and equal **male suffrage** was introduced for the Reichsrat.

## Political ideologies

The great political currents from which today's party families emerged arose in the 19th century (see [Democracy and Political Theory](/en/history-and-politics/democracy-and-political-theory/)).

| Ideology | Core value | Social base in the 19th century | Key demands |
| -------- | ---------- | ------------------------------- | ----------- |
| **Liberalism** | individual freedom | propertied and educated middle class | constitution, fundamental rights, rule of law, free market economy, separation of church and state; voting rights often tied to property |
| **Conservatism** | order, tradition | nobility, Church, peasants | preservation of monarchy, religion and established institutions; reforms only slowly (Edmund Burke) |
| **Socialism** | equality, solidarity | working class | equality, social security, socialisation of the means of production; split into a revolutionary (communism) and a reformist wing (social democracy) |
| **Nationalism** | nation | across social classes | unity and self-determination of the nation; later often exclusionary |
| **Christian social movement** | Christian values | lower middle class, peasants, Catholics | social reform based on Catholic social teaching |

**Adam Smith** founded economic liberalism in 1776: competition in free markets leads, as if by an "invisible hand", to prosperity for all; the state should limit itself to protection, justice and public institutions.

**Karl Marx** and **Friedrich Engels** developed scientific socialism (**Marxism**): history is a history of class struggles. Under capitalism, the bourgeoisie, which owns the means of production, exploits the proletariat. A revolution would lead to the "dictatorship of the proletariat" and finally to a classless society. Main works are the **Communist Manifesto** (1848) and **Capital** (vol. 1, 1867).

In Austria, three political **camps** (_Lager_) formed around 1890 and shaped politics well into the 20th century:

- **Social Democrats** (Victor Adler)
- **Christian Socials** (Karl Lueger, Mayor of Vienna 1897 to 1910; municipalisation of gas, electricity and trams, but also deliberate use of antisemitism)
- **German Nationalists** (Georg von Schönerer: radical German nationalism and racist antisemitism, a model for the young Hitler)

## Imperialism

**Imperialism** refers to the striving of states for rule over other countries, especially the division of Africa and Asia among the European powers, the USA and Japan between about 1880 and 1914 (**high imperialism**).

Motives:

- **economic**: raw materials, markets, capital investment
- **power-political**: prestige, naval bases, rivalry between the great powers
- **ideological**: social Darwinism and racism ("right of the stronger", alleged superiority of white people), a sense of mission ("civilising mission", "white man's burden"), missionary work
- **domestic**: distraction from social tensions, emigration outlet for surplus population

At the **Berlin Conference** of 1884/85, the European powers laid down rules for dividing Africa, without Africans taking part. By 1914, almost all of Africa (except Ethiopia and Liberia) and large parts of Asia had been colonised. Colonial rule was marked by exploitation and violence: in the Congo Free State of the Belgian king Leopold II, millions of people died from forced labour and terror; in German South West Africa, German troops committed **genocide against the Herero and Nama** from 1904 to 1908. Arbitrarily drawn borders still have effects today (see [The Cold War and Decolonisation](/en/history-and-politics/cold-war-and-decolonisation/)).

Austria-Hungary had no overseas colonies (apart from a small concession in Tianjin, China). Its area of expansion was the **Balkans**: in 1878 it occupied Bosnia-Herzegovina after the Congress of Berlin and annexed it in 1908. This intensified the conflict with Serbia and Russia.

## Austria-Hungary

### From neo-absolutism to the Compromise

After the defeats of 1859 and 1866, neo-absolutism had failed. The state needed the consent of its peoples, above all of the self-confident Hungarians. The **Compromise** (_Ausgleich_) of **1867** created the **Dual Monarchy of Austria-Hungary**:

- two states with their own parliament, government and constitution: "the kingdoms and lands represented in the Reichsrat" (**Cisleithania**, unofficially "Austria") and the lands of the Hungarian crown (**Transleithania**). The border roughly followed the river Leitha.
- common to both were the **monarch** (Francis Joseph as Emperor of Austria and King of Hungary, crowned in Budapest in 1867), the **army**, **foreign policy** and the **finances** needed for them (k. u. k. = "imperial and royal")
- customs and trade union, renegotiated every ten years

For Cisleithania, the **December Constitution** was issued in December 1867. Its **Basic Law on the General Rights of Nationals** of 21 December 1867 guarantees equality before the law, personal liberty, inviolability of property, and freedom of religion, opinion, assembly and association. It is still part of the constitution of the Republic of Austria today. Article 19 guaranteed all "ethnic groups" equal rights for their language in schools, offices and public life.

### The nationalities question

Austria-Hungary was a **multi-ethnic state**. According to the 1910 census it had around 51 million inhabitants; the largest language groups were Germans (c. 23 %), Hungarians (c. 20 %), Czechs, Poles, Ruthenians (Ukrainians), Romanians, Croats, Slovaks, Serbs, Slovenes and Italians. No group had a majority.

- The Compromise favoured Germans and Hungarians. Other peoples, especially the **Czechs**, demanded similar rights (trialism). In Hungary, strict **Magyarisation** was pursued.
- Language dispute: the **Badeni language ordinances** of 1897, which sought to introduce Czech as a second internal official language in Bohemia and Moravia, triggered fierce protests by German nationalists and tumult in parliament and were withdrawn. Afterwards the Reichsrat was often paralysed by obstruction.
- The Moravian Compromise of 1905 showed a possible solution with national electoral registers.
- Some of the nationalities sought autonomy within the monarchy, others (South Slav and Italian nationalists) union with nation states. These tensions contributed significantly to the outbreak of the [First World War](/en/history-and-politics/first-world-war/).

At the same time, Vienna around 1900 was a world city of culture and science (**Viennese Modernism**: Sigmund Freud, Gustav Klimt, Otto Wagner, Gustav Mahler, Arthur Schnitzler, Ludwig Boltzmann).
