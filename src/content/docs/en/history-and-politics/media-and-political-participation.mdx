Democracy requires citizens to be informed and able to form their opinions freely. Almost everything we know about politics reaches us through the **media**. This is why free media are regarded as a precondition of democracy, and why authoritarian regimes first try to control the media. Over the last twenty years, social media have changed who spreads news, how fast it spreads and how easily false information arises. At the same time they open up new opportunities for participation. This page combines both topics: critical media use and your own political action.

## The media in a democracy

### Functions

| Function | Meaning |
| -------- | ------- |
| **Information** | report completely, objectively and understandably on events and connections |
| **Opinion-forming** | present different positions and contribute to debate through commentary |
| **Criticism and scrutiny** | uncover abuses and hold the powerful to account (**investigative journalism**); hence the "fourth estate" |
| **Articulation** | make the interests and concerns of different groups public |
| **Education and entertainment** | convey knowledge, offer culture and entertainment |

Examples of the scrutiny function are the Watergate affair in the USA (1972 to 1974), which led to the resignation of President Nixon, or in Austria the reporting on the "Ibiza video" in 2019, which triggered a government crisis.

### Press freedom

**Freedom of opinion and of the press** is protected in Austria by Art. 13 of the Basic Law of 1867 and Art. 10 ECHR; **censorship** is prohibited. Limits are set by other rights and laws: protection against defamation and insult, personality rights and data protection, the ban on incitement to hatred and the Prohibition Act. The **Media Act** regulates, among other things, the **imprint**, **disclosure** of ownership, the **right of reply** and **editorial confidentiality** (protection of sources). The **Austrian Press Council** is a self-regulatory body that rules on breaches of the Code of Ethics for the Austrian press.

The NGO **Reporters Without Borders** publishes an annual press freedom index. Austria ranks in the European middle field; criticism includes high media concentration, the dependence of many media on government advertising and political pressure on journalists.

### The Austrian media system

- **Dual broadcasting system**: the public broadcaster **ORF** and private broadcasters (licensed since the 1990s). ORF has a legal mandate for objective, impartial and diverse reporting and has been financed since 2024 by a household levy (**ORF contribution**). The composition of its supervisory bodies is repeatedly criticised as too close to political parties; in 2023 the Constitutional Court struck down parts of the appointment rules.
- **Print media**: Austria has a very high **concentration** in the newspaper market by international standards. The tabloid _Kronen Zeitung_ reaches a large part of the population; there are also quality newspapers (e.g. _Der Standard_, _Die Presse_, _Salzburger Nachrichten_, the _Wiener Zeitung_ now only online) and strong regional newspapers.
- **Media subsidies**: the state supports media through press subsidies, subsidies for quality journalism and for digitalisation. **Government advertising** is controversial because it can create dependencies; stricter transparency rules have applied since 2024.
- **Supervision**: **KommAustria** (Communications Authority Austria) supervises broadcasting, platforms and compliance with the Media Transparency Act.

### Features of quality journalism

Serious journalism

- clearly separates **news** (reporting facts) and **commentary** (opinion),
- checks information against at least two independent sources and names them,
- hears both sides (_audiatur et altera pars_),
- labels **advertising** and paid content (_sponsored content_),
- corrects mistakes openly.

**Tabloid journalism**, by contrast, relies on emotions, personalisation, scandal, big pictures and short texts. It is not automatically false, but it often simplifies heavily.

## Propaganda

**Propaganda** is the systematic influencing of opinions and behaviour in the interest of a sender, usually a state or a movement, in which facts are selected, distorted or invented. It appeals mainly to emotions. Historical examples are war propaganda in the [First World War](/en/history-and-politics/first-world-war/) and Nazi propaganda under Joseph Goebbels (see [The Nazi Era and the Second World War](/en/history-and-politics/nazi-era-and-second-world-war/)).

Typical techniques:

- **enemy images** and dehumanisation of opponents (comparisons with animals, "vermin")
- **simplification** of complex problems to a single culprit (scapegoat)
- constant **repetition** of simple slogans
- **emotionalisation** through fear, anger and pride
- **personality cult** and staging of a leader
- claiming that "everyone" thinks this way (_bandwagon_ effect)
- control of all information channels and suppression of dissent

Propaganda must be distinguished from legitimate **political advertising** and **public relations** in a democracy: these also promote one position one-sidedly, but in open competition with other positions and under conditions of free media.

## Disinformation and manipulation

| Term | Meaning |
| ---- | ------- |
| **Disinformation** | deliberately spread false or misleading information intended to deceive or cause harm |
| **Misinformation** | false information passed on without intent to deceive |
| **Fake news** | colloquial term for invented news in the style of real reports; also used as a combat term against unwelcome serious media |
| **Conspiracy narrative** | claim that a secret group secretly controls events; hardly refutable by counter-evidence, because this is interpreted as part of the conspiracy. Many conspiracy narratives follow antisemitic patterns. |
| **Deepfake** | images, videos or audio recordings created or manipulated with AI that appear genuine |
| **Social bots** and **troll farms** | automated or coordinated accounts that artificially amplify topics and simulate opinion trends |
| **Astroturfing** | a fake grassroots movement that is actually controlled by companies or states |

States deliberately use disinformation in **hybrid conflicts** to divide societies and undermine trust in elections and institutions. The EU has therefore used the **Digital Services Act** (DSA, fully applicable since 2024) to oblige large platforms to assess and reduce systemic risks such as disinformation, remove illegal content and make advertising transparent.

### Checking information

1. **Check the source**: who is behind it (imprint)? Is the outlet known and reputable?
2. **Check the content**: do other reputable media report it? Is evidence given? How old is the report?
3. **Check images**: a reverse image search shows whether an image comes from a different context. Look for inconsistencies in videos.
4. **Check the intention**: is the aim to inform or to outrage? Who benefits?
5. Use **fact checks**: for example APA fact checks, Mimikama or Correctiv.
6. **Pause** before sharing: emotional, outrageous content spreads fastest.

The lateral reading method recommends leaving an unknown site and checking what other sources say about it, instead of examining it only from the inside.

## Social media, algorithms and filter bubbles

Social networks such as TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, X and Facebook are the most important news source for many young people. Their content is sorted by **algorithms** optimised for **attention**: what generates many reactions, comments and viewing time is shown more often. This favours emotional, polarising and surprising content (**attention economy**).

- **Filter bubble** (Eli Pariser, 2011): personalised algorithms mainly show users content that matches their previous preferences.
- **Echo chamber**: in groups of like-minded people, opinions reinforce each other, and dissenting voices hardly get through.
- Research shows that the effect of filter bubbles is smaller than often assumed, because most people use several sources. **Selective perception** has a stronger effect: people prefer to seek out information that confirms their opinion (**confirmation bias**).
- Other problems: **hate speech** and **cyberbullying**, dependence on a few platform corporations, collection of personal data, microtargeting in election campaigns (Cambridge Analytica scandal 2018), effects on mental health.
- Opportunities: fast access to information, networking, mobilisation of protests (Arab Spring, Fridays for Future), a voice for groups that hardly appear in traditional media.

In Austria, the **Communication Platforms Act** (2021) obliged large platforms to provide reporting procedures for illegal content; since the DSA, EU law largely applies. Hate postings can be criminal offences (incitement to hatred, dangerous threats, cyberbullying under § 107c of the Criminal Code).

## Political communication

Politics has to be communicated. **Political communication** covers everything that parties, governments, interest groups and the media communicate publicly about politics.

- **Agenda setting**: the media determine less _what_ people think, but strongly _what_ they think about.
- **Framing**: the same facts have a different effect depending on the frame of interpretation and choice of words ("tax relief" or "tax gift", "wave of refugees" or "people seeking protection").
- **Personalisation**: leading candidates are at the centre, programmes less so.
- **Staging** and **spin doctors**: political advisers plan images, messages and the timing of announcements (_message control_).
- **Direct communication** via their own social media channels bypasses journalistic scrutiny.
- **Election campaigns**: posters, TV debates, door-to-door canvassing, online advertising. Campaign spending for National Council elections is capped at 7 million euros per party; the Court of Audit checks compliance.
- **Opinion polls** and projections influence public perception; when interpreting them, consider sample size, margin of error and who commissioned them.

## Civil society

**Civil society** comprises all organisations and initiatives between the state, the market and the private sphere in which people voluntarily work for common concerns: associations, initiatives, foundations, religious communities, movements. There are more than 120,000 registered associations in Austria; a large part of the population volunteers, for example in fire brigades, ambulance services, sport or social institutions.

**Non-governmental organisations** (NGOs) are non-profit organisations independent of governments that work for particular goals. Examples:

| Area | Examples |
| ---- | -------- |
| Human rights | Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, SOS Mitmensch |
| Environment | Greenpeace, WWF, Global 2000 |
| Humanitarian aid and social welfare | Red Cross, Caritas, Diakonie, Volkshilfe, Médecins Sans Frontières |
| Transparency and democracy | Transparency International, epicenter.works (digital rights) |

NGOs inform, do **lobbying** and campaign work, provide practical help and take legal action to enforce rights. Critical questions are asked about their democratic legitimacy, funding and transparency.

**Social movements** are broader, less organised alliances aiming at social change: the labour movement, women's movement, peace movement, environmental movement (in Austria Zwentendorf 1978, Hainburg 1984), civil rights movement, climate movement (Fridays for Future from 2018).

## Demonstrations and freedom of assembly

**Freedom of assembly** is a fundamental right (Art. 12 of the Basic Law, Art. 11 ECHR). Under the **Assembly Act**, a public assembly must be **notified** to the authority (provincial police directorate or district authority) at least 48 hours in advance, but does not need to be approved. The authority can only prohibit it if its purpose violates criminal law or it endangers public safety or the public good. Spontaneous assemblies are generally permitted. Participants must not be armed; face coverings are prohibited in certain cases.

Well-known rallies in Austria include the **Sea of Lights** against xenophobia in 1993, the Thursday demonstrations against the FPÖ's participation in government in 2000, the climate strikes from 2019 and protests against the COVID-19 measures in 2021/22.

## Getting involved: political engagement

Participation is more than voting. You can already take part at many levels:

- **School**: class representative, **head pupil representative** (_Schulsprecher_), school community committee (SGA), provincial and federal pupil representation; the Pupil Representation Act sets out the rights.
- **Municipality**: youth councils, youth parliaments, citizens' meetings, participation procedures in urban planning
- **Federal level**: vote from 16, sign citizens' initiative petitions, parliamentary citizens' initiatives and petitions, comments in the consultation procedure, contact with members of parliament
- **EU**: European elections, the European Citizens' Initiative, petitions to the European Parliament
- **Civil society**: associations, youth organisations of the parties, NGOs, volunteering, the **Federal Youth Council** (_Bundesjugendvertretung_) as the statutory representation of children and young people
- **Public sphere**: letters to the editor (see [Letter to the Editor](/en/languages/german_text_types/letter_to_the_editor/)), blogs, contributions to debates, demonstrations
- **Consumption**: conscious consumption, boycotts and "buycotts" as a political signal

:::tip[In practice]
When you express a view on a political topic, separate facts and opinion, back up claims with sources and engage with counter-arguments. This makes you more convincing, and you contribute to a culture of debate that democracy needs.
:::
