The **European Union** (EU) is a union of **27 states** with around 450 million inhabitants. It is neither a federal state nor a classic confederation, but a **union of states of its own kind** (_sui generis_): the member states have transferred parts of their sovereign rights to the EU, whose law applies directly and takes precedence over national law. For Austria, a large part of the rules that apply in everyday life are co-decided in Brussels and Strasbourg: from roaming rules to the General Data Protection Regulation, product standards and environmental protection.

## History and treaties

The beginnings of European integration after 1945 are described in [Europe and the World Today](/en/history-and-politics/europe-and-the-world-today/). The most important treaties at a glance:

| Treaty | Signed | In force | Content |
| ------ | ------ | -------- | ------- |
| **Treaty of Paris** | 1951 | 1952 | European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC) |
| **Treaties of Rome** | 1957 | 1958 | European Economic Community (EEC) and European Atomic Energy Community (Euratom) |
| Merger Treaty | 1965 | 1967 | common institutions for the three communities (EC) |
| **Single European Act** | 1986 | 1987 | goal of a single market by 1992, more majority decisions |
| **Maastricht Treaty** | 1992 | 1 November 1993 | founding of the **European Union**, EU citizenship, plan for economic and monetary union, cooperation in foreign policy and in justice and home affairs |
| Treaty of Amsterdam | 1997 | 1999 | more rights for Parliament, Schengen becomes EU law |
| Treaty of Nice | 2001 | 2003 | institutional preparation for eastern enlargement |
| (Constitutional Treaty) | 2004 | never | rejected in referendums in France and the Netherlands in 2005 |
| **Treaty of Lisbon** | 13 December 2007 | 1 December 2009 | today's basis: **Treaty on European Union** (TEU) and **Treaty on the Functioning of the EU** (TFEU); Charter of Fundamental Rights becomes binding; Parliament strengthened; permanent President of the European Council; High Representative for foreign policy; European Citizens' Initiative; exit clause (Art. 50 TEU) |

| 2007 | Bulgaria, Romania | 27 |
| 2013 | Croatia | 28 |
| 2020 | **withdrawal of the United Kingdom** (Brexit) on 31 January 2020 | 27 |

Accession requires the **Copenhagen criteria** (1993): stable democratic institutions and rule of law, respect for human rights and protection of minorities, a functioning market economy and the ability to adopt all EU law (the _acquis_). **Candidate countries** are currently Albania, Bosnia-Herzegovina, Georgia, Moldova, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Serbia, Turkey and Ukraine; Kosovo has applied.

## The institutions of the EU

| Institution | Seat | Composition | Main tasks |
| ----------- | ---- | ----------- | ---------- |
| **European Parliament** | Strasbourg (plenary), Brussels, Luxembourg | **720 members** (since 2024), directly elected every five years; Austria: 20 | legislation together with the Council, budget, election of the Commission President, scrutiny of the Commission (motion of censure) |
| **Council of the EU** (Council of Ministers) | Brussels | one minister per state, depending on the topic (e.g. Agriculture Council); presidency rotates every six months | legislation together with Parliament, budget, coordination of policies |
| **European Council** | Brussels | heads of state or government, its President (since December 2024 António Costa) and the Commission President | general political goals and priorities; no legislation; proposes the Commission President |
| **European Commission** | Brussels | 27 members (one per state), President since 2019 **Ursula von der Leyen** | "guardian of the treaties"; **right of initiative** for laws, implementing the budget, infringement proceedings, representation in trade matters |
| **Court of Justice of the EU** (CJEU) | Luxembourg | one judge per state; plus the General Court | uniform interpretation of EU law (**preliminary ruling procedure** at the request of national courts), actions for annulment, infringement proceedings |
| **European Central Bank** (ECB) | Frankfurt am Main | Executive Board and governors of the national central banks of the euro states; President Christine Lagarde | monetary policy for the euro, primary objective **price stability** (inflation of 2 % over the medium term), banking supervision |
| **European Court of Auditors** | Luxembourg | one member per state | audit of EU revenue and expenditure |

:::caution[Easily confused]
The **Council of the EU**, the **European Council** and the **Council of Europe** are three different bodies. The Council of Europe in Strasbourg is not an EU institution at all but a separate international organisation with 46 member states (see [International Organisations and Human Rights](/en/history-and-politics/international-organisations-and-human-rights/)).
:::

### European Parliament

Members do not sit by nationality but in **political groups** of like-minded parties (e.g. European People's Party, Socialists and Democrats, Liberals (Renew Europe), Greens, Patriots for Europe, European Conservatives and Reformists, the Left). The distribution of seats is **degressively proportional**: small states have more seats per inhabitant than large ones (Malta 6, Germany 96 seats). In Austria you can also vote in European elections from the age of 16; the threshold is 4 %. In the European election in June 2024, the FPÖ won 6 seats in Austria, the ÖVP 5, the SPÖ 5, the Greens 2 and NEOS 2.

### Council of the EU

Depending on the area, the Council decides **unanimously** (e.g. foreign and security policy, taxation, accessions) or mostly by **qualified majority**. This is achieved under the **double majority** rule when at least **55 % of the member states** (15 of 27) representing at least **65 % of the EU population** agree. Austria held the Council presidency in 1998, 2006 and 2018.

## Legislation

### Legal acts

A distinction is made between **primary law** (the treaties and the Charter of Fundamental Rights) and **secondary law** (legal acts adopted by the institutions). Art. 288 TFEU names five types:

| Legal act | Binding effect | Application | Example |
| --------- | -------------- | ----------- | ------- |
| **Regulation** | binding in its entirety | **directly** applicable in all member states, without transposition | General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), Roaming Regulation, AI Act |
| **Directive** | binding as to the **result** to be achieved | must be **transposed into national law** within a deadline; states choose form and methods | Consumer Rights Directive, Working Time Directive, NIS 2 Directive on cybersecurity |
| **Decision** | binding on those to whom it is addressed | addressed to specific states, companies or persons | competition fine against a corporation |
| **Recommendation** | not binding | | |
| **Opinion** | not binding | | |

If a state fails to transpose a directive on time, the Commission can open **infringement proceedings**. EU law takes **precedence** over conflicting national law (case law of the CJEU since 1964, _Costa v ENEL_).

### The ordinary legislative procedure

Most EU laws are made in the **ordinary legislative procedure** (Art. 294 TFEU), in which Parliament and Council are equal partners:

1. The **Commission** submits a proposal (monopoly of initiative). Parliament, Council, member states or a **European Citizens' Initiative** (one million signatures from at least seven states) can ask it to do so.
2. **First reading in Parliament**: it adopts its position (with or without amendments).
3. **First reading in the Council**: if it approves Parliament's position, the act is adopted. Otherwise it adopts its own position.
4. **Second reading** in Parliament and Council.
5. In case of continued disagreement: **Conciliation Committee** with equal numbers of representatives of Council and Parliament, followed by a **third reading**.

In practice, Parliament, Council and Commission usually reach agreement in informal **trilogues**, so most laws are adopted at first reading. National parliaments check whether the EU respects the **principle of subsidiarity**: the EU should only act if an objective can be better achieved at EU level than at national level. In Austria, the National Council and the Federal Council take part through their EU committees and can give the government binding opinions.

## The single market and the four freedoms

Since 1993 there has been the **European single market**, an area without internal borders with four **fundamental freedoms**:

| Freedom | Meaning | Example |
| ------- | ------- | ------- |
| **Free movement of goods** | no customs duties or quantitative restrictions between member states; common external tariff | A product approved in Germany may in principle also be sold in Austria (_Cassis de Dijon_). |
| **Free movement of persons** | EU citizens may live, work and study in any member state | studying in the Netherlands, a job in Ireland |
| **Freedom to provide services** | companies may offer services in other member states | An Austrian IT company serves customers in Italy. |
| **Free movement of capital and payments** | money and investments may flow freely across borders | an account in another EU state, SEPA transfers |

In addition, there is the **Schengen Area** without checks on persons at internal borders (29 states, including non-EU states such as Switzerland and Norway; Ireland and Cyprus are not part of it). In the event of threats, states can temporarily reintroduce border controls, which Austria has done since 2015.

## The euro

**Economic and monetary union** was agreed in the Maastricht Treaty. The **euro** was introduced as book money on 1 January 1999 and as cash on **1 January 2002**. Since Bulgaria joined on 1 January 2026, **21 states** have belonged to the euro area.

To take part, the **convergence criteria** (Maastricht criteria) must be met:

- **Price stability**: inflation no more than 1.5 percentage points above the average of the three most price-stable states
- **Budgetary discipline**: annual budget deficit no more than **3 %** and public debt no more than **60 %** of gross domestic product
- **Exchange rate stability**: at least two years in the ERM II exchange rate mechanism without devaluation
- **Interest rates**: long-term interest rates no more than 2 percentage points above the average of the three most price-stable states

The deficit and debt rules continue to apply after accession through the **Stability and Growth Pact**, but they were often breached and were reformed in 2024. Advantages of the euro are the elimination of exchange rate risks and exchange costs and price transparency; a disadvantage is that individual states can no longer pursue their own monetary policy to respond to crises (see the euro crisis in [Europe and the World Today](/en/history-and-politics/europe-and-the-world-today/)).

## Policy areas and budget

The EU may only act in the areas assigned to it by the treaties (**principle of conferral**). A distinction is made between:

- **exclusive competence**: customs union, competition rules for the single market, monetary policy for the euro area, common commercial policy, conservation of fish stocks
- **shared competence**: single market, agriculture, environment, consumer protection, transport, energy, area of freedom, security and justice
- **supporting competence**: education (e.g. **Erasmus+**), culture, health, tourism, industry

The **Common Foreign and Security Policy** (CFSP) is largely decided unanimously. The EU budget is planned through a seven-year **Multiannual Financial Framework** (2021 to 2027: around 1.2 trillion euros, plus the recovery programme **NextGenerationEU** after the pandemic). It amounts to around 1 % of the EU's economic output. The largest items are agricultural and cohesion policy (support for weaker regions).

## Austria in the EU

- **1989** application for membership, **12 June 1994** referendum with 66.58 % yes, accession on **1 January 1995** (see [Austria after 1945](/en/history-and-politics/post-war-austria/))
- Austria is a **net contributor**: it pays more into the EU budget than it directly receives back, but benefits economically from the single market, especially through exports and eastern enlargement.
- **Neutrality and the EU**: Austria takes part in the Common Security and Defence Policy (Art. 23j B-VG). The mutual assistance clause (Art. 42(7) TEU) contains an exception for neutral states (the "Irish clause").
- Austria provides one Commissioner (since December 2024 Magnus Brunner, responsible for internal affairs and migration) and 20 members of the European Parliament.
- Compared with other member states, approval of the EU in Austria tends to be average to below average; topics such as transit traffic, nuclear power, migration and food standards are often controversial.
