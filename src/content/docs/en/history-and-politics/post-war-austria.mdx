The **Second Republic** is regarded as a success story: a destroyed, occupied country became a prosperous, stable, neutral state and later a member of the European Union. The parties that had fought each other in 1934 now worked closely together. At the same time, this consensus had its downsides: the long suppression of the Nazi past, the allocation of posts along party lines (**Proporz**) and politics that was often made behind closed doors.

## The occupation 1945 to 1955

Like Germany, Austria was divided into **four occupation zones**:

| Occupying power | Area |
| --------------- | ---- |
| **Soviet Union** | Lower Austria, Burgenland, Mühlviertel (Upper Austria north of the Danube) |
| **USA** | Salzburg, Upper Austria south of the Danube |
| **Britain** | Carinthia, Styria, East Tyrol |
| **France** | Tyrol, Vorarlberg |

**Vienna** was divided into four sectors; the 1st district (Innere Stadt) was **inter-Allied** and administered jointly by all four powers on a monthly rotating basis ("Four in a Jeep"). The highest authority was the **Allied Council**.

Unlike Germany, Austria had a **common government** for the whole country from the start. Renner's Provisional State Government was recognised by all four powers in October 1945. In the **National Council election of 25 November 1945**, the ÖVP won 85 seats, the SPÖ 76 and the KPÖ only 4, although the Soviet Union had expected a much better result for the communists. **Leopold Figl** (ÖVP) became chancellor of an all-party government, and Karl Renner Federal President. From 1947 a **grand coalition** of ÖVP and SPÖ governed, lasting until 1966. The **Second Control Agreement** (1946) strengthened the government: laws came into force unless the Allied Council unanimously rejected them.

Reconstruction:

- The first post-war years were marked by **hunger** (daily rations at times below 1,000 kcal), housing shortages and the black market. Women cleared the rubble ("rubble women").
- **Nationalisation**: in 1946/47, major banks, heavy industry (VÖEST, Alpine Montan) and the electricity industry were nationalised, partly to protect former German property from Soviet seizure. In its zone, the Soviet Union confiscated former German property (USIA companies, oil).
- **Marshall Plan** (European Recovery Program): from 1948 Austria received around one billion US dollars, one of the highest amounts per capita in Europe. The funds created the ERP Fund, which still grants loans today. They were used, for example, to build the Kaprun power stations.
- The **wage and price agreements** (1947 to 1951) between the social partners were intended to curb inflation. The fourth agreement provoked the **October Strike** in September and October 1950, in which mainly communists took part. The government regarded it as an attempted coup; historians today mostly see it as a strike movement without intent to overthrow the state.
- In 1949, former "less incriminated" National Socialists were allowed to stand again. The **League of Independents** (VdU) entered parliament; the **FPÖ** emerged from it in 1956.

## State Treaty and neutrality

From 1947, negotiations on a state treaty dragged on for years because the Cold War blocked agreement. After Stalin's death (1953), the Soviet Union changed its policy. Chancellor **Julius Raab** and foreign minister **Leopold Figl**, together with the SPÖ politicians Adolf Schärf and Bruno Kreisky, negotiated in Moscow in April 1955. In the **Moscow Memorandum**, Austria undertook to declare neutrality on the Swiss model.

On **15 May 1955**, the foreign ministers of the four powers (Molotov, Macmillan, Dulles, Pinay) and Figl signed the **State Treaty** "for the re-establishment of an independent and democratic Austria" in **Belvedere Palace** in Vienna. Figl then showed the document to the cheering crowd from the balcony. He spoke the famous sentence "Austria is free!" in his speech in the Marble Hall.

Contents of the State Treaty:

- re-establishment of Austria as a sovereign, independent and democratic state within the borders of 1 January 1938
- **ban on union** (Anschluss): no political or economic union with Germany
- rights of the **Slovene and Croat minorities** (Article 7: school teaching, official language, bilingual place-name signs). Implementation was disputed for a long time (**place-name sign dispute** in Carinthia, resolved in 2011).
- ban on National Socialist organisations and on the restoration of the Habsburgs
- ban on certain weapons (e.g. nuclear weapons)
- Austria took over former German property in the Soviet zone in exchange for payment in money and oil deliveries.
- The clause on Austria's shared responsibility for the war was deleted from the preamble at the last minute at Figl's request.

The State Treaty came into force on 27 July 1955; the occupation troops had to withdraw within 90 days. On **26 October 1955**, after the withdrawal, the National Council passed the **Federal Constitutional Law on Permanent Neutrality**. In it, Austria undertakes not to join any military alliances and not to permit the establishment of military bases of foreign states on its territory. 26 October has been the **National Day** since 1965.

Neutrality is **military** neutrality, not neutrality of opinion. Austria understood it as an **active neutrality policy**: membership of the UN (1955) and the Council of Europe (1956), participation in UN peacekeeping missions (from 1960 in the Congo, for many years in Cyprus and on the Golan Heights), Vienna as the seat of international organisations (IAEA 1957, OPEC 1965, UN City 1979, CSCE/OSCE). During the crises in **Hungary in 1956** and **Czechoslovakia in 1968**, Austria took in tens of thousands of refugees. How neutrality has changed since EU accession is described in [International Organisations and Human Rights](/en/history-and-politics/international-organisations-and-human-rights/).

## Social partnership

**Social partnership** is a voluntary cooperation, hardly regulated by law, between the large interest groups of employers and employees and the government.

| Employers' side | Employees' side |
| --------------- | --------------- |
| **Austrian Economic Chamber** (WKÖ), compulsory membership | **Federal Chamber of Labour** (AK), compulsory membership |
| **Chamber of Agriculture** (LK), compulsory membership | **Austrian Trade Union Federation** (ÖGB), voluntary, cross-party with factions |

The social partners negotiate the **collective agreements** every year (minimum wages and working conditions for entire sectors, see [Labour Law](/en/economy_and_law/law/labour_law/)), comment on draft legislation, co-manage social insurance and help shape laws on labour, social affairs and the economy. The **Joint Commission on Wages and Prices**, founded in 1957, was the central body for a long time.

Advantages: very few strikes (Austria has particularly few strike days by international comparison), social peace, predictability for businesses. Criticism: little transparency, decisions made outside parliament, preference for organised interests, close links with the ÖVP (Economic League, Farmers' League) and SPÖ (trade unionists). Its influence has declined since the 1990s, but it remains important, for example for short-time work schemes in the financial crisis of 2009 and the COVID-19 crisis.

## The Kreisky era

After the ÖVP single-party government under **Josef Klaus** (1966 to 1970), the SPÖ under **Bruno Kreisky** won the 1970 election and formed a minority government tolerated by the FPÖ. In 1971, 1975 and 1979 it won an **absolute majority**. Kreisky, who as a Jew and socialist had fled to Sweden during the Nazi era, wanted to "flood" Austria with democracy and modernity.

Important reforms:

- **Education**: free school books (1972), free travel for pupils (1971), abolition of tuition fees (1972), Universities Organisation Act (1975) with student co-determination
- **Family and equality**: **family law reform** (1975: marriage as a partnership instead of the husband as "head of the family"), mother-child health booklet (1974), ministry and state secretariats for women's affairs (Johanna Dohnal)
- **Criminal law**: criminal law reform of 1975 with the **time-limit solution** (abortion not punishable in the first three months), decriminalisation of homosexuality (1971)
- **Military service**: reduction to six months, introduction of **civilian service** (1975)
- **Foreign policy**: active neutrality policy, engagement in the Middle East conflict, construction of the **UN City** (1979)
- **Economy**: **Austro-Keynesianism**: after the oil shock of 1973, the state kept unemployment low through spending and investment, even at the cost of rising public debt ("A few billion schillings of debt worry me less than a few hundred thousand unemployed")

Setbacks were the conflict with Simon Wiesenthal over the Nazi past of FPÖ leader Friedrich Peter (1975), the **AKH scandal** and the **referendum on the Zwentendorf nuclear power plant** on **5 November 1978**: 50.47 % voted against commissioning it. The completed power station never went into operation; since the **Nuclear Ban Act** (1978, given constitutional status in 1999), the use of nuclear fission to generate energy has been banned in Austria.

## The 1980s: upheavals

- **Hainburg**: in December **1984**, thousands of people occupied the **Stopfenreuth wetlands** to prevent the construction of a Danube power station near Hainburg. After police operations, the government abandoned the project. The occupation is regarded as the birth of the Austrian environmental movement; in 1986 the **Greens** entered the National Council, and in 1996 the Donau-Auen National Park was founded.
- **Crisis of the nationalised industries**: losses in the billions in nationalised industry (VOEST-Alpine 1985) led to privatisations.
- **Scandals**: wine scandal (glycol, 1985), Noricum scandal (illegal arms exports), Lucona affair.
- **Jörg Haider** took over the FPÖ in 1986 and turned it into a right-wing populist party; Chancellor Franz Vranitzky (SPÖ) then ended the coalition with the FPÖ and again formed a grand coalition with the ÖVP (1987 to 2000).

### The Waldheim debate

During the 1986 presidential election campaign, it became known that the ÖVP candidate, former UN Secretary-General **Kurt Waldheim**, had concealed in his CVs his time as a Wehrmacht officer in the Balkans (including on the staff of an army group involved in war crimes). He justified himself by saying he had "only done his duty". Waldheim was elected in June 1986. In 1988 an international **historians' commission** found no evidence of personal involvement in war crimes but stated that he must have known about them. The USA put him on its _watch list_; as Federal President (until 1992) he was largely isolated internationally.

The debate triggered the largest public examination of the Nazi past up to that point and led to the revision of the **victim thesis** (see [The Nazi Era and the Second World War](/en/history-and-politics/nazi-era-and-second-world-war/)). The statement of many war veterans that they had only "done their duty" became a symbol of this suppressed past.

## EU accession and the 1990s

After the end of the Cold War, joining the EC became possible despite neutrality. Austria applied for membership in 1989 (the "letter to Brussels"), and negotiations were concluded in 1994. Because accession meant a **total revision of the Federal Constitution**, a **referendum** was mandatory. On **12 June 1994**, **66.58 %** voted yes with a turnout of around 82 %. On **1 January 1995**, Austria joined the European Union together with Finland and Sweden. In 1997 it implemented the Schengen Agreement; in 1999 the euro became book money and in 2002 cash (1 euro = 13.7603 schillings). More on this in [The European Union](/en/history-and-politics/european-union/).

Other developments: during the Yugoslav wars, Austria took in around 90,000 refugees from Bosnia. The FPÖ grew strongly in elections; with the "Austria First" petition for a referendum (1993) it made migration a central issue. In response, the **Sea of Lights** in January 1993 became the largest demonstration in the Second Republic up to that time.

## Recent history

| Time | Development |
| ---- | ----------- |
| 2000 to 2007 | ÖVP-FPÖ government (from 2005 ÖVP-BZÖ) under **Wolfgang Schüssel**. The other 14 EU states froze their bilateral relations (**"sanctions"** of the EU-14) but lifted the measures in September 2000 after a report by three "wise men". Pension reform, purchase of fighter jets, compensation for Nazi-era forced labourers |
| 2007 | **voting age 16** for all federal elections, introduction of **postal voting**, extension of the legislative period to five years |
| 2007 to 2017 | grand coalitions under Gusenbauer, Faymann and Kern (SPÖ); financial and economic crisis 2008/09 with bank rescues (Hypo Alpe Adria) |
| 2013 | referendum (_Volksbefragung_): almost 60 % in favour of retaining conscription and civilian service |
| 2015/16 | **refugee movement**: around 88,000 asylum applications in Austria in 2015; asylum law subsequently tightened |
| 2016 | presidential election: the Constitutional Court annulled the run-off because of irregularities in counting postal ballots; **Alexander Van der Bellen** won the rerun (took office 2017, re-elected 2022). |
| 2017 to 2019 | ÖVP-FPÖ government under **Sebastian Kurz** |
| May 2019 | **"Ibiza affair"**: a secretly recorded video showed FPÖ leader Heinz-Christian Strache offering public contracts to the supposed niece of an oligarch in return for support. The coalition collapsed; on 27 May 2019 the National Council passed a vote of **no confidence** in a federal government for the first time. **Brigitte Bierlein** became the first female chancellor (caretaker government of experts). |
| 2020 to 2024 | ÖVP-Greens government (Kurz, from 2021 Schallenberg, from December 2021 Karl Nehammer); **COVID-19 pandemic** with lockdowns from March 2020; terrorist attack in Vienna on 2 November 2020; in 2022/23 the highest inflation since the 1970s following the Russian attack on Ukraine |
| 29 September 2024 | National Council election: the **FPÖ** became the largest party for the first time (28.85 %). After failed negotiations first between ÖVP, SPÖ and NEOS, then between FPÖ and ÖVP, **ÖVP, SPÖ and NEOS** formed the first three-party coalition since 1947, sworn in on **3 March 2025**, with **Christian Stocker** (ÖVP) as chancellor and **Andreas Babler** (SPÖ) as vice-chancellor. |

The structure of today's political system is covered in [The Austrian Political System](/en/history-and-politics/austrian-political-system/).
