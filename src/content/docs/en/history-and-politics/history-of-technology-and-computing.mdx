Technology changes societies, and societies determine which technology is developed and how it is used. For an HTL, this history is doubly relevant: it explains where the tools you work with every day come from, and it shows that technical decisions always have social, economic and political consequences as well. The social side of the first industrialisation is covered on the page [Industrialisation](/en/history-and-politics/industrialisation/).

## The industrial revolutions

The division into four "industrial revolutions" comes from the discussion around **Industry 4.0**, a term popularised at the Hannover Messe in 2011. Historians view this numbering critically because change usually happens gradually, but it is useful as an overview.

| Stage | Beginning (approx.) | Core technology | Examples | Social consequences |
| ----- | ------------------- | --------------- | -------- | ------------------- |
| **1.0** | late 18th century | **mechanisation** with water and steam power | power loom (1785), steam engine, railway | factory, wage labour, urbanisation, social question |
| **2.0** | c. 1870 | **electrification**, mass production, division of labour | assembly line (Ford 1913), combustion engine, chemicals, telephone | mass consumption, large corporations, white-collar workers, Taylorism |
| **3.0** | c. 1970 | **electronics and IT**, automation | programmable logic controller (PLC, 1969), industrial robots, computers | decline of industrial work, growth of services, globalisation |
| **4.0** | c. 2010 | **cyber-physical systems**, networking, data | Internet of Things, digital twins, AI, additive manufacturing | platform economy, data protection, new skills, debate about jobs |

**Taylorism** (after Frederick W. Taylor, 1911) refers to the scientific breakdown of work into the smallest measured steps. Together with the assembly line in Henry Ford's car plants (**Fordism**), it lowered costs so much that workers could buy the Ford Model T themselves, but it made work monotonous and externally controlled (Charlie Chaplin, "Modern Times", 1936).

## Austrian inventors and engineers

| Person | Achievement |
| ------ | ----------- |
| **Josef Ressel** | ship's propeller (patent 1827) |
| **Peter Mitterhofer** | wooden typewriter models (1864 to 1869) |
| **Siegfried Marcus** | early vehicles with petrol engines (around 1870 and 1888/89) |
| **Carl Auer von Welsbach** | gas mantle, metal filament lamp (osmium), flint (cerium-iron); founded the Treibacher chemical works, his Auergesellschaft registered the Osram trademark in 1906 |
| **Viktor Kaplan** | Kaplan turbine for hydroelectric power stations (1912/13) |
| **Ferdinand Porsche** | Lohner-Porsche with wheel hub motors (1900), later the VW Beetle |
| **Paul Eisler** | printed circuit board (1936 in England) |

Austria was also a leader in steel production: the **LD process** (Linz-Donawitz, basic oxygen steelmaking), introduced in Linz and Donawitz in 1952/53, is still used for most of the world's crude steel.

## History of calculating machines

| Time | Development |
| ---- | ----------- |
| from c. 2500 BC | abacus and counting boards |
| 1623 | Wilhelm Schickard: "calculating clock" with gear wheels |
| 1642 | Blaise Pascal: adding machine "Pascaline" |
| 1673 | Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz: stepped reckoner for all four basic arithmetic operations; later describes the **binary system** |
| 1805 | Joseph-Marie Jacquard: loom controlled by **punched cards** |
| 1822 | Charles Babbage: design of the _Difference Engine_ |
| 1837 | Babbage: concept of the _Analytical Engine_ with arithmetic unit (_mill_), memory (_store_), punched card input and conditional branching |
| 1843 | **Ada Lovelace** publishes a program for calculating Bernoulli numbers on the Analytical Engine: regarded as the first computer program |
| 1847/54 | George Boole: algebra of logic (**Boolean algebra**) |
| 1890 | Herman Hollerith: punched card tabulating machines for the US census; his company later became part of **IBM** |

Babbage's machines were not completed in his lifetime because precision engineering was too imprecise and funding too scarce. The Science Museum in London built Difference Engine No. 2 from his plans by 1991 and proved that it works.

## The first computers

In 1936, **Alan Turing** described the **Turing machine**, a mathematical model that can compute any computable function, and thereby founded theoretical computer science. During the Second World War, he worked at **Bletchley Park** on breaking the German **Enigma**; the electronic computer **Colossus** was also built there in 1943/44. The war accelerated computer development because the military had to calculate ballistics, carry out cryptanalysis and later design nuclear weapons.

| Computer | Year | Significance |
| -------- | ---- | ------------ |
| **Zuse Z3** (Konrad Zuse, Berlin) | 12 May 1941 | first working, freely programmable, fully automatic computer; electromechanical with around 2,000 relays, binary floating-point numbers, program on punched tape |
| **Harvard Mark I** (Howard Aiken, IBM) | 1944 | electromechanical mainframe |
| **ENIAC** (Eckert and Mauchly, Philadelphia) | 1945, presented to the public in 1946 | first electronic general-purpose computer; around 17,500 vacuum tubes, 27 t, about 150 kW power consumption; programmed by re-plugging cables, among others by six women programmers |
| **Manchester Baby, EDSAC** | 1948/49 | first computers with a program stored in memory |

In 1945, **John von Neumann** described the architecture named after him, the **von Neumann architecture**: arithmetic unit, control unit, memory, input and output, with program and data in the same memory. Almost all of today's computers essentially follow this principle.

## Computer generations

| Generation | Time | Technology | Examples |
| ---------- | ---- | ---------- | -------- |
| 1 | c. 1945 to 1955 | vacuum tubes | ENIAC, UNIVAC I (first commercial computer in the USA, 1951) |
| 2 | c. 1955 to 1965 | **transistors** | IBM 1401, Mailüfterl; first high-level programming languages FORTRAN (1957), COBOL (1959) |
| 3 | c. 1965 to 1975 | **integrated circuits** (IC) | IBM System/360 (1964), DEC PDP series, UNIX operating system (1969) |
| 4 | from c. 1971 | **microprocessors** | Intel 4004 (1971), personal computers |

- The **transistor** was invented in 1947 at Bell Labs by John Bardeen, Walter Brattain and William Shockley (Nobel Prize 1956). It replaced the unreliable, hot vacuum tubes and made miniaturisation possible.
- Jack Kilby (Texas Instruments, 1958) and Robert Noyce (Fairchild, 1959) developed the **integrated circuit**.
- In 1965, Gordon Moore predicted that the number of transistors on a chip would double regularly (later refined to roughly every two years, **Moore's law**). The Intel 4004 had around 2,300 transistors; today's processors have tens of billions.
- Grace Hopper developed early compilers and shaped COBOL.

## Personal computers

In the 1970s, computers became small and cheap enough for private individuals.

- **1975**: the **Altair 8800** kit computer; Bill Gates and Paul Allen found **Microsoft** and supply a BASIC interpreter.
- **1977**: **Apple II** (Steve Jobs, Steve Wozniak), Commodore PET.
- **1981**: **IBM PC** with an Intel 8088 processor and the MS-DOS operating system. Because IBM chose an open architecture, a market for compatible clones emerged ("IBM-compatible"), which defined the PC standard.
- **1982**: Commodore 64, the best-selling home computer.
- **1984**: Apple **Macintosh** with graphical user interface and mouse (the ideas came from the Xerox PARC research centre).
- **1985/1990/1995**: Microsoft Windows 1.0, 3.0 and Windows 95.
- **1991**: the Finnish student Linus Torvalds publishes the Linux kernel. Together with the GNU project (Richard Stallman, 1983), a free operating system emerges; the **open-source movement** today shapes servers, smartphones (Android) and the cloud.

## The internet and the World Wide Web

| Year | Development |
| ---- | ----------- |
| 1969 | **ARPANET**: the US Department of Defense funds a packet-switched network; on 29 October the first message is transmitted between UCLA and Stanford |
| 1971 | first email between two computers (Ray Tomlinson, introduction of the @) |
| 1974 | Vinton Cerf and Robert Kahn describe the protocol from which **TCP/IP** emerges |
| 1 January 1983 | ARPANET switches to TCP/IP: the birth of the internet |
| 1983/84 | Domain Name System (DNS) |
| 1989/90 | **Tim Berners-Lee** designs the **World Wide Web** at **CERN** in Geneva with HTML, HTTP and URLs |
| 1990 | Austria is connected to the internet (University of Vienna) |
| 1993 | CERN releases the web for free use; graphical browser Mosaic |
| 1998 | founding of Google |
| 2004 to 2006 | "Web 2.0": Facebook (2004), YouTube (2005), Twitter (2006): users create content themselves |

The internet is a network of networks based on open standards. The web is just one service that runs on it, alongside email, file transfer or streaming.

## Smartphones and the mobile revolution

Mobile phones became mass-market products in the 1990s with the digital **GSM** standard (the first SMS was sent in 1992). On **9 January 2007**, Apple presented the **iPhone**, which combined telephone, internet, music player and camera with a multi-touch screen; the App Store and the first Android smartphone followed in 2008. Mobile broadband networks (3G, 4G/LTE from 2010, 5G from 2019) and cloud services made the smartphone the most important computer for most people. Consequences include constant availability, social media as a central source of information (see [Media and Political Participation](/en/history-and-politics/media-and-political-participation/)), mobile payments, platform services such as ride-hailing and delivery services, and new questions about data protection and addictive behaviour.

## Artificial intelligence

| Year | Development |
| ---- | ----------- |
| 1950 | Turing proposes a test of whether a machine can think (**Turing test**) |
| 1956 | **Dartmouth Conference**: John McCarthy coins the term _artificial intelligence_ |
| 1958 | Frank Rosenblatt: **perceptron**, an early artificial neural network |
| 1970s and late 1980s | "**AI winters**": disappointed expectations lead to cuts in research funding |
| 1980s | **expert systems** with hard-coded rules; backpropagation for training neural networks (1986) |
| 1997 | IBM **Deep Blue** defeats world chess champion Garry Kasparov |
| 2012 | breakthrough of **deep learning** in image recognition (AlexNet) thanks to large amounts of data and graphics processors |
| 2016 | **AlphaGo** (DeepMind) defeats Go master Lee Sedol |
| 2017 | **Transformer** architecture ("Attention Is All You Need") |
| 30 November 2022 | **ChatGPT** becomes publicly available; large language models spread rapidly |
| 2024 | **EU Artificial Intelligence Act** (_AI Act_) as the first comprehensive AI law, with risk-based rules |

AI raises social questions: changes to occupations, copyright in training data, bias and discrimination, energy consumption of data centres, deepfakes and disinformation, responsibility for wrong decisions and the concentration of power in a few corporations.

## Technology and society

The history of technology is more than a list of inventions. Some fundamental questions:

- **Technological determinism or social shaping?** Does technology determine society, or do people, markets and politics decide which technology prevails? Research today emphasises the interaction: the internet arose from military research but was shaped by universities, open standards and commercial interests.
- **Work**: every wave of automation triggered fears of unemployment. The **Luddites** in England destroyed weaving machines in 1811/12. In the long run, new occupations usually emerged, but the transitions were often hard for those affected.
- **Environment**: industrialisation was based on fossil fuels. The increase in CO₂ in the atmosphere since the beginning of industrialisation is the main cause of climate change. But technology is also part of the solution (renewable energy, efficiency).
- **Dual use**: many technologies can be used for civilian and military purposes (rockets, nuclear fission, drones, AI, cryptography).
- **Ethics and responsibility**: the atomic physicist Robert Oppenheimer, the debate about nuclear power (Zwentendorf, Chernobyl 1986, Fukushima 2011) and today genetic engineering and AI show that engineers bear responsibility for the consequences of their work. Technology assessment is a discipline in its own right, in Austria for example at the Institute of Technology Assessment of the Austrian Academy of Sciences.
- **Digital divide**: access to technology and digital skills are unequally distributed, between countries as well as within a society.
