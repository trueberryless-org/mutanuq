History is not simply "the past" but our **knowledge about** the past. This knowledge comes from historians questioning, testing and interpreting traces of the past. Because every interpretation depends on questions, perspectives and the sources available, history is always a **reconstruction**. If you know how this reconstruction is made, you can assess historical accounts and recognise when history is distorted for political purposes.

## Sources and accounts

A **source** is anything that comes from the period under investigation and provides information about it. An **account** is a text written later (school book, academic book, documentary) that explains the past on the basis of sources. Historians also speak of **primary sources** (from the time) and **secondary literature** (about the time).

| Type of source | Examples | Typical strengths and limitations |
| -------------- | -------- | --------------------------------- |
| **Written sources** | charters, laws, letters, diaries, newspapers, minutes | very informative, but often written by elites |
| **Visual sources** | paintings, photos, caricatures, posters, maps | vivid, but designed and often staged |
| **Material sources** | tools, coins, buildings, clothing, archaeological finds | show everyday life and technology, but must be interpreted |
| **Oral sources** | interviews with contemporary witnesses (_oral history_), songs, legends | experiences "from below", but altered by memory |
| **Audiovisual sources** | sound recordings, films, newsreels, videos | appear authentic, but are edited and framed |
| **Digital sources** | websites, social media posts, emails, databases | available in huge quantities, easy to alter and short-lived |

Historians also distinguish **tradition sources**, which were deliberately created for posterity (chronicle, monument, memoirs), and **remains**, which came about incidentally (invoice, tax list, rubbish pit). Remains are usually less deliberately coloured than tradition sources.

## Source criticism

No source speaks for itself. **Source criticism** examines how credible and informative a source is.

- **External source criticism**: Is the source genuine? When, where and by whom was it made? Is it complete or a later copy? A famous Austrian example is the _Privilegium maius_ (1358/59): Duke Rudolf IV had it forged to give Austria supposedly ancient privileges. The forgery was only proven scientifically in the 19th century.
- **Internal source criticism**: What does the source say? What was the author's intention, for whom did he write, what did he actually know? What does he leave out?

### Steps of a source analysis

1. **Describe**: type of source, author, time and place of origin, addressee, topic.
2. **Examine**: summarise the content in your own words, clarify key terms, analyse structure and language.
3. **Contextualise**: establish the historical context: what was happening at the time? What position did the author hold?
4. **Evaluate**: credibility, standpoint and interests of the author, value for your own question. What can the source answer, and what not?

:::tip[Remember]
A source is not "true" or "false"; it is always **written from a perspective**. Even a one-sided propaganda speech is a valuable source, namely about the intentions and methods of those who made it.
:::

## Periodisation

To make the past manageable, it is divided into **periods** (epochs). The boundaries are conventions of historical scholarship and are always disputed, because change usually happens slowly. For Europe, the following division is common:

| Period | Approximate time span | Usual markers |
| ------ | --------------------- | ------------- |
| **Prehistory and protohistory** | up to c. 3000 BC (varies by region) | the invention of writing ends prehistory |
| **Antiquity** | c. 3000 BC to AD 476 | early civilisations; deposition of the last Western Roman emperor in 476 |
| **Middle Ages** | c. 500 to 1500 | end of Western Rome; printing press, discovery of America 1492, Reformation 1517 |
| **Early modern period** | c. 1500 to 1789 | French Revolution 1789 |
| **Modern period** | from 1789 | revolutions and industrialisation |
| **Contemporary history** | usually from 1917/18 or from 1945 | the period of people still living; contemporary witnesses can still be interviewed |

This division is **Eurocentric**: the boundaries do not fit China, India or the Americas. Years before the birth of Christ count backwards (500 BC is before 400 BC); there is no year zero. More neutral notations are BCE (before the common era) and CE.

## Historical thinking

The Austrian curricula for history and political education are based on the competence model of the research group FUER Geschichtsbewusstsein. It distinguishes four areas of competence:

| Competence | What it enables you to do |
| ---------- | ------------------------- |
| **Historical questioning competence** | ask your own questions about the past and recognise the questions behind others' work |
| **Historical methodological competence** | analyse sources (re-construction) and take apart and test accounts (de-construction) |
| **Historical orientation competence** | gain insights from history for the present and future, reflect on your own world view |
| **Historical subject competence** | know and use concepts such as "revolution", "state" or "fascism" critically |

There is a similar model for political education with political judgement, action, methodological and subject competence (see [Democracy and Political Theory](/en/history-and-politics/democracy-and-political-theory/)).

Important basic concepts of historical thinking:

- **Multiperspectivity**: the same event is experienced differently by those involved (for example victors and defeated, men and women, rulers and ruled).
- **Controversy**: historians also interpret events differently. What is disputed should also appear disputed in the classroom.
- **Relevance to the present**: we question the past from today's perspective. This is legitimate, but must not lead to judging people of earlier times simply by today's standards without knowing their context (**presentism**).
- **Historical culture**: how a society deals with the past: monuments, commemoration days, museums, films, street names, computer games.
- **Culture of remembrance and politics of history**: states and groups use history to create identity or justify claims. One example is the Austrian "victim thesis" (see [The Nazi Era and the Second World War](/en/history-and-politics/nazi-era-and-second-world-war/)).

## Deconstructing accounts

School books, documentaries and Wikipedia articles are also constructions. When **deconstructing** them, ask:

- Who made the account, when and for whom?
- Which sources does it rely on, and are they disclosed?
- Which perspectives are present, and which are missing?
- How are things selected, weighted and judged (for example through choice of words, music, selection of images)?
- Does the account clearly distinguish between facts, interpretations and opinions?

## Analysing visual sources and caricatures

A **caricature** (political cartoon) is a drawing that criticises political or social conditions through exaggeration, distortion and symbols. It always takes a position.

1. **Describe**: What is shown (people, objects, text, caption)? Who is the artist, when and where was it published?
2. **Interpret**: Which **symbols** are used (scales for justice, dove for peace, bear for Russia, Michel in a nightcap for Germany, John Bull for Britain, Marianne for France)? What is exaggerated?
3. **Contextualise**: Which event does it refer to?
4. **Evaluate**: What position does the artist take? Is the criticism justified? How did it affect the audience of the time?

The same steps apply to **photos** and **posters**. In addition, ask about framing, perspective (low or high angle), staging and possible manipulation. In the Soviet Union, people who had fallen from favour were already retouched out of photos; today image editing and AI-generated images make large-scale forgery possible.

## Analysing statistics and charts

Numbers appear objective, but they too are selected and designed.

1. **Topic and source**: What is shown, and who collected the data (statistics office, party, interest group)?
2. **Time and place**: For which period and which area do the data apply?
3. **Type of values**: absolute numbers or percentages? Index values (e.g. 1913 = 100)? Adjusted for inflation?
4. **Presentation**: Does the y-axis start at zero? Are the intervals between years even? Has a section been chosen that exaggerates a trend?
5. **Statement**: main message, developments, highest and lowest values, comparison.
6. **Evaluation**: What does the statistic explain, and what not? A temporal connection (**correlation**) is not yet a cause-and-effect relationship (**causation**).

Example: after the Great Depression, the unemployment rate in Austria rose to around a quarter of employees in 1933. The number explains the hardship, but only the context (banking crisis, austerity, political radicalisation) makes clear why democracy collapsed (see [The Interwar Period](/en/history-and-politics/interwar-period/)).
