The twenty years between the world wars were a time of great hopes and severe crises. After 1918, democracies were founded in almost all of Europe; by 1938 most of them had been replaced by dictatorships. The examples of Austria and Germany allow a close study of why democracies can fail: economic hardship, a lack of basic consensus, violence on the streets, disregard of the constitution by those in power and the absence of democrats willing to defend it. These questions are at the heart of political education.

## The First Republic

### Founding and constitution

The young republic faced enormous problems: hunger, coal shortages, hundreds of thousands of returning soldiers and unemployed, border fighting and doubts about its own viability. The government of **Social Democrats** and **Christian Socials** (coalition until 1920) passed important **social laws**, mainly on the initiative of the Social Democrat Ferdinand Hanusch: the **eight-hour day** (1918/19), unemployment insurance (1920), holiday entitlement, the **Works Councils Act** (1919) and the founding of the **Chambers of Labour** (1920). The **nobility** was abolished (Nobility Abolition Act 1919), and the House of Habsburg was expelled from the country (Habsburg Law).

On **1 October 1920**, the Constituent National Assembly adopted the **Federal Constitutional Law** (_Bundes-Verfassungsgesetz_, B-VG). With many amendments, it is still in force today. Its most important co-author was the constitutional lawyer **Hans Kelsen**.

- Austria became a **democratic, federal republic** with nine federal provinces (Vienna became a separate province in 1922).
- Parliament (National Council and Federal Council) was at the centre; the Federal President was initially elected by the Federal Assembly and had little power.
- A new feature was the **Constitutional Court**, which can review laws for their constitutionality and repeal them. Kelsen's model of a separate, centralised constitutional jurisdiction became a model worldwide.
- The B-VG contained no catalogue of fundamental rights because the parties could not agree. Instead, the Basic Law of 1867 was adopted.

The **constitutional amendment of 1929**, under pressure from the Heimwehr, strengthened the **Federal President**: direct election by the people (first held in 1951), appointment and dismissal of the federal government, dissolution of the National Council, emergency decree powers. This structure essentially still applies today (see [The Austrian Political System](/en/history-and-politics/austrian-political-system/)).

### Economy

By 1922, inflation had destroyed the savings of the middle classes. Chancellor **Ignaz Seipel** (Christian Social) obtained a League of Nations loan of 650 million gold crowns with the **Geneva Protocols** of 1922. In return, Austria had to consolidate its budget (dismissal of tens of thousands of civil servants), accept supervision by a League of Nations commissioner and again renounce union with Germany. In 1925 the **schilling** was introduced (10,000 crowns = 1 schilling). Unemployment, however, remained high even in the "good" years.

### The political camps

Society was divided into **camps** (_Lager_), which formed their own milieus with associations, newspapers, sports and youth organisations. People often lived in one camp "from the cradle to the grave".

| Camp | Party | Base and goals | Paramilitary organisation |
| ---- | ----- | -------------- | ------------------------- |
| **Socialist** | Social Democratic Workers' Party (SDAP); Otto Bauer, Karl Renner | workers, Vienna; goal of socialism by democratic means (Linz Programme 1926, with the controversial phrase "dictatorship of the proletariat" as a means of defence) | **Republican Protection League** (_Schutzbund_, 1923) |
| **Christian Social** | Christian Social Party (CS); Ignaz Seipel, Engelbert Dollfuss | peasants, lower middle class, civil servants, Catholic Church; the provinces | **Heimwehr** (home guards, from 1918/20) |
| **German National** | Greater German People's Party, Landbund; from the 1930s increasingly the NSDAP | civil servants, academics, some peasants; goal of union with Germany | later SA and SS |

There was also a small Communist Party (KPÖ, founded 1918). From 1920 bourgeois coalitions governed; the Social Democrats were in opposition at federal level but governed Vienna.

In **Red Vienna**, the Social Democrats implemented an extensive reform programme: with the earmarked **housing tax** (Hugo Breitner), more than 60,000 council flats were built by 1934 (e.g. the Karl-Marx-Hof), together with baths, kindergartens, health care (Julius Tandler) and school reforms (Otto Glöckel).

### Escalation of violence

The two camps distrusted each other deeply. The bourgeois parties feared a socialist revolution, the Social Democrats a counter-revolution. Armed paramilitary groups marched against each other.

- On **30 January 1927** in **Schattendorf** (Burgenland), members of a right-wing veterans' association fired on Schutzbund members and killed a war invalid and a child.
- On **14 July 1927**, a jury acquitted the perpetrators. On **15 July**, a crowd of outraged workers marched to the **Palace of Justice** and set it on fire. The police fired into the crowd; there were 89 dead, including some police officers. After this, the gap between the camps could hardly be bridged.
- In the **Korneuburg Oath** (1930), the Heimwehr openly professed fascism and rejected "Western democratic parliamentarism".

## The Great Depression

After a speculative bubble, prices collapsed on the **New York Stock Exchange** in October 1929 (**"Black Thursday"**, 24 October, and "Black Tuesday", 29 October). American banks withdrew short-term loans from Europe, industrial production collapsed worldwide and world trade shrank by around two thirds.

The crisis was worsened by political reactions: states raised tariffs (protectionism) and cut spending during the crisis (**deflationary policy**), which reduced demand further. Only later did the ideas of John Maynard Keynes prevail, namely that the state should spend more in a crisis (US President Roosevelt's **New Deal** from 1933).

In Austria, the **Creditanstalt**, the largest bank in Central Europe, collapsed in May 1931 and had to be rescued with state money. Unemployment rose to around a quarter of the workforce (1933); many long-term unemployed no longer received benefits (_Ausgesteuerte_). The sociological study **"Marienthal: The Sociography of an Unemployed Community"** (1933, Marie Jahoda, Paul Lazarsfeld, Hans Zeisel) showed that long-term unemployment does not lead to revolt but to resignation and apathy. Hardship drove many voters to radical parties, in Austria and even more so in Germany to the NSDAP.

## From the end of democracy to the corporate state

### Elimination of parliament

**Engelbert Dollfuss** (Christian Social) became chancellor in 1932 of a coalition with a majority of only one vote. On **4 March 1933**, during a vote on a railway workers' strike, all three presidents of the National Council resigned in order to be able to vote. The rules of procedure provided no procedure for this case. Dollfuss used the situation, spoke of a **"self-elimination" of parliament** and on 15 March used the police to prevent it from reconvening. From then on he governed by emergency decrees based on the **War Economy Enabling Act** of 1917.

Step by step he built up a dictatorship:

- elimination of the **Constitutional Court** (May 1933)
- bans on the **Republican Protection League** (March 1933), the **KPÖ** (May 1933) and the **NSDAP** (June 1933, after terrorist attacks)
- founding of the **Fatherland Front** as a single organisation (May 1933)
- reintroduction of the death penalty under martial law (November 1933), establishment of **detention camps** (Wöllersdorf) for political opponents
- reliance on fascist Italy under Mussolini as a protecting power against Hitler's Germany

### The civil war of 1934

On **12 February 1934**, Schutzbund members in **Linz** (Hotel Schiff) offered armed resistance to a police search for weapons. The fighting spread to Vienna (council housing estates such as the Karl-Marx-Hof), Steyr, Upper Styria and other industrial towns. The army, police and Heimwehr used artillery against residential complexes. By 15 February the uprising had been crushed. There were more than 300 dead on both sides; nine Schutzbund members were executed under martial law, including the severely wounded Karl Münichreiter. The **Social Democratic Party** and the trade unions were **banned**, and their leaders arrested or driven into exile (Otto Bauer).

### The "corporate state" (Austrofascism)

On **1 May 1934** the **May Constitution** came into force. It invoked God ("In the name of God, the Almighty, from whom all law emanates") and defined Austria as a "Christian, German federal state on a corporative basis". Instead of parties and elected parliaments, occupational estates were supposed to order society (following the encyclical _Quadragesimo anno_, 1931); in reality, power lay with the government. The Concordat with the Vatican (1933, in force 1934) gave the Catholic Church great influence.

The name of the regime is disputed in research and politics: common terms are **"Austrofascism"**, **"corporate state"** (_Ständestaat_) and **"chancellor dictatorship"**. There is broad consensus that it was a **dictatorship**: no free elections, no opposition, censorship, political prisoners. Unlike National Socialism, it lacked a mass movement, a racist programme and an expansionist foreign policy; the state saw itself as an opponent of Hitler and as the "better" German Austria.

On **25 July 1934**, Austrian National Socialists attempted a coup (**July Putsch**). They occupied the Federal Chancellery, where Dollfuss was shot. The putsch failed, partly because Mussolini deployed troops at the Brenner Pass. His successor was **Kurt Schuschnigg**. When Mussolini moved closer to Hitler after the Abyssinian War of 1935/36, Austria lost its protecting power. In the **July Agreement** of 1936, Hitler recognised Austria's independence; in return, National Socialists were amnestied and taken into the government. How this development ended in the "Anschluss" of 1938 is described in [The Nazi Era and the Second World War](/en/history-and-politics/nazi-era-and-second-world-war/).

## The Russian Revolution and the Soviet Union

The First World War deepened the crisis of the backward Tsarist Empire. In the **February Revolution** of 1917 (March in the Western calendar), Tsar Nicholas II abdicated; a bourgeois Provisional Government continued the war. In parallel, workers' and soldiers' councils (**soviets**) emerged. With German help, **Lenin** returned from exile in Switzerland and demanded "Peace, land, bread" and "All power to the soviets". In the **October Revolution** (25 October in the Julian calendar, 7 November 1917), the **Bolsheviks** seized power in an armed coup. They dissolved the freely elected Constituent Assembly, in which they were in the minority, in January 1918.

- In the **civil war** (1918 to 1921/22), the **Red Army** (Leon Trotsky) prevailed against the "Whites" and foreign intervention forces. The secret police, the Cheka, persecuted opponents ("Red Terror"). The tsar's family was murdered in 1918.
- After famines and uprisings, Lenin allowed limited private trade again with the **New Economic Policy** (NEP, 1921).
- On **30 December 1922**, the **Union of Soviet Socialist Republics** (USSR) was founded, a one-party state of the Communist Party.
- After Lenin's death (1924), **Joseph Stalin** prevailed. From 1928 he had heavy industry built up with **five-year plans** and agriculture forcibly **collectivised**. Millions of peasants were expropriated and deported as "kulaks"; in the famine of 1932/33, millions of people died, especially in Ukraine (**Holodomor**, recognised as genocide by Ukraine and many states) and in Kazakhstan.
- In the **Great Terror** of 1936 to 1938, Stalin had real and alleged opponents persecuted in show trials and mass arrests; around 700,000 people were shot and millions deported to camps of the **Gulag**.

## Fascism in Italy

Italy was among the victors but felt cheated of territorial gains ("mutilated victory"). Strikes, factory occupations and the bourgeoisie's fear of communism were exploited by **Benito Mussolini**, a former socialist who founded the fascist movement in 1919 (named after the _fasces_, the bundle of rods carried by Roman officials' attendants). After the **March on Rome** in October 1922, King Victor Emmanuel III appointed him prime minister. By 1926 he had established a dictatorship.

Features of **fascism**:

- leader cult (_Duce_), one-party state, rejection of democracy, liberalism and Marxism
- extreme nationalism, glorification of violence and war, expansionist policy (Abyssinian War 1935/36)
- mass organisations, propaganda, uniforms and rituals
- corporatism: employers and employees in state-controlled corporations instead of trade unions
- squadristi as paramilitary thugs (Blackshirts)

South Tyrol was systematically **Italianised**: German was banned in schools and offices, place names and surnames were Italianised and workers from the south were settled there.

## The Weimar Republic

After the November Revolution of 1918, Philipp Scheidemann (SPD) proclaimed the republic on 9 November. The National Assembly, meeting in Weimar, adopted a democratic constitution on **11 August 1919** with women's suffrage, fundamental rights and pure proportional representation. The directly elected **Reich President** had a very strong position thanks to the **emergency decree Article 48** and the right to dissolve the Reichstag.

| Phase | Features |
| ----- | -------- |
| **Crisis years** 1919 to 1923 | uprisings from the left (Spartacists) and right (Kapp Putsch 1920), political murders (Erzberger, Rathenau), occupation of the Ruhr, **hyperinflation** 1923 (in November one US dollar cost 4.2 trillion marks), Hitler putsch of 8/9 November 1923 in Munich |
| **"Golden Twenties"** 1924 to 1929 | currency reform (Rentenmark), Dawes Plan, American loans, foreign minister Gustav Stresemann: Locarno Treaties 1925, joining the League of Nations 1926; cultural flowering in Berlin |
| **Dissolution** 1930 to 1933 | Great Depression, around 6 million unemployed (1932), **presidential cabinets** without a parliamentary majority (Brüning, Papen, Schleicher) governing by emergency decree; rise of the NSDAP and KPD |

The **NSDAP** under **Adolf Hitler** rose from 2.6 % (1928) to 18.3 % (1930) and **37.3 %** in July 1932, becoming the largest party. On **30 January 1933**, Reich President Paul von Hindenburg appointed Hitler chancellor of a coalition government. Conservative politicians around Franz von Papen believed they could "frame" and control him.

Reasons for the failure of the Weimar Republic:

- **Burdens from the outset**: the Treaty of Versailles, the "stab-in-the-back myth" (the lie that the army had been "undefeated in the field" and betrayed by revolutionaries at home), reparations
- **Economic crises**: inflation in 1923 and the Great Depression
- **A republic without republicans**: elites in the military, judiciary, administration and universities were often hostile to democracy.
- **Structural weaknesses of the constitution**: Article 48, the right of dissolution and the chancellor's dependence on the president made it possible to govern around parliament.
- **Radicalisation and violence**: street fighting between the SA, the Red Front Fighters' League and the Reichsbanner
- **Lack of willingness to compromise** among the democratic parties, and attacks on the republic by the NSDAP and KPD
