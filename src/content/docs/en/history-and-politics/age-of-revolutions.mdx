Between 1770 and 1871, the foundations of modern politics were laid in Europe and America: human rights, constitutions, separation of powers, popular sovereignty and the nation state. These ideas did not prevail in a straight line. Revolutions were followed by restoration and repression, and nationalism, which was initially linked with freedom, later became a cause of conflict. This section is fundamental for political education, because the concepts we still use to talk about politics emerged here.

## The Enlightenment

The **Enlightenment** of the 17th and 18th centuries put **reason** in place of tradition and church authority. In 1784, Immanuel Kant defined it as "man's emergence from his self-incurred immaturity" (see also the literary period [Enlightenment](/en/languages/literature/enlightenment/)). The following thinkers were central for politics:

| Thinker | Main work | Key idea |
| ------- | --------- | -------- |
| **Thomas Hobbes** | Leviathan (1651) | In the state of nature there is a war of all against all; people transfer their power to a strong ruler in a **social contract**, who guarantees security. |
| **John Locke** | Two Treatises of Government (1689) | Every human being has natural rights to **life, liberty and property**; a government that violates them may be removed. |
| **Montesquieu** | The Spirit of the Laws (1748) | **Separation of powers** into legislature, executive and judiciary, so that "power checks power". |
| **Jean-Jacques Rousseau** | The Social Contract (1762) | **Popular sovereignty**: power comes from the people; laws should correspond to the general will (_volonté générale_). |
| **Voltaire** | letters, satires, Treatise on Tolerance (1763) | fight against religious intolerance and arbitrary justice, freedom of opinion |

The ideas spread through newspapers, salons, coffee houses, reading societies and the **Encyclopédie** (Diderot and d'Alembert, from 1751). Some rulers such as Frederick II of Prussia and Joseph II adopted them in **enlightened absolutism**, but without sharing their power (see [The Early Modern Period](/en/history-and-politics/early-modern-period/)).

## The American Revolution

After 1763, the thirteen British colonies on the east coast of North America protested against new taxes that the London Parliament imposed without their participation ("**No taxation without representation**", Boston Tea Party 1773). This led to the War of Independence (1775 to 1783).

- The **Declaration of Independence** of 4 July 1776 (main author Thomas Jefferson) stated, following Locke, that all men are created equal and have unalienable rights to "life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness".
- With French support, the Americans won; in the Peace of Paris of 1783, Britain recognised their independence.
- The **Constitution** of 1787 (in force from 1789) is the oldest written national constitution still in force. It created a **presidential democracy** with separation of powers and mutual control (_checks and balances_) and a **federal state**. The **Bill of Rights** (1791) added fundamental rights.
- George Washington became the first president.

Limits: the rights did not apply to slaves, the indigenous population or women. Slavery was only abolished in 1865 after the Civil War.

## The French Revolution

### Causes

- **Society of estates**: clergy (First Estate) and nobility (Second Estate), together only a few per cent of the population, had privileges and paid hardly any taxes. The **Third Estate** (bourgeoisie, peasants, urban lower classes) bore the burden.
- **State bankruptcy**: wars (including support for the Americans) and court expenditure had left France heavily in debt.
- **Poor harvests** in 1788 made the price of bread rise sharply.
- The self-confident bourgeoisie was shaped by the ideas of the Enlightenment.

### Course

| Date | Event |
| ---- | ----- |
| 5 May 1789 | Louis XVI convenes the **Estates-General** to approve new taxes |
| 17 June 1789 | the representatives of the Third Estate declare themselves the **National Assembly** |
| 20 June 1789 | **Tennis Court Oath**: not to separate until France has a constitution |
| 14 July 1789 | **storming of the Bastille** in Paris (today the national holiday) |
| 4 August 1789 | abolition of feudal rights and privileges |
| 26 August 1789 | **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen** |
| 1791 | constitution: constitutional monarchy with property-based suffrage |
| 1792 | war against Austria and Prussia; fall of the monarchy, proclamation of the **republic** |
| 21 January 1793 | execution of Louis XVI; Marie Antoinette, a daughter of Maria Theresa, follows in October |
| 1793/94 | **Reign of Terror** of the Jacobins under Robespierre (Committee of Public Safety); tens of thousands of executions |
| July 1794 | fall and execution of Robespierre |
| 1795 to 1799 | rule of the **Directory** |
| 9 November 1799 | Napoleon's coup (18 Brumaire) |

The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen of 1789 begins with the sentence: "Men are born and remain free and equal in rights." It guarantees liberty, property, security and resistance to oppression, equality before the law, freedom of opinion and religion, separation of powers and popular sovereignty. In 1791, **Olympe de Gouges** demanded the same rights for women in a "Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen"; she was executed in 1793.

The Revolution shaped political terms to this day: the labels **"left"** and **"right"** come from the seating arrangement in the National Assembly (supporters of change to the left, defenders of the existing order to the right of the president). The motto **"Liberty, equality, fraternity"** became a guiding principle of democracy.

## Napoleon

**Napoleon Bonaparte**, a successful general of the revolutionary wars, took power in 1799 as First Consul and crowned himself emperor in 1804. He secured some results of the Revolution but ruled as a dictator.

- **Civil Code** (_Code civil_, 1804): uniform civil law with equality before the law, protection of property, civil marriage and freedom of trade. It was adopted in many European countries.
- Centralised administration (prefects), concordat with the Church, founding of the Banque de France.

### Napoleon and Austria

- In 1805 Napoleon defeated Austria and Russia at **Austerlitz** (Battle of the Three Emperors) and occupied Vienna.
- In 1806, 16 German states joined Napoleon in the **Confederation of the Rhine**. Francis II, who had already taken the title **Emperor of Austria** (as Francis I) in **1804**, laid down the crown of the Holy Roman Empire on 6 August **1806**. The empire ceased to exist. In the secularisations of 1803, Salzburg, until then a spiritual principality, became secular; it finally came to Austria in 1816.
- In 1809, Archduke Charles defeated Napoleon at **Aspern** (Napoleon's first defeat in a major field battle); shortly afterwards Napoleon won at **Wagram**. In the **Tyrolean struggle for freedom**, Andreas Hofer and his riflemen fought against Bavarians and French; Hofer was shot in Mantua in 1810.
- In 1810, foreign minister Metternich arranged Napoleon's marriage to the emperor's daughter **Marie Louise**.

The **Russian campaign** of 1812 ended in disaster: of more than 600,000 soldiers, only a small fraction returned. In the **Battle of the Nations at Leipzig** in October 1813, Russia, Prussia, Austria and Sweden defeated Napoleon. He was exiled to Elba, returned in 1815 ("Hundred Days") and was finally beaten at **Waterloo** on 18 June 1815 and exiled to St Helena.

The paradox of Napoleonic rule: it spread the ideas of the Revolution but in the occupied countries (Germany, Spain, Tyrol) awakened a **nationalism** directed against France.

## The Congress of Vienna and the Metternich system

From September 1814 to June 1815, the monarchs and diplomats of Europe reorganised the continent in Vienna. The host and leading figure was the Austrian foreign minister **Klemens Wenzel Lothar von Metternich**. Besides the negotiations, the Congress was known for its balls and festivities ("The Congress dances").

Principles:

- **Restoration**: re-establishment of the pre-revolutionary order
- **Legitimacy**: return of the "rightful" dynasties (such as the Bourbons in France)
- **Balance** of the five great powers (pentarchy: Britain, Russia, Austria, Prussia, France)
- **Solidarity** of the monarchs against revolutions (**Holy Alliance** of Russia, Austria and Prussia, 1815)

Results: instead of the old empire, the **German Confederation** was created, a loose confederation of 39 (later fewer) states under Austrian presidency with the Federal Diet in Frankfurt. Austria received Lombardy-Venetia and regained Salzburg and Tyrol, but gave up the Austrian Netherlands. Switzerland was recognised as neutral. The Congress order prevented major wars between the great powers for around 40 years.

In the **Metternich system**, liberal and national movements were suppressed. The **Carlsbad Decrees** of 1819 introduced censorship, surveillance of universities and bans on student fraternities. In Austria, a network of informers monitored the population. The middle classes withdrew into private life (**Biedermeier**), while politically engaged authors shaped the **Vormärz** (see [Biedermeier and Vormärz](/en/languages/literature/biedermeier_and_vormaerz/)).

## The revolutions of 1848

In February 1848 the king was overthrown in Paris; the revolution quickly spread across Europe. Causes were political repression, demands for constitutions and nation states, an economic crisis with poor harvests and the hardship of the lower classes during early industrialisation.

### In Austria

| Date | Event |
| ---- | ----- |
| 13 March 1848 | uprising in Vienna (students, citizens, workers); **Metternich resigns** and flees to England |
| April 1848 | Pillersdorf Constitution, rejected by the revolutionaries as inadequate |
| July 1848 | the first elected **Reichstag** meets in Vienna |
| 7 September 1848 | **emancipation of the peasants**: abolition of manorialism and the robot (motion by deputy Hans Kudlich) |
| October 1848 | October Revolution in Vienna, crushed at the end of October by imperial troops under Windisch-Graetz |
| 2 December 1848 | abdication of Ferdinand I, accession of the 18-year-old **Francis Joseph I** |
| March 1849 | dissolution of the Reichstag, which had moved to Kremsier, imposed March Constitution |
| 1849 | suppression of the Hungarian revolution with Russian help |
| 1851 | New Year's Eve Patent: return to absolutism (**neo-absolutism**) |

In Italy, Field Marshal **Radetzky** crushed the uprisings in northern Italy (Radetzky March by Johann Strauss the Elder). In Prague the Slav Congress met.

### In Germany

On 18 May 1848, the first elected all-German **National Assembly** met in **St Paul's Church** in Frankfurt. It adopted fundamental rights and in 1849 a constitution. The question of borders was disputed:

- **Greater German solution**: with Austria (but only its German-speaking areas, which would have split the Habsburg Monarchy)
- **Lesser German solution**: without Austria, under Prussian leadership

The assembly chose the lesser German solution and offered the imperial crown to the Prussian king Frederick William IV. He rejected a crown "from the gutter" of the revolution. The revolution failed.

### Assessment

The revolution of 1848 failed because of disunity among the revolutionaries (liberals against democrats, bourgeoisie against workers, national antagonisms) and the military power of the monarchs. Lasting results in Austria were the **emancipation of the peasants**, the experience of parliamentarism and the debate on fundamental rights, which the constitution of 1867 drew on.

## Nationalism

A **nation** is a large community of people who feel connected by a common language, history, culture or political will. There are two ideas of the nation:

- **Civic nation** (subjective, "nation of will"): anyone who identifies with the state and its constitution belongs to the nation (France, USA, Switzerland). In 1882 Ernest Renan called the nation a "daily plebiscite".
- **Cultural nation** (objective): the nation is defined by language and culture and exists even without its own state (Germans and Italians before 1871).

The historian Benedict Anderson described nations as "imagined communities", because their members never all know each other but still feel connected.

In the early 19th century, nationalism was mostly linked with **liberalism** and popular sovereignty: the nation should govern itself instead of serving princes. In the late 19th century it became increasingly **aggressive and exclusionary** (superiority of one's own nation, antisemitism, racial theories, imperialism). For the multi-ethnic Habsburg state it was an existential question (see [Industrialisation](/en/history-and-politics/industrialisation/)).

## The unification of Italy and Germany

### Italy

The driving force was the Kingdom of **Sardinia-Piedmont** under Prime Minister **Camillo Cavour**, supported by France.

- 1859: Austrian defeat at **Magenta** and **Solferino**; Austria loses Lombardy. The suffering of the wounded at Solferino led Henry Dunant to found the **Red Cross** (1863).
- 1860: **Giuseppe Garibaldi** and his volunteers ("Expedition of the Thousand") conquer Sicily and Naples.
- 1861: proclamation of the **Kingdom of Italy** under Victor Emmanuel II.
- 1866: Italy gains Venetia after the war of Prussia and Italy against Austria.
- 1870: capture of Rome, which becomes the capital.

### Germany

Prussian Prime Minister **Otto von Bismarck** achieved the lesser German unification "from above", by "iron and blood", as he said in 1862:

1. **Second Schleswig War** 1864: Prussia and Austria together against Denmark over Schleswig and Holstein.
2. **Austro-Prussian War** 1866: a dispute over the administration of the duchies led to war between Prussia and Austria. Prussia won at **Königgrätz** on 3 July 1866. The German Confederation was dissolved and Austria withdrew from German politics. Prussia founded the North German Confederation in 1867.
3. **Franco-Prussian War** 1870/71: the southern German states fought alongside Prussia. After the victory, the Prussian king William I was proclaimed German Emperor in the Hall of Mirrors at **Versailles** on **18 January 1871**. France had to cede Alsace-Lorraine, which deepened Franco-German hostility.

The German Empire was a **constitutional monarchy** with a Reichstag elected by universal male suffrage, but with a chancellor responsible only to the emperor. For Austria, 1866 meant a reorientation towards the south-east and the compromise with Hungary in 1867.
