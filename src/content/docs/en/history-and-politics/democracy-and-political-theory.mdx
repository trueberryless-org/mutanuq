**Politics** is the sum of all actions by which generally binding decisions are made and enforced in a society: laws, taxes, the budget, education, security. Because people have different interests and values, politics always involves conflict. Democracy is a procedure for resolving these conflicts without violence, according to rules and with respect for everyone's rights. Political education is meant to enable you to form your own reasoned judgement and to take part.

## Competences of political education

The Austrian competence model for political education (Krammer et al., 2008) describes four competences:

| Competence | What it enables you to do |
| ---------- | ------------------------- |
| **Political judgement competence** | assess political decisions, problems and controversies independently and with reasons |
| **Political action competence** | formulate and represent your own positions, take part in decisions, find compromises |
| **Political methodological competence** | analyse political information (statistics, media reports, speeches) and communicate politically yourself |
| **Political subject competence** | understand and use critically concepts such as power, state, justice or democracy |

Teaching follows the **Beutelsbach Consensus** (1976): teachers must not overwhelm pupils (ban on indoctrination), what is controversial in academia and politics must also appear controversial in the classroom (requirement of controversy), and learners should be enabled to recognise and represent their own interests.

## Basic concepts

Political science distinguishes three dimensions of politics:

| Dimension | Term | Question | Example |
| --------- | ---- | -------- | ------- |
| **Form** | _polity_ | Which rules and institutions exist? | constitution, parliament, electoral law |
| **Content** | _policy_ | Which problems are solved, and how? | climate policy, education policy, tax reform |
| **Process** | _politics_ | How do actors push through their interests? | election campaign, coalition negotiations, lobbying |

Other basic concepts:

- **Power**: according to Max Weber, any chance within a social relationship to assert one's own will even against resistance.
- **Authority** (_Herrschaft_): institutionalised power in which commands are obeyed. Weber distinguished three forms of **legitimacy**: traditional (hereditary monarchy), charismatic (leader personality) and legal-rational authority (by law and procedure, typical of the modern state).
- **State**: according to Georg Jellinek's three-element theory, a **state territory**, a **state people** and **state authority**. The state claims the **monopoly on the use of force**: only it may legitimately use coercion.
- **Sovereignty**: independence of a state externally and supreme decision-making power internally. In a democracy it lies with the people (**popular sovereignty**).

## Forms of state and systems of government

Aristotle's classical classification (see [Antiquity](/en/history-and-politics/antiquity/)) asks how many rule and whether they do so for the good of all or for their own benefit. Today the main distinctions are:

| Criterion | Forms |
| --------- | ----- |
| Head of state | **monarchy** (hereditary, e.g. the United Kingdom, Sweden, Spain as parliamentary monarchies; Saudi Arabia as an absolute monarchy) or **republic** (elected, e.g. Austria, Germany, France) |
| Form of rule | **democracy** or **autocracy** (authoritarian and totalitarian dictatorships) |
| Structure of the state | **unitary state** (France), **federal state** (Austria, Germany, Switzerland, USA), **confederation** (formerly the German Confederation; the EU is regarded as a union of its own kind) |

### Authoritarian and totalitarian systems

An **authoritarian** system restricts opposition, press freedom and elections but leaves areas of private life untouched. A **totalitarian** system (Nazi Germany, the Soviet Union under Stalin) strives for complete control over state, society and the individual. Features according to Carl J. Friedrich and Zbigniew Brzeziński: a binding ideology, a mass party with a leader, terror by secret police, a monopoly over media and weapons, central control of the economy. Widespread today are **hybrid regimes** or "electoral autocracies", in which elections are held but the media, judiciary and opposition are controlled so that there is no fair competition.

### Parliamentary and presidential systems of government

| | Parliamentary system | Presidential system |
| - | -------------------- | ------------------- |
| Head of government | elected by parliament or dependent on its confidence | the president is both head of state and head of government, directly elected (or via electors) |
| Removal | parliament can bring down the government with a **vote of no confidence** | only for violations of law through impeachment |
| Relationship of powers | government and parliamentary majority form a political unit; the opposition scrutinises | strict separation, mutual control (_checks and balances_) |
| Examples | United Kingdom, Germany, Austria (with elements of a semi-presidential system) | USA, Brazil |

France has a **semi-presidential** system: the directly elected president has great power, and there is also a prime minister dependent on parliament. Austria's Federal President also has extensive powers under the constitution but traditionally exercises them with restraint (see [The Austrian Political System](/en/history-and-politics/austrian-political-system/)).

## Democracy

**Democracy** (Greek "rule of the people") is based on popular sovereignty. In 1863, Abraham Lincoln described it as "government of the people, by the people, for the people". Features of a modern liberal democracy:

- **free, equal, secret, universal and direct elections** at regular intervals
- a **multi-party system** and the possibility of a peaceful change of power
- **majority rule** with **protection of minorities**: the majority decides, but must not violate the fundamental rights of the minority and must leave it the chance to become the majority itself
- **fundamental and human rights**, especially freedom of opinion, the press, assembly and association
- **separation of powers** and the **rule of law**
- **pluralism**: a diversity of opinions, interests and organisations is legitimate
- **publicity** and free media as a check on power

The philosopher **Karl Popper**, born in Vienna, stressed that the decisive feature of democracy is that a government can be **voted out without bloodshed**.

### Direct and representative democracy

| | Direct democracy | Representative democracy |
| - | ---------------- | ------------------------ |
| Who decides? | the people themselves in votes | elected representatives (members of parliament) |
| Examples | referendums in Switzerland (several times a year at federal level), open-air assemblies (_Landsgemeinden_) in two cantons, the Athenian Assembly | Austria, Germany, most states |
| Advantages | high legitimacy, closeness to citizens, participation | expertise, time for deliberation, compromise and overall balancing, protection against momentary moods |
| Disadvantages | complex questions are reduced to yes or no, susceptibility to campaigns and populism, protection of minorities more difficult, low turnout | remoteness, influence of lobbies, disenchantment with politics |

Most democracies are representative with **direct-democratic elements** (in Austria the citizens' initiative petition, referendum and consultative referendum). In addition, **deliberative** forms are becoming more important, such as randomly selected **citizens' assemblies** like the Austrian Climate Council (2022).

### Theories of democracy

- **Identity theory** (Rousseau): there is a general will; rulers and ruled should be identical. Criticism: this can lead to the suppression of dissenting opinions.
- **Competitive theory** (Joseph Schumpeter, also an Austrian): democracy is a competition between elites for voters' votes.
- **Pluralist theory** (Ernst Fraenkel): the common good only results afterwards from the fair balancing of many interests.

## Separation of powers

The **separation of powers** according to John Locke and Montesquieu (see [The Age of Revolutions](/en/history-and-politics/age-of-revolutions/)) is intended to prevent abuse of power by distributing state authority among different bodies that check each other.

| Type | Powers |
| ---- | ------ |
| **Horizontal** | **legislature** (law-making: parliament), **executive** (implementation: government and administration), **judiciary** (jurisdiction: independent courts) |
| **Vertical** | distribution between levels: EU, federal state, provinces, municipalities (**federalism**, **subsidiarity**) |
| **Temporal** | offices for limited terms, regular elections, term limits |
| **Societal** | control by the media ("fourth estate"), interest groups, civil society and the opposition |

In parliamentary systems one speaks rather of an **interlocking of powers**, because the government emerges from the parliamentary majority. The actual scrutiny of the government is then carried out mainly by the **opposition** (questions, committees of inquiry, Court of Audit) and the independent judiciary.

## The rule of law

Under the **rule of law** (_Rechtsstaat_), all state power is bound by law. The opposite is arbitrariness.

- **Principle of legality**: the administration may act only on the basis of laws (Art. 18 B-VG).
- **Primacy of the constitution**: laws must comply with the constitution; a constitutional court can repeal them.
- **Fundamental rights** are enforceable in court.
- **Independent courts**: judges are not subject to instructions, cannot be removed and cannot be transferred against their will.
- **Legal protection**: decisions of authorities can be appealed.
- **Legal certainty**: laws are public (promulgation), clear and in principle not retroactive; no punishment without law (_nulla poena sine lege_).
- **Proportionality** of interference with fundamental rights.

The **hierarchy of norms** and the sources of law are described in [Basic Structures of Austrian Law](/en/economy_and_law/law/legal_system/).

## Political ideologies and the party spectrum

An **ideology** is a closed system of ideas and values that explains society and sets goals. Today's party families are rooted in the ideologies of the 19th century (see [Industrialisation](/en/history-and-politics/industrialisation/)):

| Party family | Core values | Typical positions |
| ------------ | ----------- | ----------------- |
| **Social democracy** | social justice, solidarity | welfare state, workers' rights, redistribution through taxes, public services |
| **Christian democracy and conservatism** | tradition, family, subsidiarity, personal responsibility | social market economy, order and security, Christian values |
| **Liberalism** | individual freedom | civil rights, market economy, lean state, education; usually open on social issues |
| **Greens** | ecology, sustainability, grassroots democracy | climate protection, environmental protection, equality, human rights |
| **Right-wing populism and nationalism** | nation, identity, "the people" against "the elites" | restricting migration, EU scepticism, law and order |
| **Left and communism** | equality | socialisation, criticism of capitalism, redistribution |

The **left-right scheme** comes from the French Revolution. It roughly classifies parties according to whether they emphasise more equality and change (left) or more tradition, order and difference (right). Because it mixes economic and social policy, political science often uses **two axes**: an **economic** one (state or market) and a **social** one (liberal-cosmopolitan or authoritarian-traditional).

At the margins are **extremist** positions that reject the free democratic order and in some cases advocate violence (right-wing extremism, left-wing extremism, religious fundamentalism such as Islamism). **Populism** is not an ideology of its own but a political style: it sets a supposedly homogeneous, "pure people" against a "corrupt elite" and claims to be the sole representative of the true will of the people. Populism can combine with left-wing or right-wing content.

### Functions of parties

Parties are permanent associations that seek political power by taking part in elections. In Austria, the **Political Parties Act** regulates their founding, financing and transparency. They

- aggregate interests and formulate programmes,
- select people for political office and nominate candidates,
- form the government and the opposition,
- mediate between citizens and the state,
- contribute to political opinion-forming and education.

## Political participation

**Participation** covers all activities by which citizens try to influence political decisions.

| Form | Examples |
| ---- | -------- |
| **Institutionalised participation** | voting, standing for election, signing a citizens' initiative petition, taking part in referendums, submitting petitions and citizens' initiatives to parliament, pupil representation |
| **Non-institutionalised (conventional) participation** | involvement in parties, trade unions, associations, NGOs, letters to the editor, contacting members of parliament, online petitions |
| **Unconventional and protest forms** | registered demonstrations, boycotts, vigils |
| **Civil disobedience** | deliberate, non-violent, public breaking of rules to draw attention to injustice, with willingness to accept the legal consequences (Gandhi, Martin Luther King, Hainburg 1984). Its legitimacy is disputed. |
| **Illegal, violent forms** | damage to property, violence against people: not legitimate in a democracy |

More on civil society, NGOs and the media can be found in [Media and Political Participation](/en/history-and-politics/media-and-political-participation/).
