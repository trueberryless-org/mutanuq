This page covers contemporary history since the end of the Cold War. Because many of these developments are not yet complete and are politically controversial, it is particularly important here to distinguish between **established facts**, **interpretations** and **opinions** and to know several perspectives (see [Historical Methods](/en/history-and-politics/historical-methods/)). The account reflects the situation as of autumn 2026.

## European integration

After two world wars, Western European politicians such as **Robert Schuman**, **Jean Monnet**, **Konrad Adenauer** and **Alcide De Gasperi** wanted to make war between their states impossible by closely interlinking their economies. The idea: whoever jointly manages coal and steel, the basic materials of armament, can no longer wage war against each other.

| Year | Step |
| ---- | ---- |
| 1949 | founding of the **Council of Europe** (not an EU institution, see [International Organisations and Human Rights](/en/history-and-politics/international-organisations-and-human-rights/)) |
| 1950 | **Schuman Declaration** on 9 May (today Europe Day) |
| 1951 | **European Coal and Steel Community** (ECSC) with France, the FRG, Italy and the Benelux states |
| 1957 | **Treaties of Rome**: European Economic Community (EEC) and Euratom |
| 1960 | Austria and other states found the free trade area **EFTA** |
| 1968 | EEC customs union completed |
| 1979 | first direct election of the European Parliament |
| 1985 | Schengen Agreement (initially outside Community law) |
| 1993 | **single market**; the **Maastricht** Treaty comes into force: European Union |
| 2002 | **euro** cash |
| 2004/07 | eastern enlargement |
| 2009 | Treaty of **Lisbon** comes into force |
| 2020 | Brexit |

The enlargements, institutions and functioning of the EU are covered in detail on the page [The European Union](/en/history-and-politics/european-union/).

## Globalisation

**Globalisation** refers to the increasing worldwide interconnection of economies, politics, culture, communication and the environment. It is not new (see discoveries and colonialism in [The Early Modern Period](/en/history-and-politics/early-modern-period/)), but it accelerated strongly from the 1990s:

- **Causes**: end of the Cold War and the opening of China (from 1978) and Eastern Europe, reduction of tariffs (GATT, from 1995 the **World Trade Organization** WTO, China a member from 2001), liberalisation of capital markets, cheap container transport, internet and mobile communications.
- **Features**: global value chains (a smartphone consists of components from dozens of countries), multinational corporations, worldwide financial markets, migration, global media and consumer patterns.
- **Winners and losers**: hundreds of millions of people, especially in China, India and Southeast Asia, escaped extreme poverty. In industrialised countries, the well educated and export sectors benefited, while industrial jobs moved away. Inequality within many states increased.
- **Criticism** comes from the left (anti-globalisation criticism of working conditions, the environment and corporate power, for example Attac) and from the right (loss of national sovereignty, migration). Crises such as the pandemic and the war in Ukraine showed the vulnerability of long supply chains; since then there has been debate about **deglobalisation**, "friendshoring" and strategic autonomy.
- **Global challenges** such as **climate change** can only be solved by states together. In the **Paris Climate Agreement** (2015), almost all states agreed to limit global warming to well below 2 °C, preferably 1.5 °C, compared with pre-industrial levels.

## The Yugoslav wars

Yugoslavia was a socialist federal state of six republics (Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia-Herzegovina, Serbia, Montenegro, Macedonia) and two autonomous provinces (Kosovo, Vojvodina). After the death of **Josip Broz Tito** (1980), economic crisis and nationalism intensified tensions, especially the policies of the Serbian leader **Slobodan Milošević**.

| Time | Conflict |
| ---- | -------- |
| 1991 | independence of **Slovenia** after a ten-day war. Austria pushed early for the recognition of Slovenia and Croatia. |
| 1991 to 1995 | war in **Croatia** between the Croatian army and Serb militias and the Yugoslav People's Army (Vukovar 1991) |
| 1992 to 1995 | war in **Bosnia-Herzegovina** between Bosniaks, Serbs and Croats. Around 100,000 dead, more than two million displaced, siege of **Sarajevo** (1992 to 1996), "**ethnic cleansing**" and mass rape |
| July 1995 | **Srebrenica** genocide: Bosnian Serb troops under Ratko Mladić murdered around 8,000 Bosniak men and boys in a UN safe area. |
| November/December 1995 | **Dayton Agreement**: Bosnia-Herzegovina remains one state consisting of two entities (the Federation and Republika Srpska); international High Representative |
| 1998/99 | **Kosovo War**; NATO air strikes on Yugoslavia without a UN mandate (March to June 1999), followed by international administration; in 2008 Kosovo declared independence, which is still not recognised by all states (including Serbia) |
| 2001 | conflict in North Macedonia, ended by the Ohrid Agreement |

The **International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia** in The Hague (1993 to 2017) convicted, among others, Radovan Karadžić and Ratko Mladić of genocide. Milošević died in 2006 before his trial ended. Slovenia (2004) and Croatia (2013) are now EU members, and the other successor states are candidate or potential candidate countries.

## 9/11 and terrorism

On **11 September 2001**, 19 terrorists of the Islamist network **al-Qaeda** under **Osama bin Laden** hijacked four passenger aircraft. They flew two into the towers of the **World Trade Center** in New York and one into the **Pentagon** near Washington; the fourth crashed in Pennsylvania after resistance by the passengers. Almost 3,000 people died.

Consequences:

- **War on terror**: NATO invoked collective defence for the first time. The USA and its allies overthrew the Taliban regime in **Afghanistan** (2001). The international mission lasted until 2021; after the withdrawal, the Taliban took power again in August 2021.
- **Iraq War** (2003): the USA and Britain attacked Iraq without a UN mandate and overthrew Saddam Hussein. The justification that Iraq possessed weapons of mass destruction turned out to be false. Years of civil war and terror followed.
- **Security versus freedom**: many states expanded surveillance, data retention and the powers of intelligence services. Torture at Abu Ghraib, the Guantánamo detention camp and the mass surveillance revealed by Edward Snowden in 2013 triggered debates about the limits of the rule of law.
- **Islamist terrorism in Europe**: attacks in Madrid (2004), London (2005), Paris (2015, including on Charlie Hebdo and the Bataclan), Brussels (2016), Berlin (2016) and Vienna (2 November 2020, four dead). The terrorist militia **"Islamic State"** (IS) conquered large areas of Syria and Iraq in 2014 and was defeated militarily by 2019.
- Terrorism also comes from **right-wing extremists** (Oslo and Utøya 2011, Christchurch 2019, Hanau 2020) and in the past from left-wing extremists (Red Army Faction) and separatists (ETA, IRA).

## The financial and economic crisis of 2008

In the USA, banks had for years granted mortgage loans to borrowers with low creditworthiness (**subprime loans**). These loans were bundled into complex securities and sold worldwide. When house prices fell from 2006 and many borrowers could no longer pay, these securities lost massive value. Banks distrusted each other and stopped lending to one another.

- On **15 September 2008**, the investment bank **Lehman Brothers** went bankrupt. The crisis engulfed the entire global financial system.
- States rescued banks with billions ("too big to fail"); in Austria these included Kommunalkredit and **Hypo Alpe Adria**, whose winding-up cost taxpayers billions. Economic stimulus packages and short-time work were intended to cushion the economic slump.
- **Euro crisis** (from 2010): Greece, Ireland, Portugal, Spain and Cyprus could no longer finance their debts. They received rescue loans in return for strict austerity conditions. The euro rescue fund **ESM** was founded, and in 2012 the ECB announced it would do "whatever it takes".
- Long-term consequences: stricter banking regulation (banking union, higher capital requirements), a long period of low interest rates, loss of trust in elites and the rise of populist parties.

## Migration and flight

Migration has always existed (see guest workers in Austria from the 1960s, refugees from Hungary in 1956, Czechoslovakia in 1968, Poland in 1981 and Bosnia in the 1990s). A distinction is made between **flight** (from war, persecution or disasters) and **migration** for economic, family or educational reasons. Under the **Geneva Refugee Convention** (1951), a refugee is someone with a well-founded fear of persecution because of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion. Its core is the ban on returning refugees to a state where they face persecution (_non-refoulement_).

- In **2015/16**, more than a million people came to Europe, mainly from Syria, Afghanistan and Iraq, many via the "Balkan route" through Austria. The EU member states were long unable to agree on a common distribution; the **Dublin system** (responsibility of the state of first entry) did not work. In 2016 the EU concluded an agreement with Turkey.
- In 2022 millions of people fled from Ukraine to the EU; for the first time they received temporary protection under the Temporary Protection Directive without an asylum procedure.
- In 2024 the EU adopted a **Pact on Migration and Asylum** with border procedures, common databases and a solidarity mechanism; it applies from mid-2026.
- The debate moves between humanitarian obligations, questions of integration, the need for workers in ageing societies and the desire for control and limitation.

## The COVID-19 pandemic

At the end of 2019, a new coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2) appeared in **Wuhan** (China). The World Health Organization (WHO) declared its spread a **pandemic** on **11 March 2020**. The WHO estimates actual excess mortality in 2020 and 2021 at around 15 million deaths worldwide.

- States responded with **lockdowns** (in Austria for the first time from 16 March 2020), curfews, school closures, mask and testing requirements, border closures and short-time work.
- Effective **vaccines** were developed within a year, including novel mRNA vaccines. In 2022 Austria passed a general vaccination mandate but never enforced it and repealed it the same year.
- The measures led to debates about restrictions of fundamental rights, scientific policy advice, disinformation and trust in institutions; there were large protests. The Constitutional Court struck down some ordinances as unlawful.
- The WHO declared the international health emergency over in May 2023.

## Russia's war against Ukraine

Ukraine became independent in 1991 when the Soviet Union broke up. In the **Budapest Memorandum** (1994), it gave up the Soviet nuclear weapons stationed on its territory; in return, Russia, the USA and Britain assured it that its borders would be respected.

- **2013/14**: when President Yanukovych did not sign an association agreement with the EU, mass protests (**Euromaidan**) took place in Kyiv. Yanukovych fled to Russia in February 2014.
- **2014**: Russia occupied the Ukrainian peninsula of **Crimea** and **annexed** it in March 2014 after a referendum that is not recognised internationally. In **Donbas**, separatists supported and armed by Russia fought against the Ukrainian army. The **Minsk** agreements (2014/15) did not bring lasting peace; by early 2022 more than 14,000 people had died.
- **24 February 2022**: Russia began a **full-scale invasion** of Ukraine. The attack on Kyiv failed, but Russia occupied large parts of the east and south. In September 2022 it declared four Ukrainian regions to be Russian territory in violation of international law.
- In March 2022 the UN General Assembly condemned the attack with 141 votes as a violation of the UN Charter. In 2023 the **International Criminal Court** issued an arrest warrant against President Vladimir Putin for the deportation of Ukrainian children.
- **Consequences**: tens of thousands of civilians killed and very high military losses on both sides, millions of refugees, massive destruction. The EU imposed extensive **sanctions** on Russia and supported Ukraine financially, with humanitarian aid and militarily (Austria, as a neutral state, only takes part in non-lethal aid). Europe reduced its dependence on Russian gas, and energy prices and inflation rose sharply. **Finland** (2023) and **Sweden** (2024) joined NATO. Ukraine and Moldova received EU candidate status in 2022.
- Since 2025 there have been negotiations mediated by the USA, prisoner exchanges and short, limited truces, but by autumn 2026 no lasting ceasefire and no peace treaty.

The Russian leadership justifies the war with an alleged threat from NATO enlargement and the protection of Russian speakers; it denies that Ukraine is a separate nation. Ukraine, the EU, the USA and a large majority of UN member states regard it as a **war of aggression in violation of international law**, which breaches the prohibition of the use of force and territorial integrity under the UN Charter.

## Conflicts in the Middle East

Since the end of the Ottoman Empire and the British and French mandate period, the Middle East has been a region of many conflicts, in which outside powers also intervene.

### The Israeli-Palestinian conflict

| Time | Event |
| ---- | ----- |
| 1917 | **Balfour Declaration**: Britain supports a "national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine |
| 1947 | **UN Partition Plan** for a Jewish and an Arab state; accepted by the Jewish side, rejected by the Arab side |
| 14 May 1948 | founding of the State of **Israel**; attack by Arab states. For the Palestinians the war means the **Nakba** ("catastrophe"): around 700,000 fled or were expelled. At the same time, hundreds of thousands of Jews left Arab states in the following years. |
| 1967 | **Six-Day War**: Israel occupies the West Bank, East Jerusalem, the Gaza Strip, Sinai and the Golan Heights. Beginning of settlement construction in the occupied territories |
| 1973 | Yom Kippur War; 1979 peace treaty between Israel and Egypt |
| 1987 and 2000 | First and Second **Intifada** (Palestinian uprisings), the second with numerous suicide bombings |
| 1993/95 | **Oslo Accords**: mutual recognition of Israel and the PLO, Palestinian Authority; the intended final peace (**two-state solution**) was not achieved |
| 2005/07 | Israeli withdrawal from the Gaza Strip; the Islamist **Hamas** takes power there by force in 2007, Israel and Egypt impose a blockade |
| 7 October 2023 | terrorist attack by Hamas and other groups on Israel: around 1,200 people killed, more than 250 taken hostage; the day with the most Jews murdered since the Shoah |
| from October 2023 | **war in the Gaza Strip**: according to the health authority in Gaza, which is controlled by Hamas and whose figures UN bodies consider largely plausible, tens of thousands of Palestinians were killed; large parts of the territory were destroyed and the humanitarian situation was catastrophic. International courts are dealing with allegations of war crimes and genocide, which Israel rejects. |
| October 2025 | **ceasefire** based on a US plan; the hostages still alive were released, and Israel released around 2,000 Palestinian prisoners |

The conflict is interpreted very differently. Key points of dispute are borders, settlements, the status of Jerusalem, the right of return of refugees, security and terror, and mutual recognition. For Austria and Germany, the history of the Holocaust adds a special responsibility for Israel's security and for combating **antisemitism**; at the same time, they are committed to international law and a two-state solution.

### Other conflicts

- **Arab Spring** (from December 2010): protests against authoritarian rulers toppled the regimes in Tunisia, Egypt, Libya and Yemen. Except in Tunisia, where democracy has since been dismantled again, military rule or civil war followed.
- **Syria**: the civil war from 2011 cost hundreds of thousands of lives and drove millions to flee. Russia and Iran supported the regime of Bashar al-Assad, which also used poison gas. In December 2024 Assad was overthrown by rebels.
- **Iran**: the Islamic Republic (since 1979) pursues a nuclear programme, which it describes as civilian, and supports groups such as Hezbollah in Lebanon. The nuclear agreement negotiated in Vienna (2015) lost its effect after the US withdrawal in 2018. In June 2025 Israel and the USA attacked Iranian nuclear and military facilities. Within the country, protests, for example in 2022 after the death of Jina Mahsa Amini in police custody ("Woman, Life, Freedom"), were violently suppressed.
- **Yemen**: civil war since 2014 with the involvement of Saudi Arabia and Iran, one of the worst humanitarian crises in the world.
