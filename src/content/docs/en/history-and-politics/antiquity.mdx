**Antiquity** covers the period from the first civilisations to the end of the Western Roman Empire in AD 476. It produced institutions that still shape Europe today: writing, law as a rule valid for everyone, democracy, Roman law, Christianity as a state religion and a network of towns and roads on which many present-day towns, including in Austria, are built.

## Early civilisations

From around 3500 BC, the first **civilisations** (advanced cultures) emerged along large rivers: in Mesopotamia on the Euphrates and Tigris (Sumerians, later Babylonians and Assyrians), in Egypt on the Nile, in the Indus Valley and in China on the Yellow River. They are also called **river civilisations** because irrigation agriculture produced surpluses that could feed many people who did not work in farming.

Features of a civilisation:

- **writing** (cuneiform, hieroglyphs) for administration, taxes and law
- **cities** with temples, palaces and markets
- **division of labour** and specialisation (farmers, craftsmen, priests, scribes, warriors)
- central **rule** (king, pharaoh), often legitimised by religion
- **administration** and taxes
- knowledge of astronomy, mathematics and calendars (Nile flood)

The **law stele of Hammurabi** (around 1750 BC) is one of the oldest surviving collections of laws. It shows the idea that law should be written down and public; however, punishments depended on social rank, and the principle of retaliation ("an eye for an eye") applied.

## Greece

### The polis

Greece was never a unified state. The mountainous terrain encouraged the emergence of many independent **poleis** (city-states, singular _polis_), which were linked by language, religion (the Olympian gods), the Olympic Games and the oracle of Delphi. From the 8th century BC, Greeks founded colonies around the Mediterranean and the Black Sea, such as Massalia (Marseille) and Byzantion (later Constantinople, Istanbul).

**Sparta** was a militaristic state with two kings, a council of elders and a small class of full Spartan citizens who ruled over a large number of unfree helots. **Athens**, by contrast, developed into a democracy.

### Athenian democracy

| Step | Content |
| ---- | ------- |
| **Solon** (594 BC) | abolition of debt bondage, division of citizens into four classes by wealth (timocracy) |
| **Cleisthenes** (508/507 BC) | reorganisation into ten tribes (phylai), Council of 500, ostracism; regarded as the beginning of democracy |
| **Pericles** (c. 461 to 429 BC) | daily allowances for offices so that poorer citizens could take part; heyday of Athens |

The institutions of Athenian democracy:

- **Assembly** (_ekklesia_): all male full citizens aged 18 and over; decided laws, war and peace directly (direct democracy).
- **Council of 500** (_boule_): prepared decisions; its members were **chosen by lot** from the tribes.
- **People's courts** with hundreds of jurors chosen by lot.
- **Strategoi**: ten elected generals, one of the few offices filled by election rather than lot.
- **Ostracism**: with potsherds, a politician considered dangerous could be banished for ten years.

Limitations: only part of the population had political rights. **Women, slaves and metics** (resident foreigners) were excluded. Estimates assume around 30,000 to 40,000 full citizens out of a total population of several hundred thousand.

Greek philosophers were the first to think systematically about forms of government. **Aristotle** distinguished rule by one, by few and by many, each in a good form (monarchy, aristocracy, polity) and a degenerate form (tyranny, oligarchy, democracy in the sense of rule by the poor in their own interest). This classification still has an influence today (see [Democracy and Political Theory](/en/history-and-politics/democracy-and-political-theory/)).

### The Hellenistic period

**Alexander the Great** (King of Macedonia 336 to 323 BC) conquered the Persian Empire as far as Egypt and India. In the successor kingdoms, Greek language and culture spread throughout the eastern Mediterranean (**Hellenism**). Alexandria in Egypt, with its library, became a centre of science (Euclid, Archimedes, Eratosthenes).

## Rome

### The Republic

According to tradition, the Romans expelled their last king in 509 BC and founded a **republic** (_res publica_, "public matter"). It combined elements of monarchy, aristocracy and democracy (**mixed constitution**):

- **Magistrates**: officials elected annually, always at least two at a time (**annuality** and **collegiality** as protection against abuse of power). At the top were two **consuls**. In emergencies a dictator could be appointed for a maximum of six months.
- **Senate**: assembly of former magistrates, mostly from the nobility (patricians); great influence on foreign policy and finance.
- **Popular assemblies**: elected magistrates and passed laws; however, the votes of the rich carried more weight.
- In the **Conflict of the Orders** (5th to 3rd century BC), the plebeians won a share in decision-making, for example the tribunes of the people with a right of veto and the **Law of the Twelve Tables** (around 450 BC), Rome's first written law.

Rome first conquered Italy, then the western Mediterranean in the **Punic Wars** (264 to 146 BC) against Carthage. Expansion brought wealth but also social crises: small farmers became impoverished, while large estates worked by slaves grew. In the 1st century BC, civil wars between powerful generals led to the end of the Republic. **Gaius Julius Caesar** conquered Gaul, had himself appointed dictator for life and was assassinated on 15 March 44 BC.

### The imperial period

Caesar's great-nephew **Octavian** prevailed in the civil war and in 27 BC received the honorary title **Augustus**. He formally retained the republican institutions but united the decisive powers in his own person (**Principate**). About two centuries of relative peace followed (_Pax Romana_). Under Emperor Trajan, the empire reached its greatest extent around AD 117, from Britain to Mesopotamia.

What held it together:

- a network of **roads** (around 80,000 km of paved highways), bridges and aqueducts
- a professional army with camps on the **limes**, the fortified frontier along the Rhine and Danube
- a common administrative and legal order, Latin as the official language in the West
- in AD 212, citizenship for almost all free inhabitants of the empire (_Constitutio Antoniniana_)

In the 3rd century the empire fell into crisis (frequently changing "soldier emperors", pressure on the frontiers, inflation). **Diocletian** (284 to 305) reformed administration and taxes and divided rule among several emperors. **Constantine** favoured Christianity (Agreement of Milan 313) and founded Constantinople (330). Under **Theodosius I**, Christianity became the state religion in 380; after his death in 395 the empire was permanently divided.

The **Western Roman Empire** disintegrated in the 5th century under pressure from Germanic groups (see [The Middle Ages](/en/history-and-politics/middle-ages/)); in 476 the Germanic military leader Odoacer deposed the last emperor. The **Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire** lasted until the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453.

### Roman law

Roman law is one of the most important legacies of antiquity. Emperor **Justinian** had it collected between 529 and 534 in what was later called the **Corpus iuris civilis**. From the 11th century it was studied again at the University of Bologna and shaped the civil law of continental Europe, including the Austrian **ABGB** (General Civil Code) of 1811 (see [Civil Law](/en/economy_and_law/law/civil_law/)). Principles that go back to Roman law:

- _Pacta sunt servanda_: agreements must be kept.
- _In dubio pro reo_: when in doubt, in favour of the accused.
- _Audiatur et altera pars_: the other side must also be heard.
- The distinction between **ownership** (legal control over a thing) and **possession** (actual control).

## Noricum and Roman Austria

Before the Romans, the area of present-day Austria was settled by **Celts**. Well known are the salt mines of **Hallstatt**, after which an entire period of the early Iron Age is named (Hallstatt culture, c. 800 to 450 BC), and the princely graves on the Dürrnberg near Hallein. The Celtic tribes formed the **Kingdom of Noricum**, whose centre was probably on the Magdalensberg in Carinthia. Norican iron (_ferrum Noricum_) was known throughout the Roman Empire for its quality.

Noricum was incorporated into the Roman Empire largely peacefully in **15 BC** and became a province under Emperor Claudius (41 to 54 AD). The east of present-day Austria belonged to the province of **Pannonia**, Vorarlberg and parts of Tyrol to the province of **Raetia**. The Danube formed the imperial frontier (limes).

| ---------- | -------------------- | ------------ |
| **Carnuntum** | Petronell-Carnuntum and Bad Deutsch-Altenburg (Lower Austria) | legionary fortress and civilian town, capital of Upper Pannonia |
| **Vindobona** | Vienna | legionary fortress on the Danube |
| **Lauriacum** | Enns (Upper Austria) | legionary fortress |
| **Iuvavum** | Salzburg | town (municipium) |
| **Virunum** | Zollfeld near Klagenfurt | capital of Noricum |
| **Brigantium** | Bregenz | settlement on Lake Constance |
| **Aguntum** | near Lienz | town (municipium) |
| **Flavia Solva** | near Leibnitz | town (municipium) |

**Carnuntum** on the Danube east of Vienna, with at times around 50,000 inhabitants, was one of the largest cities in the Danube region and from the beginning of the 2nd century the capital of the province of Upper Pannonia. Emperor **Marcus Aurelius** waged war against the Marcomanni from here and wrote parts of his "Meditations" in the Danube region. In AD 308, the **Imperial Conference** under Diocletian took place in Carnuntum and reorganised the balance of power in the empire. Today the Carnuntum Archaeological Park shows full-size reconstructed buildings; the Heathens' Gate (Heidentor) is a landmark of the region.

In the 5th century, Roman order on the Danube collapsed. The biography of **Saint Severinus** (died 482) describes how the Romanised population of Noricum Ripense lived under pressure from Germanic groups. In 488 Odoacer had a large part of them resettled in Italy.
