The rule of National Socialism (in Germany 1933 to 1945, in Austria 1938 to 1945) led to the most devastating war in history and to the **Holocaust**, the genocide of the European Jews. Studying this period is a central part of political education: it shows how a dictatorship arises and functions, how many people took part or looked away, and why human rights, the rule of law and democracy must be protected. In Austria, the Prohibition Act of 1947, which makes any National Socialist activity and Holocaust denial a criminal offence, expresses this responsibility.

## Nazi ideology

Nazi ideology was not a closed system but a combination of older ideas, which Hitler set out in "Mein Kampf" (1925/26):

- **Racism**: humanity consists of "races" of unequal value that are in eternal struggle (**social Darwinism**). The "Aryans" were said to be the highest, the Jews the most dangerous "counter-race".
- **Antisemitism**: hatred of Jews was a central element. Jews were blamed for every evil, for capitalism as well as communism and the defeat of 1918.
- **National community** (_Volksgemeinschaft_): a supposedly harmonious, racially "pure" people without class conflict, from which all "community aliens" were excluded.
- **Leader principle**: absolute authority of the "Führer" and obedience from top to bottom instead of elections and majorities.
- **Living space in the East**: conquest and settlement of Eastern Europe, enslavement or annihilation of the Slavic population.
- **Anti-Marxism, anti-liberalism, anti-democratic thinking**, rejection of the Treaty of Versailles.
- **Militarism** and glorification of violence.

## The Nazi state

Within a few months, the regime abolished democracy in Germany (**seizure of power**):

1. The **Reichstag Fire Decree** (28 February 1933) suspended fundamental rights and remained in force until 1945. Communists and Social Democrats were arrested and taken to early concentration camps such as **Dachau**.
2. The **Enabling Act** (passed on 23 March 1933) allowed the government to pass laws without the Reichstag. Only the SPD voted against; the KPD deputies had already been arrested or had fled.
3. **Gleichschaltung** (forcible coordination): federal states, trade unions (2 May 1933), parties (July 1933: NSDAP the only party), press, associations and culture were subjected to the NSDAP. In May 1933, books by unwelcome authors were publicly burned.
4. After the murder of the SA leadership (the **"Röhm Putsch"**, 30 June 1934) and Hindenburg's death, Hitler combined the offices of Reich President and Reich Chancellor. The Wehrmacht swore an oath to him personally.

Rule was secured through **terror** and **seduction**: the Gestapo, the SS (Heinrich Himmler) and concentration camps on the one hand; propaganda (Joseph Goebbels, radio, film, mass rallies), youth organisations (Hitler Youth, League of German Girls), leisure offers ("Strength through Joy") and the reduction of unemployment through rearmament and state contracts (motorway construction) on the other. Rearmament was financed with debts that were to be covered by war and plunder.

## The "Anschluss" of 1938

Under pressure from Hitler, Chancellor Schuschnigg had to appoint the National Socialist Arthur Seyss-Inquart as interior minister in the **Berchtesgaden Agreement** (12 February 1938). To save Austria's independence, Schuschnigg announced a plebiscite for **13 March**. Hitler demanded that it be called off. On the evening of **11 March 1938**, Schuschnigg resigned ("God protect Austria"). That same night, National Socialists took power in Austria, and attacks on Jews and political opponents began.

- On **12 March 1938**, the German Wehrmacht marched in without resistance, welcomed by cheering crowds in many places.
- On **13 March**, the "Law on the Reunification of Austria with the German Reich" was issued.
- On **15 March**, Hitler spoke on the **Heldenplatz** in Vienna to a huge crowd.
- In the "referendum" of **10 April 1938**, officially 99.73 % in Austria voted yes. The vote was not free: Jews and arrested opponents were excluded, open voting was expected and propaganda was everywhere. Cardinal Innitzer and the Social Democrat Karl Renner had also called for a yes vote.

Austria disappeared as a state and was divided into **"Reichsgaue"** (from 1942 called the "Alpine and Danube Reichsgaue"; the name Austria was to disappear). Britain and France protested only formally; Mexico alone lodged an official protest with the League of Nations.

## Persecution

Immediately after the "Anschluss", a wave of violence began in Austria whose brutality shocked even observers from Germany. Jews were humiliated (for example forced to "scrub" the streets), and their flats and shops were looted. As early as 1 April 1938, the first transport of Austrian prisoners, mostly political opponents, left for Dachau concentration camp.

### Stages of the persecution of Jews

| Stage | Measures |
| ----- | -------- |
| **Exclusion** | boycott of Jewish shops (1933), dismissal from public service, occupational bans |
| **Deprivation of rights** | **Nuremberg Laws** 1935: Jews lose Reich citizenship, marriage ban between Jews and those "of German blood"; in force in Austria from May 1938 |
| **Robbery** | **"Aryanisation"**: expropriation of Jewish businesses, flats and assets; "Reich flight tax"; in Vienna the "Central Office for Jewish Emigration" under Adolf Eichmann organised the expulsion |
| **Violence** | **November Pogrom** 9/10 November 1938: synagogues and prayer houses set on fire (almost all in Vienna), shops destroyed, thousands of men taken to concentration camps, many murdered |
| **Expulsion** | around 130,000 Austrian Jews were able to flee by 1941, often losing all their property |
| **Deportation and extermination** | from 1939/41 deportations to ghettos and extermination camps in the East |

Of the around 206,000 people in Austria who were considered Jews under the Nuremberg Laws in 1938, more than **65,000 were murdered**.

### Other groups of victims

- **Roma and Sinti**: genocide (_Porajmos_); most Burgenland Roma were deported from Austria and murdered; there was a camp in Lackenbach.
- **People with disabilities** and the mentally ill: in **"Aktion T4"**, more than 70,000 people were gassed in 1940/41, including at **Hartheim Castle** near Linz, where around 30,000 people were murdered by 1944. At the Vienna clinic **Am Spiegelgrund**, around 800 children were killed.
- **Political opponents**: communists, socialists, monarchists, Catholic and Christian Social functionaries
- **Carinthian Slovenes**: in April 1942, deportation of hundreds of families and expropriation of their farms
- **Jehovah's Witnesses**, homosexuals, people persecuted as "asocial" or "work-shy"
- **Forced labourers**: millions of people from occupied territories and prisoners of war had to work in the Reich, also in Austria, for example at the Hermann Göring Works in Linz or in the construction of the power station in Kaprun.

## The Holocaust (Shoah)

The **Holocaust** (Greek "wholly burnt") or **Shoah** (Hebrew "catastrophe") refers to the systematic, state-organised murder of around **six million** European Jews.

- After the invasion of Poland, Jews were crammed into **ghettos** (Warsaw, Łódź), where many died of hunger and disease.
- From June 1941, SS and police **Einsatzgruppen** (mobile killing units), supported by the Wehrmacht and local helpers, murdered over a million Jews in the Soviet Union in mass shootings (e.g. Babyn Yar near Kyiv, September 1941: more than 33,000 people in two days).
- At the **Wannsee Conference** on 20 January 1942, senior officials under Reinhard Heydrich coordinated the organisation of the "**Final Solution of the Jewish Question**", which was already under way.
- In **extermination camps** in occupied Poland (Belzec, Sobibor, Treblinka, Chełmno, Majdanek, **Auschwitz-Birkenau**), people were murdered with gas. In Auschwitz alone, around 1.1 million people were killed, about one million of them Jews.
- The perpetrators were not only Germans: among the organisers, Austrians were disproportionately represented, for example **Adolf Eichmann** (who grew up in Linz), Ernst Kaltenbrunner (head of the Reich Security Main Office), Odilo Globocnik (head of "Operation Reinhard") and Franz Stangl (commandant of Sobibor and Treblinka).

**Mauthausen concentration camp** in Upper Austria, with the Gusen subcamp and around 40 further subcamps, was the largest concentration camp on Austrian soil. Of around 190,000 prisoners, at least 90,000 died from forced labour in the quarry (the "Stairs of Death"), hunger, disease, gas and abuse. Today it is a memorial site.

27 January, the day Auschwitz was liberated, has been the United Nations' **International Holocaust Remembrance Day** since 2005. In Austria, **5 May**, the day Mauthausen was liberated, is a day of remembrance against violence and racism.

## Resistance

Resistance ranged from refusal in everyday life and help for the persecuted to armed struggle. It was life-threatening: in Austria, thousands of resistance fighters were executed or died in custody.

- **Communists and socialists**: organised underground groups and distributed leaflets; the KPÖ made up the majority of those persecuted.
- **Religiously motivated resistance**: the Innviertel farmer **Franz Jägerstätter** refused military service for reasons of faith and was executed in 1943 (beatified in 2007). Sister Restituta Kafka was executed in 1943.
- **Armed resistance**: **Carinthian Slovenes** fought as partisans against the regime, the only militarily organised resistance movement on Austrian territory.
- **Military resistance**: in Germany, a group around Colonel **Claus Schenk Graf von Stauffenberg** tried to kill Hitler on **20 July 1944** and bring about a coup. In Vienna, Major **Carl Szokoll** was involved; in April 1945 he also tried to hand Vienna over to the Red Army without a fight.
- **O5**: a cross-party resistance group in the final phase (the abbreviation stands for Oe, the first letters of Österreich).
- **Students and individuals**: the **White Rose** in Munich (Sophie and Hans Scholl, executed 1943); Georg Elser, who carried out a bomb attack on Hitler in 1939.
- **Help for the persecuted**: people who hid Jews or helped them escape are honoured by the Yad Vashem memorial as **"Righteous Among the Nations"**, including more than 100 Austrians.

## The Second World War

### The road to war

Hitler broke the Treaty of Versailles step by step: withdrawal from the League of Nations (1933), reintroduction of conscription (1935), occupation of the demilitarised Rhineland (1936), "Anschluss" of Austria (1938). Britain and France pursued a policy of **appeasement** to avoid war. In the **Munich Agreement** (September 1938), they agreed to the cession of the Sudeten areas. In March 1939, Hitler occupied the rest of Czechoslovakia. After that, Britain and France guaranteed Poland's independence.

In the **Hitler-Stalin Pact** of 23 August 1939, Germany and the Soviet Union agreed on non-aggression and, in a secret additional protocol, on the division of East Central Europe.

### Course of the war

| Phase | Events |
| ----- | ------ |
| **"Blitzkrieg" campaigns** 1939 to 1941 | invasion of **Poland** on 1 September 1939; declarations of war by Britain and France on 3 September; the Soviet Union occupies eastern Poland. 1940 occupation of Denmark, Norway, the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg; defeat of **France** in June 1940. The Battle of Britain fails. 1941 Balkan campaign (Yugoslavia, Greece) and North Africa |
| **War of annihilation in the East** from 1941 | invasion of the **Soviet Union** on **22 June 1941** ("Operation Barbarossa"), planned as a racially motivated war of annihilation: hunger as a weapon, millions of dead Soviet prisoners of war, mass murder of Jews. Advance to just outside Moscow, stopped in winter 1941 |
| **World war** from December 1941 | Japanese attack on **Pearl Harbor** (7 December 1941), Germany declares war on the USA. Opponents: the **Allies** (USA, Soviet Union, Britain, China and others) against the **Axis powers** (Germany, Italy, Japan and others) |
| **Turning point** 1942/43 | defeat at **Stalingrad** (surrender on 2 February 1943), in North Africa (El Alamein, Tunis) and in the Pacific (Midway). Goebbels proclaims "total war". Allied landing in Italy 1943, fall of Mussolini |
| **Collapse** 1944/45 | landing in **Normandy** on 6 June 1944 (D-Day), major Soviet offensive; bombing war against German (and Austrian) cities. Allied troops reach Germany; Hitler commits suicide in Berlin on 30 April 1945. **Unconditional surrender** on **8 May 1945** |
| **Pacific** 1945 | atomic bombs on **Hiroshima** (6 August) and **Nagasaki** (9 August); Japanese surrender on **2 September 1945** |

According to common estimates, the Second World War claimed **60 to 65 million lives**, more than half of them civilians. The Soviet Union suffered the highest losses with around 27 million dead, followed by China; Poland lost around a sixth of its population.

## Austria in the war and the question of responsibility

Around **1.2 to 1.3 million** Austrians served in the Wehrmacht and the Waffen-SS; about **250,000** of them were killed or went missing. In addition there were civilian bombing victims and the victims of persecution. From 1943, Austrian cities were also bombed, especially Vienna, Linz, Wiener Neustadt, Graz, Innsbruck and Villach. Industry in Austria was massively expanded for armaments production, using forced labour and concentration camp prisoners.

### The Moscow Declaration

In the **Moscow Declaration** of 30 October 1943 (published on 1 November), the foreign ministers of the USA, Britain and the Soviet Union declared that Austria, **"the first free country to fall a victim to Hitlerite aggression"**, should be restored. At the same time, however, it stated that Austria had a **responsibility for participation in the war** on the side of Hitler's Germany which it could not evade, and that in the final settlement account would be taken of its own contribution to its liberation.

### The victim thesis and its revision

After 1945, the Second Republic invoked almost exclusively the first part: Austria had been the **first victim** of National Socialism (**victim thesis**); the state had not existed from 1938 to 1945 and therefore bore no responsibility. This view

- made the State Treaty and the reconstruction of an Austrian identity easier,
- but suppressed the broad approval of the "Anschluss", the high number of Austrian NSDAP members (around 700,000 out of 6.7 million inhabitants, including family members a large part of the population) and the participation of many Austrians in Nazi crimes,
- delayed the **restitution** of stolen property and **compensation** of the victims by decades.

**Denazification** began with the Prohibition Act (1945/47) and people's courts, which convicted more than 13,000 people by 1955. From 1948, most "less incriminated" former National Socialists were amnestied and courted as voters.

Only the **Waldheim debate** of 1986 triggered a broad public examination of the past (see [Austria after 1945](/en/history-and-politics/post-war-austria/)). On **8 July 1991**, Chancellor **Franz Vranitzky** acknowledged for the first time officially in the National Council the **shared responsibility** of Austrians for the crimes of the Nazi regime and apologised on behalf of the Republic. This was followed by:

- 1995: **National Fund of the Republic of Austria for Victims of National Socialism**
- 1998 to 2003: Historians' Commission on the confiscation of property and restitution; 1998 Art Restitution Act
- 2000: Reconciliation Fund for former forced labourers; 2001 Washington Agreement and General Settlement Fund
- memorials, the Memorial to the Austrian Jewish Victims of the Shoah on Judenplatz (2000) and the Shoah Wall of Names Memorial in Ostarrichipark in Vienna (2021)
- since 2020, descendants of people persecuted by the Nazis can obtain Austrian citizenship without giving up their existing one.

## The end of the war in 1945

From the end of March 1945, the **Red Army** advanced onto Austrian territory. In the **battle for Vienna** (until 13 April 1945), large parts of the city were destroyed, and St. Stephen's Cathedral burned. In the last weeks of the war, further crimes took place, such as death marches of Hungarian Jews and massacres of forced labourers. American, British and French troops occupied the west and south of Austria; Yugoslav partisans entered Carinthia.

On **27 April 1945**, representatives of the **SPÖ**, **ÖVP** and **KPÖ** proclaimed **Austria's independence** in Vienna. A **Provisional State Government** under **Karl Renner** put the Federal Constitutional Law back into force in its 1929 version. On **8 May 1945**, the war in Europe ended. The road to the Second Republic is described in [Austria after 1945](/en/history-and-politics/post-war-austria/).
