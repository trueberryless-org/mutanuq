Austria is a **democratic, parliamentary, federal republic**. Its basis is the **Federal Constitutional Law** (_Bundes-Verfassungsgesetz_, B-VG) of 1920 in the version of 1929, which was put back into force in 1945 (for its origins see [The Interwar Period](/en/history-and-politics/interwar-period/)). This page describes how the organs of state work together, how laws are made and how you yourself can take part. Legal foundations such as the hierarchy of norms are covered in [Basic Structures of Austrian Law](/en/economy_and_law/law/legal_system/).

## The Federal Constitution

Austrian constitutional law is not contained in a single document. In addition to the B-VG, there are numerous **federal constitutional laws** and **constitutional provisions** in ordinary laws, such as the Basic Law of 1867 with fundamental rights, the **European Convention on Human Rights** (with constitutional status since 1964), the Neutrality Act (1955) and the Federal Constitutional Law on the Rights of Children (2011).

The National Council can only pass constitutional laws with a **two-thirds majority** when at least half of its members are present; they must be expressly designated as a "constitutional law" or "constitutional provision".

### Basic principles

The constitution rests on basic principles (_Baugesetze_). A **total revision**, meaning a change to one of these principles, additionally requires a **mandatory referendum** (Art. 44(3) B-VG). This has happened only once so far: on EU accession in 1994.

| Principle | Meaning | Basis |
| --------- | ------- | ----- |
| **Democratic** | "Austria is a democratic republic. Its law emanates from the people." Laws are passed by the elected parliament. | Art. 1 B-VG |
| **Republican** | The head of state is elected for a fixed term; no monarchy. | Art. 1, Art. 60 B-VG, Habsburg Law |
| **Federal** | Austria consists of nine autonomous provinces (_Länder_) with their own legislation and administration. | Art. 2 B-VG |
| **Rule of law** | The entire state administration may only be exercised on the basis of laws; independent courts and constitutional jurisdiction provide control. | Art. 18, Art. 137 ff. B-VG |
| **Separation of powers** | separation of legislation, administration and jurisdiction; separation of justice and administration at all levels | Art. 94 B-VG |
| **Liberal** | Fundamental rights and freedoms protect the individual against the state. | Basic Law 1867, ECHR |

Some legal scholars do not count the separation of powers and the liberal principle as separate basic principles but as parts of the rule of law and democratic principles.

## Legislation: parliament

The Austrian Parliament consists of two chambers, the **National Council** (_Nationalrat_) and the **Federal Council** (_Bundesrat_). Together they form the **Federal Assembly**, for example for swearing in the Federal President or for a decision on a declaration of war.

### National Council

- **183 members**, elected for **five years** (legislative period)
- elects from among its members a **President** and two further presidents. Since October 2024, Walter Rosenkranz (FPÖ) has been President of the National Council.
- The members of a party form a **parliamentary group** (_Klub_, at least five members).
- Most of the work takes place in **committees**, which discuss bills.
- Members have a **free mandate**: they are not bound by instructions. In practice they usually vote as a bloc with their group (**party discipline**, not legally binding).
- **Immunity**: members may not be prosecuted for their votes and statements in parliament (professional immunity).

Functions of the National Council:

| Function | Instruments |
| -------- | ----------- |
| **Legislation** | passing federal laws and constitutional laws; approval of international treaties |
| **Budget** | passing the Federal Finance Act (budgetary sovereignty) |
| **Political oversight** | written and oral **questions** (right of interpellation), **committees of inquiry** (since 2015 a minority right: a quarter of the members can set one up), resolutions, **vote of no confidence** against the government or individual ministers |
| **Legal oversight** | impeachment of ministers before the Constitutional Court |
| **Participation in implementation** | involvement in EU affairs (Main Committee), consent to certain ordinances |
| **Electoral function** | election of the presidents of the Court of Audit and the Ombudsman Board, nomination of constitutional judges |

### Federal Council

The **Federal Council** represents the interests of the **provinces** in federal legislation. Its **60 members** (as of 2026) are delegated by the provincial parliaments. How many members a province sends depends on the number of its citizens: the most populous province (Lower Austria) sends 12, every province at least 3. The chair rotates every six months in alphabetical order of the provinces.

For most laws, the Federal Council has only a **suspensive veto**: it can raise a reasoned objection within eight weeks. The National Council can then pass a **resolution of insistence** with at least half of its members present, and the law is passed anyway. The Federal Council has an **absolute veto** on constitutional laws that restrict the competences of the provinces and on changes that concern itself. It does not participate in the budget. Because its power is limited and its members usually vote along party lines rather than according to provincial interests, reform of the Federal Council is regularly discussed.

### How a federal law is made

1. **Legislative initiative** (bill):
   - **Government bill**: from the federal government; usually after a public **consultation procedure** in which provinces, chambers, NGOs and citizens can comment. Most laws originate this way.
   - **Private member's motion**: from at least five members of the National Council
   - **Committee motion** of a National Council committee
   - **Motion of the Federal Council** or of a third of its members
   - **Citizens' initiative petition** (_Volksbegehren_) with at least 100,000 signatures
2. **Debate in the National Council**: first reading (optional), referral to a **committee**, committee report, **second reading** in plenary (debate, amendments), **third reading** (vote on the bill as a whole). Ordinary laws require a simple majority with a third of the members present.
3. **Federal Council**: approval, expiry of the deadline without objection, or objection (then possibly a resolution of insistence).
4. **Authentication** by the **Federal President**: he certifies that the law was passed in accordance with the constitution.
5. **Countersignature** by the **Federal Chancellor**.
6. **Promulgation** in the **Federal Law Gazette** (now electronically in the Legal Information System RIS). The law generally comes into force on the day after promulgation unless otherwise specified.

## Executive: Federal President and federal government

### Federal President

The **Federal President** is the **head of state**. He or she is directly elected by the people for **six years**; one re-election is permitted. A candidate is elected with more than half of the valid votes; otherwise there is a **run-off** between the two candidates with the most votes. Anyone who has the right to vote in National Council elections and is at least 35 years old on election day can stand. **Alexander Van der Bellen** has been in office since 2017 (re-elected in 2022).

Powers:

- **appointment of the Federal Chancellor** and, on his or her proposal, of the other **members of the government**; **dismissal** of the federal government (without a proposal) or of individual members (on the chancellor's proposal)
- **dissolution of the National Council** on the proposal of the federal government, but only once for the same reason
- **authentication** of federal laws
- **representing the Republic externally**, concluding international treaties
- **supreme command of the armed forces**
- appointment of judges and senior civil servants, pardons, awarding titles
- **emergency decree powers** within narrow limits

Almost all acts of the Federal President require a proposal by the federal government and the **countersignature** of the chancellor or the responsible minister. This limits the president's actual power. The Federal Presidents of the Second Republic have always exercised their powers with restraint (**renunciation of role**), but have influence through their standing and speeches. Removal is possible by referendum, which must be demanded by the Federal Assembly; if the people reject the removal, the National Council is deemed dissolved.

### Federal government

The **federal government** consists of the **Federal Chancellor**, the **Vice-Chancellor** and the **federal ministers**; state secretaries support them but are not members. It is the highest executive body of the federation and decides unanimously in the **Council of Ministers**. Unlike the German chancellor, the Austrian chancellor has no power to set policy guidelines; ministers run their departments independently and can issue instructions to their ministries.

Because the government depends on the **confidence of the National Council** (a vote of no confidence is possible with a simple majority), in practice it needs a **parliamentary majority**. Therefore **coalitions** almost always govern. Since **3 March 2025**, a coalition of **ÖVP, SPÖ and NEOS** has governed, with **Christian Stocker** (ÖVP) as Federal Chancellor.

Only one vote of no confidence has succeeded in the Second Republic: on 27 May 2019 against the government of Sebastian Kurz.

## Provinces and municipalities

### The federal provinces

Austria has **nine federal provinces**: Burgenland, Carinthia, Lower Austria, Upper Austria, Salzburg, Styria, Tyrol, Vorarlberg and Vienna. Each province has its own **provincial constitution**.

- **Provincial parliament** (_Landtag_): legislation of the province, directly elected (usually for five years, in Upper Austria for six).
- **Provincial government** headed by the **governor** (_Landeshauptmann_ or _Landeshauptfrau_): elected by the provincial parliament. The governor also implements federal law in many areas (**indirect federal administration**).

The **distribution of competences** (Art. 10 to 15 B-VG) determines who passes and implements laws in which matters. The federation is responsible for, among other things, foreign policy, civil and criminal law, schools (largely), trade law and transport. The provinces are mainly responsible for spatial planning, building law, nature conservation, hunting, youth protection and kindergartens. Anything not expressly assigned to the federation falls under the competence of the provinces (**general clause**, Art. 15). In practice, Austria is a rather **centralised federal state**: the important legislative competences lie with the federation, but the provinces have considerable weight in administration and through the **Conference of Provincial Governors**.

### Districts and municipalities

Austria has **94 districts** (administrative districts), which are administered by **district authorities** (_Bezirkshauptmannschaften_) or by the **statutory cities** themselves, and **2,092 municipalities** (as of 2026). The municipalities are **self-governing bodies** with their own sphere of competence (e.g. local planning, building procedures, municipal roads, water supply, kindergartens, compulsory school buildings) and a delegated sphere (tasks they carry out on behalf of the federation or province, such as registration and electoral matters).

The organs of a municipality are the directly elected **municipal council**, the **municipal executive board** (in cities the city council or city senate) and the **mayor**. In six provinces (Burgenland, Carinthia, Upper Austria, Salzburg, Tyrol, Vorarlberg) the mayor is directly elected by the people; elsewhere by the municipal council. Vienna is both a province and a municipality; its municipal council is also the provincial parliament, and its mayor is also the governor.

## Elections

### Electoral principles

According to Art. 26 B-VG, the National Council election is **universal**, **equal**, **direct**, **secret**, **personal** and **free** and follows the principle of **proportional representation**.

| Principle | Meaning |
| --------- | ------- |
| universal | All citizens aged 16 and over may vote, regardless of gender, income or education. |
| equal | Every vote counts the same. |
| direct | Voters elect the members of parliament directly, without electors. |
| secret | No one may find out how someone voted. |
| personal | You must vote yourself; proxy voting is not allowed. |
| free | The election takes place without coercion or undue influence. |

- **Right to vote** (active suffrage): Austrian citizenship and being at least **16 years old** on election day (since 2007, the first EU country to do so for nationwide elections). Austrians living abroad may also vote.
- **Right to stand for election** (passive suffrage): for the National Council from **18**, for the office of Federal President from 35.
- EU citizens resident in Austria may vote in **municipal council elections** and **European elections**, but not in National Council and provincial parliament elections.
- **Polling card and postal voting**: anyone who cannot vote at their own polling station on election day applies for a polling card (_Wahlkarte_). With it you can vote at any polling station or by **post** (since 2007).

### The electoral system

Austria has **proportional representation**: seats are distributed among the parties in proportion to their votes. The distribution takes place in three counting procedures:

1. **Regional constituencies** (39): seats according to the electoral quota (Hare method: valid votes in the province divided by the number of seats to be allocated there). A seat won here is called a **basic mandate** (_Grundmandat_).
2. **Provincial constituencies** (9): further seats according to the same quota, minus the basic mandates.
3. **Federal level**: the remaining seats according to the **D'Hondt method** on the basis of nationwide votes.

Only parties that have obtained at least **4 %** of valid votes nationwide or a basic mandate take part in the second and third procedures (**electoral threshold**). It is intended to prevent fragmentation of parliament.

**Preference votes**: in addition to the party, you can tick a candidate of the chosen party at each level. Candidates who receive enough preference votes move up the list: 14 % of their party's votes in the regional constituency, 10 % in the provincial constituency and 7 % at federal level. This strengthens the personal element within the list system.

The following overview describes the parties according to their self-presentation and their classification in political science (as of 2026). It is not an evaluation.

| Party | History | Programmatic focus |
| ----- | ------- | ------------------ |

| **ÖVP** | founded in 1945 as successor to the Christian Socials; organised in leagues (business, farmers', employees' league and others) | centre-right, Christian democratic and conservative; social market economy, relief for businesses, security, pro-European |

## Courts and oversight bodies

### Constitutional Court

The **Constitutional Court** (VfGH) in Vienna consists of a president, a vice-president, twelve other members and six substitute members. They are appointed by the Federal President on the proposal of the federal government (president, vice-president, six members), the National Council (three) and the Federal Council (three). Its tasks:

- **review of laws**: it repeals unconstitutional laws, and likewise unlawful ordinances.
- **protection of fundamental rights**: complaints against decisions of the administrative courts on grounds of violation of constitutionally guaranteed rights
- **electoral jurisdiction**: review of contested elections (e.g. annulment of the 2016 presidential run-off)
- **jurisdiction over competences**: disputes over competences between the federation, provinces and courts
- **state jurisdiction**: charges against supreme organs for violations of the law
- review of international treaties and disputes concerning committees of inquiry

There is also the **Supreme Administrative Court** (VwGH), since 2014 the **administrative courts** (Federal Administrative Court, Federal Finance Court, nine provincial administrative courts) as the first judicial instance against administrative decisions, and the ordinary courts with the **Supreme Court** (OGH) at the top.

### Court of Audit

The **Court of Audit** (_Rechnungshof_), as an organ of the National Council (and for provinces and municipalities of the provincial parliaments), audits the **financial management** of the federation, provinces, larger municipalities and companies with state participation for **economy, efficiency, effectiveness and legality**. Its reports are public. Its president is elected by the National Council for **twelve years**; re-election is not possible. Since 2012 it has also checked the parties' financial statements.

### Ombudsman Board

The **Ombudsman Board** (_Volksanwaltschaft_) checks the **public administration** for maladministration. Anyone who feels unfairly treated by an authority can contact it free of charge and without formalities. It consists of **three members**, elected by the National Council for six years; the three parties with the most seats each have the right to nominate one. Since 2012, as the **National Preventive Mechanism**, it has also inspected institutions where people are or may be deprived of their liberty (prisons, care homes, psychiatric units) to protect human rights. It can make recommendations but cannot overturn decisions.

## Direct democracy

The Austrian Federal Constitution provides three instruments of direct democracy:

| Instrument | Initiated by | Procedure | Binding? | Examples |
| ---------- | ------------ | --------- | -------- | -------- |
| **Citizens' initiative petition** (_Volksbegehren_) | citizens | To launch it, statements of support from 1 per mille of the population are needed; then a registration week (also online with ID Austria). With **100,000 signatures** (or one sixth of those eligible in each of three provinces), the National Council must deal with it. | The National Council must debate it but does not have to pass it. | Conference Centre petition 1982 (more than 1.3 million signatures, a record), genetic engineering petition 1997, women's petitions 1997 and 2018, "Don't smoke" 2018 |
| **Referendum** (_Volksabstimmung_) | National Council (majority) or mandatory | The people decide on a law already passed by the National Council. | **binding**. Mandatory for a total revision of the constitution; for partial amendments at the request of a third of the members of the National Council or the Federal Council | **Zwentendorf** 1978 (no), **EU accession** 1994 (yes) |
| **Consultative referendum** (_Volksbefragung_) | National Council on a motion by the government or members | The people are consulted in advance on a matter of fundamental importance. | not legally binding, but politically hard to ignore | **Conscription** 2013 (almost 60 % for retaining conscription and civilian service) |

There are also **petitions** and **parliamentary citizens' initiatives** (from 500 signatures) to the National Council, as well as direct-democratic instruments in provinces and municipalities.

## Interest groups

Besides the parties, **interest groups** influence politics.

- **Chambers**: statutory representative bodies with **compulsory membership** and self-government: the Economic Chamber, Chamber of Labour, Chamber of Agriculture, as well as chambers for the liberal professions (doctors, lawyers, notaries, pharmacists, civil engineers).
- **Voluntary associations**: the Austrian Trade Union Federation (ÖGB), the Federation of Austrian Industries (IV), environmental and social organisations.
- The large associations form the **social partnership** (see [Austria after 1945](/en/history-and-politics/post-war-austria/)). They contribute through consultations, collective agreements and the self-government of social insurance.
- **Lobbying** has been regulated since 2013 by the Lobbying and Interest Representation Transparency Act (lobbying register).

Compulsory membership of the chambers and the close links between associations and parties are viewed critically; stability and social peace are seen as positive.
