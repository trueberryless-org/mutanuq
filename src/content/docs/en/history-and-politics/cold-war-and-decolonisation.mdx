After 1945, two superpowers faced each other: the **USA** with a liberal-democratic, market-economy system and the **Soviet Union** with a communist one-party state and planned economy. Because both possessed nuclear weapons from 1949 and a direct war would have meant mutual destruction, they fought their conflict through an arms race, propaganda, economic aid, espionage and **proxy wars** in other countries. In 1947 the journalist Walter Lippmann popularised the term **Cold War** for this. At the same time, the colonies in Asia and Africa fought for their independence and often became theatres of the East-West conflict themselves.

## Bloc formation

The wartime alliance broke apart as early as 1945. At the conferences of **Yalta** and **Potsdam**, the victorious powers agreed on the division of Germany and Austria into occupation zones, the westward shift of Poland and the resettlement of Germans from East Central Europe (**expulsion**). In the states of East Central Europe occupied by the Red Army, communist parties took power with Soviet support by 1948. In 1946, Winston Churchill spoke of an **"iron curtain"** from Stettin to Trieste.

The USA responded with a policy of **containment**:

- **Truman Doctrine** (March 1947): support for "free peoples" against subjugation, initially Greece and Turkey.
- **Marshall Plan** (from 1948): economic aid for the reconstruction of Western Europe, to contain communism through prosperity. The Soviet Union forbade its satellite states to take part and in 1949 founded the **Council for Mutual Economic Assistance** (CMEA, Comecon).

| | Western bloc | Eastern bloc |
| - | ------------ | ------------ |
| Leading power | USA | Soviet Union |
| Political system | parliamentary democracy, multi-party system | communist one-party rule |
| Economy | market economy (social market economy in Western Europe) | central planned economy, state ownership |
| Military alliance | **NATO** (4 April 1949) | **Warsaw Pact** (14 May 1955) |
| Economic alliance | OEEC, EEC (see [The European Union](/en/history-and-politics/european-union/)) | Comecon |
| Ideology | freedom, individual rights | equality, class struggle, world revolution |

**NATO** was founded in 1949 by twelve states; the Federal Republic of Germany joined in 1955. Immediately afterwards, the Soviet Union founded the **Warsaw Pact** with seven states. It also served to control its own allies.

The **arms race** led to huge arsenals of nuclear weapons: the USA detonated the first atomic bomb in 1945, the Soviet Union in 1949; both developed hydrogen bombs and intercontinental missiles in the 1950s. The strategy of **deterrence** was based on the capacity for mutually assured destruction (MAD). With **Sputnik** (1957), the Soviet Union demonstrated its lead in rocket technology; the **space race** followed, which the USA won with the Moon landing in 1969.

## Berlin and the division of Germany

Berlin lay in the middle of the Soviet occupation zone but was divided into four sectors.

- **Berlin Blockade** (24 June 1948 to 12 May 1949): after the currency reform in the western zones, the Soviet Union blocked all land and water routes to West Berlin. The USA and Britain supplied the city for almost a year by an **airlift** with "raisin bombers". The blockade failed.
- In 1949 the **Federal Republic of Germany** (23 May, Basic Law) and the **German Democratic Republic** (7 October) were founded.
- On **17 June 1953**, hundreds of thousands of people in the GDR protested against increased work quotas and for free elections; Soviet tanks crushed the uprising.
- By 1961, around 2.7 million people had fled from the GDR to the West, many of them young and well educated. On **13 August 1961**, the GDR leadership had the **Berlin Wall** built. By 1989 at least 140 people had died at the Berlin Wall while trying to escape or as a result of the border regime.
- In 1963 US President Kennedy said in Berlin: "Ich bin ein Berliner."

## Hot wars in the Cold War

### Korea

In 1945, Korea had been divided along the 38th parallel into a Soviet and an American zone. In 1950, communist North Korea attacked the South. Under a UN mandate, mainly US troops fought for South Korea, and China intervened on the side of the North. The **Korean War** (1950 to 1953) ended with an armistice close to the old border; there is still no peace treaty today.

### Cuba

In 1959, **Fidel Castro** came to power in Cuba through a revolution and aligned himself with the Soviet Union. A CIA-organised invasion by Cuban exiles failed at the Bay of Pigs in 1961. In **October 1962**, US reconnaissance aircraft discovered Soviet missile sites in Cuba, only about 150 km from Florida. President **John F. Kennedy** imposed a naval blockade. For thirteen days the world stood on the brink of nuclear war. Finally **Nikita Khrushchev** withdrew the missiles; in return, the USA promised not to attack Cuba and later secretly withdrew its missiles from Turkey. Afterwards a "hotline" was set up between Washington and Moscow, and a test ban treaty was concluded in 1963.

### Vietnam

After the communist independence movement Viet Minh under **Ho Chi Minh** defeated France (Dien Bien Phu, 1954), Vietnam was divided. The USA supported the South to prevent communism from spreading in Asia (**domino theory**). From 1964/65 it intervened with ground troops (up to more than 500,000 soldiers) and massive bombing. The war against the North Vietnamese army and the Viet Cong guerrillas could not be won. Napalm, the defoliant Agent Orange, massacres such as **My Lai** (1968) and the television images triggered worldwide protests. The USA withdrew in 1973, and North Vietnam captured **Saigon** in 1975. The war claimed millions of lives, mostly Vietnamese. In neighbouring Cambodia, the Khmer Rouge murdered around 1.5 to 2 million people between 1975 and 1979.

## Decolonisation

The Second World War had weakened the colonial powers Britain, France and the Netherlands. The Atlantic Charter (1941) and the UN Charter (1945) proclaimed the **right of peoples to self-determination**; both superpowers rejected old-style colonialism. In the colonies, independence movements had formed, whose leaders had often studied in Europe.

| Year | Independence | Path |
| ---- | ------------ | ---- |
| 1947 | **India** and Pakistan | non-violent resistance under **Mahatma Gandhi** (Salt March 1930); partition led to mass violence with hundreds of thousands of dead and millions of refugees |
| 1949 | **Indonesia** | war against the Netherlands |
| 1954 | Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia | Indochina War against France |
| 1957 | **Ghana** | first independent colony south of the Sahara (Kwame Nkrumah) |
| 1960 | 17 African states, including Nigeria, Congo, Senegal | "Year of Africa" |
| 1962 | **Algeria** | war against France (1954 to 1962) with hundreds of thousands of dead |
| 1975 | Angola, Mozambique | after the Carnation Revolution in Portugal |
| 1990 | Namibia | from South Africa |

In 1955, representatives of 29 Asian and African states met in **Bandung** (Indonesia). In 1961, Yugoslavia (Tito), India (Nehru), Egypt (Nasser) and others founded the **Non-Aligned Movement** in Belgrade, which did not want to join either bloc. The term **"Third World"** originally referred to these states alongside the Western and Eastern blocs. Today people tend to speak of the **Global South**.

In **South Africa**, the white minority government established **apartheid** from 1948, a system of legal racial segregation. After decades of resistance and international sanctions, **Nelson Mandela** was released in 1990 after 27 years in prison and became president in 1994 after the first free elections.

### Neocolonialism and consequences

Formal independence often did not end dependence. In 1965, Kwame Nkrumah coined the term **neocolonialism**: former colonial powers and large corporations continued to influence the new states economically (exports of raw materials at fluctuating prices, foreign debt, unequal terms of trade) and politically (support for compliant governments, military interventions). Critics see similar patterns in today's investments and loans as well, for example within China's Belt and Road Initiative.

Other consequences of the colonial period:

- **Borders** drawn without regard to settlement areas led to civil wars and secession conflicts (Biafra 1967 to 1970, Sudan, Congo). In **Rwanda** in 1994, Hutu extremists committed genocide against around 800,000 Tutsi and moderate Hutu.
- Economies were geared to exporting a few raw materials (**monocultures**).
- Many states became authoritarian one-party states or military dictatorships, often supported by one of the superpowers.
- The debate about the **return** of looted cultural property (such as the Benin Bronzes) and about apologies and compensation is still ongoing.

## 1968

The year **1968** stands for a worldwide protest movement, mainly of young people. It was directed against the Vietnam War, authoritarian structures in universities, families and the state, the parents' generation's failure to come to terms with the Nazi past (in Germany and Austria), consumer society and racism.

- **USA**: civil rights movement; **Martin Luther King** was assassinated on 4 April 1968, and Robert F. Kennedy shortly afterwards.
- **France**: in **May 1968**, student protests and a general strike involving millions led to a state crisis.
- **Germany**: extra-parliamentary opposition, protests against the emergency laws, assassination attempt on Rudi Dutschke.
- **Austria**: the movement remained comparatively small (for example the action "Art and Revolution" at the University of Vienna, which the tabloid press denounced as obscene), but its social concerns fed into the reforms of the Kreisky era.
- **Czechoslovakia**: in the **Prague Spring**, the Communist Party under Alexander Dubček attempted "socialism with a human face" with press freedom and reforms. On **21 August 1968**, Warsaw Pact troops invaded and ended the experiment (**Brezhnev Doctrine**: limited sovereignty of socialist states).

Long-term effects: the women's movement, environmental movement, peace movement, liberalisation of education, sexuality and law, more co-determination.

## Détente

After the Cuban Missile Crisis, both sides looked for ways to reduce the risk of nuclear war (**détente**).

- **Arms control**: Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968), **SALT I** (1972, limitation of strategic weapons), ABM Treaty.
- **New Ostpolitik** of the Federal Republic under Willy Brandt ("change through rapprochement"): treaties with the Soviet Union and Poland (1970, Brandt's kneeling in Warsaw), **Basic Treaty** with the GDR (1972).
- **CSCE**: the **Helsinki Final Act** (1 August 1975) of the Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe, signed by 35 states including Austria, guaranteed the inviolability of borders and committed the signatories to respect human rights. Civil rights groups in the East such as **Charter 77** in Czechoslovakia invoked it.

At the end of the 1970s the conflict intensified again: the Soviet Union deployed new intermediate-range missiles (SS-20), NATO responded with the **Double-Track Decision** (1979: deployment of new missiles if negotiations failed), and the Soviet Union invaded **Afghanistan** (1979 to 1989). A large peace movement emerged in Western Europe.

## The end of communism 1989/91

### Causes

- **Economic crisis** of the Eastern bloc: planned economy without innovation, shortages, debts to the West, high arms costs
- **Mikhail Gorbachev** (from 1985) wanted to reform the system with **glasnost** (openness) and **perestroika** (restructuring) and abandoned the Brezhnev Doctrine: the states of East Central Europe were allowed to go their own way.
- **Civil movements**: **Solidarność** in Poland (1980, under Lech Wałęsa), Charter 77, church groups in the GDR
- The **Chernobyl** disaster (1986) shook confidence in the Soviet leadership.

### German reunification

After the first free Volkskammer election in the GDR (March 1990), the two German states negotiated unification. In the **Two Plus Four Treaty** (12 September 1990) with the four victorious powers, Germany obtained full sovereignty, finally recognised the Oder-Neisse border with Poland and remained in NATO. On **3 October 1990**, the GDR acceded to the Federal Republic.

### Dissolution of the Soviet Union

The Warsaw Pact dissolved on 1 July 1991. The Baltic states and other Soviet republics declared independence. A coup attempt by communist hardliners against Gorbachev in August 1991 failed because of resistance led by **Boris Yeltsin**. In December 1991, Russia, Ukraine and Belarus founded the **Commonwealth of Independent States** (CIS); Gorbachev resigned on 25 December, and on **26 December 1991** the Soviet Union ceased to exist. Fifteen independent states emerged from it.

The Cold War was over. In 1989 the political scientist Francis Fukuyama spoke of the "end of history", meaning the final victory of liberal democracy. Developments since then (see [Europe and the World Today](/en/history-and-politics/europe-and-the-world-today/)) have not confirmed this expectation.
