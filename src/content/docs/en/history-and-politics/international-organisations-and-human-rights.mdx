After two world wars, states created organisations to secure peace, promote cooperation and protect the rights of the individual, even against their own state. International organisations have no army of their own and there is no world police; their effectiveness depends on whether states, especially powerful ones, keep to their rules. Nevertheless, they have decisively shaped international law and human rights. Vienna is one of the four main seats of the United Nations and the seat of the OSCE, which underlines the importance of these organisations for Austria.

## Overview

| Organisation | Founded | Seat | Members | Purpose |
| ------------ | ------- | ---- | ------- | ------- |
| **United Nations** (UN) | 1945 | New York (further offices in Geneva, Vienna, Nairobi) | 193 states | peace, security, human rights, development |
| **OSCE** | 1975 as CSCE, OSCE since 1995 | Vienna | 57 participating states | security and cooperation in Europe, North America and Central Asia |
| **NATO** | 1949 | Brussels | 32 states | collective defence |
| **Council of Europe** | 1949 | Strasbourg | 46 states | human rights, democracy, rule of law |
| **European Union** | 1993 (predecessors from 1951) | Brussels | 27 states | economic and political integration (see [The European Union](/en/history-and-politics/european-union/)) |

## The United Nations

The **League of Nations** (1920 to 1946) had failed to prevent the Second World War: the USA never joined, Germany, Japan and Italy left, and it had no means of stopping aggressors. The **Charter of the United Nations** was signed in San Francisco by 50 states on **26 June 1945** and came into force on **24 October 1945** (today **United Nations Day**). Austria has been a member since **14 December 1955**.

### Purposes and principles

- maintaining world peace and international security
- friendly relations based on equal rights and the **self-determination of peoples**
- international cooperation on economic, social, cultural and humanitarian problems and promotion of human rights
- **sovereign equality** of all members
- **prohibition of the use of force** (Art. 2(4)): the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state is prohibited. The only exceptions are **self-defence** against an armed attack (Art. 51) and measures decided by the **Security Council**.
- non-interference in internal affairs

### The main organs

| Organ | Composition | Tasks |
| ----- | ----------- | ----- |
| **General Assembly** | all 193 states, one vote each | discusses all matters covered by the Charter, adopts the budget, elects non-permanent Security Council members; its resolutions are **recommendations**, not binding |
| **Security Council** | **15 members**: 5 permanent members with a **veto** (USA, Russia, China, France, United Kingdom) and 10 non-permanent members elected for two years | primary responsibility for peace and security; can take **binding** decisions: sanctions (Chapter VII), peacekeeping missions, military enforcement measures |
| **Secretariat** | Secretary-General (since 2017 António Guterres) and staff | administration, mediation in conflicts |
| **International Court of Justice** (ICJ) | 15 judges, The Hague | decides disputes between **states**, gives advisory opinions |
| **Economic and Social Council** (ECOSOC) | 54 states | coordinates economic and social work, specialised agencies and cooperation with NGOs |
| Trusteeship Council | suspended since 1994 | administered former colonial territories until independence |

A Security Council decision on substantive matters requires **nine votes** and must not be opposed by any permanent member. Because of the veto, the Security Council is often blocked in conflicts in which a veto power is involved, in the Cold War as in the Russian war against Ukraine. Reform (more permanent seats for Africa, Latin America, India, Germany, Japan, restriction of the veto) has been negotiated for decades. Austria was a non-permanent member in 1973/74, 1991/92 and 2009/10. The Austrian **Kurt Waldheim** was UN Secretary-General from 1972 to 1981.

### Specialised agencies and programmes

The UN system includes numerous specialised agencies and programmes, such as the World Health Organization (**WHO**), UNESCO (education, science, culture, World Heritage), the children's fund **UNICEF**, the refugee agency **UNHCR**, the World Food Programme (WFP), the International Labour Organization (ILO), the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund (IMF).

In **Vienna** (Vienna International Centre, "UN City", opened 1979), the **International Atomic Energy Agency** (IAEA), the Industrial Development Organization (**UNIDO**), the Office on Drugs and Crime (**UNODC**), the Office for Outer Space Affairs (UNOOSA) and the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty Organization (CTBTO) are among those based there.

### Peacekeeping

The UN deploys **peacekeeping forces** (blue helmets), usually after a ceasefire and with the consent of the parties to the conflict. Austria has taken part in UN missions since 1960; more than 100,000 Austrian soldiers and civilians have since served in international missions, for example in Cyprus, on the Golan Heights, in Lebanon and in Kosovo. The concept of the **Responsibility to Protect** (2005) states that the international community may intervene if a state fails to protect its population from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity; however, it remains tied to the Security Council and is disputed.

The **2030 Agenda** with 17 **Sustainable Development Goals** (SDGs), adopted by all member states in 2015, sets goals such as ending poverty and hunger, quality education, gender equality and climate action.

## The OSCE

The **Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe** emerged from the **CSCE**, whose Helsinki Final Act of 1975 was an important step in détente (see [The Cold War and Decolonisation](/en/history-and-politics/cold-war-and-decolonisation/)). It has been called the OSCE since 1 January 1995, and its **Secretariat** is in **Vienna**. With 57 participating states from Europe, North America and Central Asia, it is the world's largest regional security organisation.

It follows a **comprehensive concept of security** with three dimensions: politico-military (arms control, confidence-building measures), economic and environmental, and human (human rights, democracy, media freedom). It is known for **election observation** (by the Office for Democratic Institutions and Human Rights, ODIHR, in Warsaw) and monitoring missions such as the Special Monitoring Mission to Ukraine (2014 to 2022). Decisions require the **consensus** of all participating states, which has severely paralysed the organisation since the Russian attack on Ukraine.

## NATO

The **North Atlantic Treaty Organization** was founded on **4 April 1949** as a defensive alliance of Western states against the Soviet Union. At its core is **Article 5** of the North Atlantic Treaty: an armed attack against one member is regarded as an attack against all (**collective defence**); each member provides assistance with the measures it deems necessary. Article 5 has been invoked only once so far, after the attacks of 11 September 2001.

After 1990, NATO enlarged eastwards (1999 Poland, Czechia, Hungary; 2004 seven further states including the Baltic states; later further Balkan states). After the Russian attack on Ukraine, **Finland** (2023) and **Sweden** (2024) joined; since then NATO has had **32 members**. In 2014 the members agreed to spend at least 2 % of GDP on defence in the medium term; in 2025 a new target of 5 % by 2035 was adopted (of which 3.5 % for core defence).

As a neutral state, Austria is **not a NATO member**, but it has taken part in the **Partnership for Peace** since 1995, for example in the Kosovo Force (KFOR).

## Austria's security policy and neutrality

**Permanent neutrality** (1955) is essentially military: no membership of military alliances, no foreign military bases, no participation in wars (see [Austria after 1945](/en/history-and-politics/post-war-austria/)). Since EU accession it has been gradually restricted: Austria takes part in the EU's **Common Security and Defence Policy**, in EU sanctions and in crisis management missions. Under international law it now applies only to a limited extent towards EU partners, but politically it remains very popular with the population.

The constitution provides for **comprehensive national defence** (Art. 9a B-VG) with four parts: **military** (the armed forces with general conscription for men), **intellectual** (awareness of the defence of democracy and freedom, part of political education), **civil** (disaster protection, civil protection) and **economic** (crisis preparedness, stockpiling). Anyone who refuses military service for reasons of conscience does **civilian service**. Newer security concepts also take into account threats from cyber attacks, disinformation, terrorism, pandemics, blackouts and climate change (**comprehensive security provision**). In 2023 Austria joined the European air defence initiative **Sky Shield**, with a neutrality reservation.

## The Council of Europe and the ECHR

The **Council of Europe** was founded in 1949 and has its seat in **Strasbourg**. Austria joined in 1956. Russia was expelled in March 2022 after the attack on Ukraine; since then the Council of Europe has had 46 member states. It is not an EU institution.

Its most important achievement is the **European Convention on Human Rights** (ECHR), signed in Rome on **4 November 1950** and in force since 1953. It protects, among other things, the right to life, the prohibition of torture, slavery and forced labour, the right to liberty and a fair trial, the right to respect for private and family life, freedom of thought, conscience and religion, freedom of expression, assembly and association, and the prohibition of discrimination. An additional protocol prohibits the death penalty.

Anyone who feels that their rights under the ECHR have been violated in a member state can, after exhausting domestic remedies, lodge an **individual application** with the **European Court of Human Rights** (ECtHR) in Strasbourg. Its judgments are binding on the states concerned. In Austria, the ECHR has had **constitutional status** since 1964; its rights can therefore be enforced directly before the Constitutional Court.

## Human rights

**Human rights** are rights to which every person is entitled simply by being human. They are:

- **universal**: they apply to all people everywhere.
- **inalienable**: they can neither be lost nor given up.
- **indivisible**: civil, political, economic, social and cultural rights depend on each other and are of equal rank.

A distinction is made between human rights (for everyone) and **civil rights** (only for citizens, such as the right to vote in National Council elections). When human rights are guaranteed in a constitution, they are called **fundamental rights**.

### History

Forerunners are the Magna Carta (1215), the English Bill of Rights (1689), the Virginia Declaration of Rights and the US Declaration of Independence (1776) and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789, see [The Age of Revolutions](/en/history-and-politics/age-of-revolutions/)). For a long time, however, these rights did not apply to everyone, for example not to women, slaves or colonised peoples.

Under the impression of Nazi crimes, the UN General Assembly adopted the **Universal Declaration of Human Rights** (UDHR) on **10 December 1948**. The commission that drafted it was chaired by **Eleanor Roosevelt**. The UDHR has 30 articles. Article 1 reads: "All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights." As a declaration of the General Assembly, it is not legally binding, but large parts of it are regarded as customary international law. 10 December is **Human Rights Day**.

The rights became binding through treaties, above all the two **UN human rights covenants** of 1966 (in force 1976): the International Covenant on **Civil and Political Rights** and the International Covenant on **Economic, Social and Cultural Rights**. Together with the UDHR they form the **International Bill of Human Rights**. In addition there are conventions against racial discrimination (1965), discrimination against women (1979), torture (1984), on the rights of the child (1989) and of persons with disabilities (2006). The **UN Human Rights Council** in Geneva regularly reviews all states.

The **International Criminal Court** (ICC) in The Hague, established by the Rome Statute (1998, in force 2002), prosecutes **individuals** for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and aggression. Important states such as the USA, Russia, China and Israel are not parties to it.

### Generations of human rights

In 1977, the lawyer Karel Vašák divided human rights into three "generations" following the values of the French Revolution. The model describes their historical development but does not imply a ranking.

| Generation | Value | Type of rights | Examples |
| ---------- | ----- | -------------- | -------- |
| **1st generation** | liberty | **civil and political rights**: rights of defence against the state and rights of participation | right to life, prohibition of torture, freedom of opinion, religion and assembly, fair trial, right to vote |
| **2nd generation** | equality | **economic, social and cultural rights**: rights to benefits and participation that the state must actively secure | right to work, fair working conditions, social security, education, health, housing |
| **3rd generation** | fraternity (solidarity) | **collective rights** of peoples and groups | right to peace, development, a healthy environment, self-determination |

Today there is debate about new rights in the digital sphere, such as data protection as a fundamental right (expressly guaranteed in the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights, Art. 8) and a right to internet access.

### Human rights under criticism

- **Universality or relativism?** Some governments argue that human rights are a "Western" concept and must be adapted to "Asian" or religious values. Critics respond that this argument is mostly used by authoritarian governments against their own populations and that the UDHR was shaped by states from all continents.
- **Double standards**: democratic states also violate human rights or criticise violations only by opponents, not by allies.
- **Enforcement**: there is no authority that can enforce human rights worldwide. Reports by NGOs such as **Amnesty International** and **Human Rights Watch** and public attention are therefore important.

## Children's rights

The **UN Convention on the Rights of the Child** was adopted on **20 November 1989** and is the most widely ratified human rights treaty in the world (all UN states except the USA). It applies to everyone under 18. Austria ratified it in 1992. Its four general principles:

1. **Non-discrimination** (Art. 2)
2. **Best interests of the child** as a primary consideration in all decisions affecting children (Art. 3)
3. **Right to life and development** (Art. 6)
4. **Respect for the views of the child** (participation, Art. 12)

The rights can be divided into **protection rights** (from violence, exploitation, child labour, abuse), **provision rights** (education, health, leisure and play, social security) and **participation rights** (freedom of expression, information, assembly).

In Austria, central parts were given constitutional status in 2011 in the **Federal Constitutional Law on the Rights of Children**. Since 1989 Austria has had an explicit **ban on violence in child-rearing**, making it one of the first countries in the world to do so. Every province has a **Children and Youth Ombudsman's Office** as a point of contact. Also important for young people are the provinces' youth protection laws (largely harmonised since 2019) and the right to have a say at school through **pupil representation**.
