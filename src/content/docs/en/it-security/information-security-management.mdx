Technical measures alone do not make an organisation secure. It takes responsibilities, rules, budgets, training and regular checks of whether all of this works. This organisational side of security is called **information security management**. It is increasingly required by law, for example by NIS2 and the GDPR.

## ISMS

An **information security management system** (ISMS) is the totality of policies, processes, responsibilities and resources with which an organisation systematically plans, implements, monitors and improves its information security. An ISMS is not software but a permanent management process.

Overall responsibility lies with **top management**. It sets the goals, provides resources and bears the remaining risk. Operational control is the job of an **information security officer** (ISO, internationally often _chief information security officer_, CISO), who reports directly to management and should not be subordinate to the head of IT, to avoid conflicts of interest.

An ISMS follows the **PDCA cycle** (Deming cycle), which is also known from quality management:

| Phase     | Content                                                                              |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Plan**  | define the scope, write the policy, analyse risks, plan measures                     |
| **Do**    | implement measures, train employees, start operation                                 |
| **Check** | measure effectiveness: metrics, internal audits, management review                   |
| **Act**   | correct deviations, improve the ISMS, start a new cycle                              |

## ISO/IEC 27001 and 27002

The **ISO/IEC 27000** series is the international standard for information security. The most important parts:

| Standard             | Content                                                                        |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **ISO/IEC 27000**    | overview and vocabulary                                                        |
| **ISO/IEC 27001**    | requirements for an ISMS, **certifiable**                                      |
| **ISO/IEC 27002**    | guidance on implementing the controls from Annex A                             |
| **ISO/IEC 27005**    | information security risk management                                           |
| **ISO/IEC 27701**    | extension for privacy information management                                  |

**ISO/IEC 27001** describes in clauses 4 to 10 what an ISMS must achieve: context of the organisation (scope, interested parties), leadership, planning (risk assessment and risk treatment), support (resources, competence, awareness, documentation), operation, performance evaluation (monitoring, internal audit, management review) and improvement.

**Annex A** lists controls that the organisation selects on the basis of its risk assessment. In the current version **ISO/IEC 27001:2022** there are **93 controls** in four themes (previously 114 controls in 14 chapters):

| Theme                       | Number | Examples                                                                       |
| --------------------------- | ------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **5 Organisational**        | 37     | policies, roles, inventory of assets, access control, supplier relationships, incident management, cloud services |
| **6 People**                | 8      | screening before employment, training and awareness, confidentiality agreements, remote working |
| **7 Physical**              | 14     | security perimeters, physical entry control, clear desk, protection against environmental threats, disposal of storage media |
| **8 Technological**         | 34     | endpoint devices, privileged access rights, protection against malware, backup, logging, network security, cryptography, secure development |

Eleven controls are new in the 2022 version, including threat intelligence, information security for the use of cloud services, configuration management, information deletion, data masking, data leakage prevention, web filtering and secure coding.

In the **Statement of Applicability** (SoA) the organisation justifies for each of the 93 controls whether it is implemented or why not. **Certification** is carried out by an accredited certification body in a two-stage audit (document review, then on-site review of implementation). The certificate is valid for three years, with annual surveillance audits in between. Certificates according to the old 2013 version have been invalid since 31 October 2025.

## BSI IT-Grundschutz

The German **Federal Office for Information Security** (BSI) has developed a very concrete, freely available method with **IT-Grundschutz** ("IT baseline protection"), which is also widely used in Austria. It is compatible with ISO 27001; certification as "ISO 27001 on the basis of IT-Grundschutz" is possible.

| Document                | Content                                                                           |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **BSI Standard 200-1**  | requirements for an ISMS                                                          |
| **BSI Standard 200-2**  | IT-Grundschutz methodology with three approaches: **basic protection** (quick start for all areas), **standard protection** (complete, the normal case) and **core protection** (the most important assets, the "crown jewels", first) |
| **BSI Standard 200-3**  | risk analysis for objects with high or very high protection requirements          |
| **BSI Standard 200-4**  | business continuity management                                                    |
| **IT-Grundschutz Compendium** | **modules** with concrete requirements, divided into process modules (such as ISMS, ORP organisation and personnel, CON concepts, OPS operations, DER detection and response) and system modules (APP applications, SYS IT systems, IND industrial IT, NET networks, INF infrastructure) |

The procedure for standard protection: delimit the information domain, structure analysis (which business processes, applications, systems and rooms exist), **determination of protection requirements** (normal, high, very high), **modelling** (assign suitable modules), IT-Grundschutz check (target/actual comparison), risk analysis for objects with increased protection requirements, implementation. The requirements of each module are graded into **basic**, **standard** and requirements for **increased protection needs**. The foundation is the catalogue of **elementary threats**, typical threats ranging from fire to malware to misconduct by people.

The latest edition of the Compendium is the 2023 edition. The BSI is currently fundamentally revising IT-Grundschutz into its successor **Grundschutz++**, which provides requirements in machine-readable form and with a stronger process orientation. In Austria there is additionally the **Austrian Information Security Handbook** (Österreichisches Informationssicherheitshandbuch) of the Federal Chancellery.

## Risk management

Risk management is the heart of every ISMS. According to ISO/IEC 27005 (based on ISO 31000) it runs as a recurring process:

1. **Establish the context**: scope, criteria for risk evaluation and for risk acceptance.
2. **Identify risks**: assets, threats, vulnerabilities, existing controls.
3. **Analyse risks**: estimate likelihood and consequences.
4. **Evaluate risks**: compare with the acceptance criteria and prioritise.
5. **Treat risks**: avoid, reduce, transfer or accept, recorded in a risk treatment plan.
6. **Accept the residual risk** by top management.
7. Alongside: **communicate** and **monitor and review**, because threats and systems change continuously.

How risks are assessed in concrete terms (risk matrix, protection requirements, treatment strategies) is described under [Security Basics](/en/it-security/security-basics/#risk-assessment).

## Policies and awareness

The rules of an ISMS are recorded in documents with different degrees of bindingness and detail:

- The **information security policy** is the fundamental document: importance of security, goals, scope, responsibilities. It is signed by top management and communicated to all employees.
- **Topic-specific policies** regulate individual topics, such as passwords and authentication, use of IT and the internet (_acceptable use policy_), private devices (_bring your own device_), working from home, classification of information, backup, clear desk.
- **Procedures and work instructions** describe concrete processes, for example how user accounts are created.

An **information classification** divides information according to its protection requirements, typically into "public", "internal", "confidential" and "strictly confidential". For each level it is defined how information is labelled, stored, transmitted and destroyed.

Because many attacks target people, **security awareness** is a mandatory measure. Effective approaches are regular, short and practical training sessions instead of one compulsory annual lecture, simulated phishing emails with immediate feedback, a simple way to report suspicious messages, and a culture in which someone who has clicked on a link reports it immediately instead of keeping quiet out of fear.

## NIS2 and NISG 2026

The EU directive **NIS2** (Directive (EU) 2022/2555) on measures for a high common level of cybersecurity replaced the first NIS directive in 2023. It greatly extends the range of obliged organisations and should have been transposed into national law by 17 October 2024. In Austria a first draft (NISG 2024) failed in the Nationalrat in July 2024 because it did not achieve the required two-thirds majority. The **Network and Information Systems Security Act 2026** (NISG 2026, BGBl. I Nr. 94/2025) was passed in December 2025 and entered into force on **1 October 2026**. It replaces the NISG 2018. The competent authority is the newly created **Federal Office for Cyber Security** (Bundesamt für Cybersicherheit) in the Federal Ministry of the Interior.

**Who is affected?** Companies and organisations in 18 sectors of high criticality (such as energy, transport, banking, health, drinking water, digital infrastructure, public administration) and other critical sectors (such as postal services, waste management, chemicals, food, manufacturing, digital services), generally from medium company size upwards (at least 50 employees, or more than 10 million euros in annual turnover and balance sheet total). They are classified as **essential** or **important entities**. Essential entities are subject to proactive supervision, important ones to supervision when there is cause. Estimates put the number of affected entities in Austria at around 4000.

**Obligations:**

- **Registration** with the authority.
- **Risk management measures** according to the state of the art, including policies on risk analysis and security, incident handling, business continuity and backup, supply chain security, secure acquisition and development, assessment of effectiveness, cyber hygiene and training, cryptography, human resources security and access control, and multi-factor authentication.
- **Reporting obligations** for significant incidents: early warning within 24 hours, notification within 72 hours, final report within one month.
- **Responsibility of management bodies**: management must approve the measures, oversee their implementation and undergo training itself. It can be held personally liable for breaches.

The directive provides for fines of up to 10 million euros or 2 % of worldwide annual turnover for essential entities and up to 7 million euros or 1.4 % for important entities, whichever amount is higher. In addition, the **Critical Entities Resilience Directive** (CER directive) applies to critical entities and concerns their physical protection.

## GDPR

The **General Data Protection Regulation** (GDPR), supplemented in Austria by the Data Protection Act (DSG), protects personal data. It explicitly requires information security. Its principles and the rights of data subjects are described under [Internet Law](/en/economy_and_law/law/internet_law/#data-protection). Particularly important for IT security are:

| Article        | Content                                                                                             |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Art. 5(1)(f)** | principle of **integrity and confidentiality**: protection against unauthorised processing, loss and destruction |
| **Art. 25**    | **data protection by design and by default**                                                        |
| **Art. 32**    | **security of processing**: appropriate **technical and organisational measures** (TOMs) according to the state of the art and the risk, such as pseudonymisation and encryption, ensuring confidentiality, integrity, availability and resilience, timely restoration after an incident and regular testing of effectiveness |
| **Art. 33, 34** | notification of personal data breaches to the data protection authority within 72 hours, and to the data subjects if there is a high risk |
| **Art. 35**    | **data protection impact assessment** for processing likely to result in a high risk                |

Breaches can be punished with fines of up to 20 million euros or 4 % of worldwide annual turnover. The competent supervisory authority in Austria is the **Datenschutzbehörde** (data protection authority).

## Penetration testing and the law

### Penetration tests

A **penetration test** (pentest) is an authorised, simulated attack to find vulnerabilities before real attackers exploit them. It differs from an automated **vulnerability scan**, which only lists known flaws, in that experts verify, exploit and combine flaws manually. **Red teaming** goes even further and tests over weeks, realistically and as unnoticed as possible, whether the organisation detects and responds to an attack.

| Type           | Testers' prior knowledge                                           |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Black box**  | none, like an external attacker                                    |
| **Grey box**   | partial, for example a normal user account                         |
| **White box**  | complete, with source code, documentation and architecture         |

The BSI divides a pentest into five phases: **preparation** (goals, scope, legal safeguards), **information gathering**, **assessment** of the information and risks, **active intrusion attempts** and **final analysis** with a report. Other widely used guides are the OWASP Web Security Testing Guide and the Penetration Testing Execution Standard (PTES). Typical tools are nmap, Burp Suite, Metasploit and the Linux distribution Kali Linux.

A mandatory prerequisite is a **written engagement** by someone who has authority over the systems: with the exact scope (which systems, address ranges, applications), time frame, permitted methods (for example whether social engineering or DoS tests are allowed), emergency contacts and rules on the confidentiality of the results (_rules of engagement_). With cloud or hosting services the provider's rules must also be observed. Without permission, the same test is a criminal offence.

### Ethical hacking and bug bounty

**Ethical hacking** means attacks with permission and with the goal of improving security. If someone discovers a flaw by chance, **coordinated vulnerability disclosure** applies: the flaw is reported confidentially to the vendor or operator (often via a `security.txt` file on the website or via CERT.at as an intermediary), who gets time for a patch before the details are published, typically about 90 days.

In **bug bounty programmes**, companies explicitly invite security researchers to test their systems within defined rules and pay a reward for reported flaws, depending on severity. Platforms such as HackerOne, Bugcrowd or Intigriti broker such programmes. The programme rules act as permission, but only for the described scope.

There are legal environments for practising: deliberately vulnerable applications such as OWASP Juice Shop and DVWA, platforms such as Hack The Box and TryHackMe, and **capture-the-flag competitions** (CTF), in Austria for example the **Austria Cyber Security Challenge** for pupils and students.

### Criminal law in Austria

The Austrian Criminal Code (Strafgesetzbuch, StGB) contains several offences relating to computer crime, some of which go back to the Council of Europe Convention on Cybercrime (Budapest Convention):

| Section         | Offence                                                 | Content                                                                        |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **§ 118a**      | Unlawful access to a computer system                    | gaining access to someone else's computer system by **overcoming a specific security measure**, with the intention of obtaining knowledge of protected personal data or causing someone a disadvantage |
| **§ 119**       | Violation of the secrecy of telecommunications          | reading messages with interception devices                                     |
| **§ 119a**      | Improper interception of data                           | intercepting data from a transmission, for example with a sniffer             |
| **§ 126a**      | Damage to data                                          | altering, deleting or rendering unusable someone else's data, for example by ransomware |
| **§ 126b**      | Disruption of the functioning of a computer system      | seriously disrupting a computer system by entering or transmitting data, for example by DoS |
| **§ 126c**      | Misuse of computer programs or access data              | producing, distributing or possessing malware or access data with the intention of committing one of the offences listed |
| **§ 148a**      | Fraudulent misuse of data processing                    | financial loss through manipulation of data processing                         |
| **§ 225a**      | Data forgery                                            | creating false data or falsifying genuine data in order to use it in legal transactions |

Since the 2023 amendment, the basic offence of § 118a carries a prison sentence of up to two years, and up to three years for systems of **critical infrastructure**. It is an offence prosecuted only with authorisation (**Ermächtigungsdelikt**): the offender is prosecuted only if the injured party consents. Note that intrusion alone can be punishable, even if nothing is damaged. "I only wanted to show that it is insecure" is no defence. Under § 126c, merely possessing tools such as nmap or Metasploit is not punishable: what matters is the intention to commit an offence with them. A commissioned pentest does not fulfil the elements of these offences, because the authorised party has given permission. In addition, civil claims for damages and breaches of the GDPR may arise.
