Europe is the second smallest continent, but one of the most densely populated and economically most closely interconnected. Geographically it is really a highly indented peninsula of Asia. Its diversity in a small space (climate, landscape, languages, states) explains much of its history and of today's differences between its regions. The institutions and history of the European Union are covered in the section [History and Civic Education](/en/history-and-politics/european-union/).

## Physical geography

### Boundaries and size

The boundary with Asia is a convention: it is usually drawn along the **Ural Mountains**, the Ural River, the Caspian Sea, the **Caucasus** and across the Black Sea and the Bosporus. Europe therefore covers about 10 million km². In the west, north and south it is bounded by the Atlantic, the Arctic Ocean and the Mediterranean.

### Structure and relief

A characteristic feature is the strong **horizontal articulation**: peninsulas (Scandinavian, Iberian, Apennine, Balkan peninsulas), islands (British Isles, Iceland) and marginal seas (North Sea and Baltic Sea, Mediterranean, Black Sea) bring almost all regions relatively close to the sea. This favoured trade and seafaring early on.

| Landform | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Lowlands** | North German and Polish lowlands as part of the North European Plain, East European Plain, Paris Basin, Pannonian Plain, Po Valley |
| **Uplands** (old, eroded) | Massif Central, Ardennes, Black Forest, Bohemian Massif |
| **High mountains** (young fold mountains) | Alps (Mont Blanc, about 4805 m), Pyrenees, Carpathians, Apennines, Dinarides, Caucasus (Elbrus, 5642 m, if the Caucasus is counted as part of Europe) |
| **Scandinavian Mountains** | old mountains, reshaped during the ice ages (fjords) |

The longest river is the **Volga** (about 3530 km). The **Danube** (about 2850 km) flows through or along ten states, more than any other river on Earth, and is connected to the Rhine via the Main-Danube Canal. The **Rhine** is Europe's busiest inland waterway; at its mouth lies Rotterdam, Europe's largest seaport.

### Climate

Europe lies mainly in the **westerlies** of the temperate latitudes. The **Gulf Stream** and its continuation, the North Atlantic Current, bring warm water to the north-west. That is why Western Europe is much milder than Canada at the same latitude; ports in northern Norway stay ice-free.

- **West**: maritime climate, mild winters, cool summers, precipitation all year
- **East**: increasing **continentality**: cold winters, hot summers, less precipitation
- **South**: Mediterranean climate with hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters
- **North**: boreal climate and tundra, mountain climate in the high mountains

## Major regions

Europe can be divided into major regions by location, culture and history. The boundaries are not clear-cut.

| Region | States (examples) |
| --- | --- |
| **Northern Europe** | Norway, Sweden, Finland, Denmark, Iceland, the Baltic states |
| **Western Europe** | United Kingdom, Ireland, France, Benelux states |
| **Central Europe** | Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Poland, Czechia, Slovakia, Hungary, Slovenia |
| **Southern Europe** | Portugal, Spain, Italy, Malta, Greece, Cyprus |
| **South-Eastern Europe** | Croatia, Serbia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro, Albania, North Macedonia, Bulgaria, Romania |
| **Eastern Europe** | Ukraine, Belarus, Moldova, European part of Russia |

## Economic areas and alliances

| Alliance | Members | Content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **European Union** (EU) | 27 states, about 450 million inhabitants (2025) | single market with the **four freedoms** (free movement of goods, persons, services and capital), common trade, agricultural, regional and environmental policy |
| **Euro area** | 21 EU states (since Bulgaria joined on 1 January 2026) | common currency, monetary policy of the European Central Bank (ECB) in Frankfurt |
| **Schengen area** | 29 states (25 EU states plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway, Switzerland) | no checks on persons at internal borders; Ireland and Cyprus do not fully participate |
| **European Economic Area** (EEA) | EU plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway | participation in the single market without EU membership |
| **EFTA** | Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway, Switzerland | free trade association; Switzerland is linked to the EU through bilateral agreements |

The United Kingdom left the EU in 2020 (**Brexit**). Several states of South-Eastern Europe as well as Ukraine, Moldova and Georgia are candidates for accession.

### Economic core regions

Economic output is unevenly distributed in Europe. In 1989 the French geographer Roger Brunet described the **Blue Banana**, a banana-shaped corridor of concentration from southern England through the Benelux states and the Rhine valley to northern Italy. Population, industry, financial centres and transport axes are concentrated here. A similar concept is the **Pentagon** between London, Paris, Milan, Munich and Hamburg. In addition, further growth regions have emerged, such as the "Sunbelt" along the northern Mediterranean coast (Barcelona, southern France) and metropolitan regions in Central and Eastern Europe (Warsaw, Prague, Budapest, Bratislava, Vienna).

## Regional disparities

Differences in economic strength are large within the EU. In 2023, according to Eurostat, GDP per capita in **purchasing power standards** (PPS) ranged from **27.6 %** of the EU average in the French overseas region of **Mayotte** to **244.7 %** in the Irish region of **Eastern and Midland**. Very high values in Luxembourg, Dublin, Prague or Brussels arise partly from commuters (who generate value added there but live elsewhere) and from the headquarters of multinational companies.

Disparities exist at several levels:

- **West-East divide**: the states of Central and Eastern Europe have been catching up strongly since joining the EU in 2004 and 2007 (for example Poland, Czechia, the Baltic states), but most are still below average.
- **North-South divide**: southern Italy (the Mezzogiorno) and parts of Greece, Spain and Portugal have lower economic strength and high youth unemployment.
- **Core-periphery**: capital regions grow faster than the rest of their country almost everywhere. Rural, peripheral regions lose inhabitants as young people move away.
- **Brain drain**: well-educated people migrate from Eastern and Southern Europe to the west and north.

## EU regional policy

**Cohesion policy** is intended to strengthen economic, social and territorial cohesion, that is to reduce the differences between regions. About **EUR 392 billion** is available for the period **2021 to 2027**, almost a third of the EU budget. With national co-financing it amounts to about half a trillion euros.

It is based on the **NUTS classification** (_Nomenclature des unités territoriales statistiques_), a uniform division of the EU into regions at three levels. In Austria the federal provinces are the NUTS 2 regions. According to GDP per capita in PPS, the NUTS 2 regions are classified as:

| Category | GDP per capita (PPS) | Funding intensity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **less developed regions** | below 75 % of the EU average | highest |
| **transition regions** | 75 % to 100 % | medium |
| **more developed regions** | above 100 % | lowest |

The most important funds are:

- **ERDF** (European Regional Development Fund): investment in innovation, digitalisation, climate protection, businesses
- **Cohesion Fund**: transport and environmental infrastructure in states with a gross national income per capita below 90 % of the EU average
- **ESF+** (European Social Fund Plus): employment, education, social inclusion
- **Just Transition Fund**: support for regions affected by the phase-out of coal and other CO₂-intensive industries
- **Interreg**: cross-border cooperation, for example between Austria and its neighbouring countries in **Euregios**

Its effect is disputed: many supported regions have caught up, such as Ireland in the 1990s or Poland. Critics, however, point to bureaucracy, deadweight effects, fraud and the fact that funds are not always used sensibly where administrations are weak. Alongside cohesion policy, the Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) is the EU's second large block of expenditure.
