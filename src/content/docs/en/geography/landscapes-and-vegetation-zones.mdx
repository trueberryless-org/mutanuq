Climate, rock, soil, water and vegetation influence each other. Together these **geofactors** form landscape zones that wrap around the Earth like belts and recur in the mountains as altitudinal zones. Understanding these relationships explains where agriculture is possible, why rainforest soils quickly become infertile after clearing and how avalanches and debris flows arise.

## Vegetation zones of the Earth

Natural vegetation is determined mainly by **temperature** (length of the growing season) and **water supply**. The zones therefore largely follow the climate zones (see [Climate and Weather](/en/geography/climate-and-weather/#climate-zones-and-the-köppen-classification)).

| Vegetation zone | Climate | Characteristics | Distribution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tropical rainforest** | always wet, warm all year | layered structure, the greatest biodiversity on Earth, fast nutrient cycle | Amazonia, Congo Basin, South-East Asia |
| **Savanna** | seasonally wet with rainy and dry seasons | grassland with trees; the longer the dry season, the sparser (moist, dry, thorn savanna) | Africa, Brazil, northern Australia |
| **Desert and semi-desert** | arid | sparse, adapted plants (succulents, deep roots) | Sahara, Arabia, Atacama, Central Asia |
| **Sclerophyllous vegetation** | dry summers, winter rain (Mediterranean climate) | evergreen plants with hard, small leaves against evaporation (holm oak, olive); often degraded to maquis | Mediterranean, California, Cape region |
| **Temperate deciduous and mixed forest** | temperate, humid | leaves shed in winter; beech, oak, maple | Central and Western Europe, eastern USA |
| **Steppe** | temperate, continental, semi-arid | treeless grassland, fertile soils | Ukraine, Kazakhstan, North American prairies, Pannonian Plain (remnants in the Seewinkel) |
| **Boreal coniferous forest** (taiga) | long, cold winters, short summers | spruce, pine, larch; the largest forest zone on Earth | Scandinavia, Siberia, Canada |
| **Tundra** | polar, warmest month below 10 °C | mosses, lichens, dwarf shrubs; permanently frozen ground (permafrost) | northern Siberia, northern Canada |
| **Ice desert** | permanently below 0 °C | no vegetation | Antarctica, Greenland |

### Altitudinal zones of the Alps

With increasing altitude the temperature falls and the vegetation changes much as it does from the equator to the pole. The boundaries are higher on the southern edge of the Alps and in the inner Alps than on the northern edge.

| Altitudinal zone | Altitude (Northern Alps, approximate) | Vegetation and land use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **colline zone** | up to about 800 m | deciduous forest, arable farming, fruit growing and viticulture, dense settlement |
| **montane zone** | up to about 1500 m | beech, fir and spruce forest, grassland, permanent settlements |
| **subalpine zone** | up to about 1800 to 2200 m | spruce, larch, Swiss stone pine; ends at the **timberline**; alpine pasture farming |
| **alpine zone** | up to about 2500 to 3000 m | dwarf shrubs, alpine grassland (meadows); alpine pastures |
| **nival zone** | above | rock, snow and ice; above the **snow line** snow remains even in summer |

## Soils

**Soil** is the uppermost, living weathering layer of the Earth's crust. It develops over centuries to millennia from rock, climate, organisms, relief and time. A soil profile is divided into **horizons**: O (litter layer), A (humus-rich topsoil), B (subsoil with weathering and accumulation), C (parent rock).

| Soil type | Zone | Properties |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ferralsol** (latosol) | tropical rainforest | deeply weathered, red (iron and aluminium oxides), **poor in nutrients**; the nutrients are held in the biomass |
| **Brown earth** (cambisol) | deciduous and mixed forest | moderately fertile, the most common soil in Central Europe |
| **Chernozem** (black earth) | steppe | very rich in humus, deep, **very fertile**; "breadbaskets" such as Ukraine |
| **Podzol** | boreal coniferous forest | acidic, leached, poor in nutrients |
| **Desert soil** | desert | hardly any humus, often salt accumulation |

For the rainforest this means: after slash-and-burn clearing, the nutrients from the ash are only enough for a few harvests; after that the soil is exhausted and threatened by erosion. Soils are a non-renewable resource; in Austria **land take** through construction is a central issue of [spatial planning](/en/geography/urbanisation-and-spatial-planning/#spatial-planning-in-austria).

## Water cycle and rivers

About 97 % of the Earth's water is salt water in the oceans. Only about 2.5 % is fresh water, and most of that is locked in glaciers and ice sheets or lies as groundwater. Rivers and lakes make up only a tiny fraction.

In the **water cycle**, water evaporates over seas and land (evaporation, in plants transpiration), condenses into clouds, falls as precipitation and flows back to the sea on the surface (runoff) or underground (infiltration, groundwater). The cycle is driven by solar energy.

A river drains its **catchment area** (drainage basin); the boundary between catchment areas is called a **watershed** (drainage divide). The **flow regime** describes when in the year a river carries a lot of water. Alpine rivers have a **glacial** or **nival** regime with the highest water level in early summer, when snow and glaciers melt; lowland rivers have a **pluvial** regime that depends on rain.

Almost all of Austria (about 96 % of its area) drains via the **Danube** into the Black Sea. Most of Vorarlberg lies in the catchment area of the **Rhine** (North Sea), and a small part of the Waldviertel drains via the Lainsitz and the Vltava into the **Elbe**. The Danube flows through Austria for about 350 km and is an international waterway; its hydroelectric power stations supply a large share of Austria's electricity.

## Glaciers

**Glaciers** form where, over years, more snow falls than melts. The snow is compacted via firn into glacier ice, which flows downhill under its own weight. In the **accumulation zone** above the equilibrium line, gain predominates; in the **ablation zone** below it, melting predominates.

Glaciers shape landscapes: they carve out **U-shaped valleys** (glacial troughs), leave behind **cirques**, push debris together into **moraines** and leave lakes behind after melting. The large lakes of the Salzkammergut and the Alpine foreland are witnesses of the **ice ages**, during which the Alps were almost completely glaciated (last maximum about 20,000 years ago).

Since the end of the Little Ice Age around 1850 the Alpine glaciers have been shrinking, strongly accelerating since the 1980s. The glacier monitoring service of the Austrian Alpine Club documents the retreat every year. The consequences: less meltwater in summer during droughts, unstable slopes due to thawing permafrost and the loss of landscape and tourist attractions.

## Natural hazards

A natural event only becomes a **natural disaster** when it affects people, buildings or infrastructure. **Risk** results from the hazard (probability and strength of the event), **exposure** (what is affected) and **vulnerability** (how susceptible it is).

### Natural hazards in Austria

| Hazard | Cause | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Avalanche** | masses of snow break loose on slopes from about 30° gradient, triggered by new snow, wind, warming or people | Galtür (Tyrol), 23 February 1999: 31 deaths |
| **Flood** | prolonged heavy rain, often amplified by snowmelt; sealed surfaces and straightened rivers speed up runoff | August 2002 and June 2013 on the Danube, Kamp and Inn; September 2024 in Lower Austria |
| **Debris flow** (German _Mure_) | mixture of water, mud and rock that rushes down steep torrents after heavy rain | St. Anton am Arlberg (Tyrol), August 2024 |
| **Rockfall** | frost weathering, thawing permafrost, joints in the rock | threat to roads and railways in the mountains |
| **Landslide** | waterlogged slopes slide along a slip surface | landslides after prolonged rain |

Protection rests on several pillars:

- **Hazard zone maps** of the Torrent and Avalanche Control service (WLV) and the federal water engineering administration designate **red zones** (hazard so great that building is impossible or only possible at great expense) and **yellow zones** (building with conditions). Municipalities must take them into account in land-use zoning.
- **Technical measures**: avalanche barriers (snow bridges, galleries), debris retention dams against debris flows, dykes, retention basins.
- **Biological measures**: **protective forests** hold snow and soil in place. A large part of Austria's mountain forest is protective forest.
- **Organisational measures**: the provinces' avalanche warning services, flood forecasts, road closures, evacuation plans.
- **Natural retention**: renaturalised rivers and floodplains (retention areas) absorb water before it reaches settlements.
