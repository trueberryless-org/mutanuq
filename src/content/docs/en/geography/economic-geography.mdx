**Economic geography** studies where economic activities take place and why. Why is there a steelworks in Linz, why are semiconductor manufacturers based in Villach, why does Tyrol live from tourism? The answers lie in location factors, in history and in political decisions. Economic fundamentals such as markets, price formation and economic policy are covered in the section [Economy and Law](/en/economy_and_law/).

## Economic sectors

| Sector | Activity | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **primary sector** | extraction of natural products | agriculture and forestry, fishing, partly mining |
| **secondary sector** | processing and production | industry, crafts and trades, construction, energy generation |
| **tertiary sector** | services | trade, transport, banking, tourism, education, health, administration |
| **quaternary sector** | knowledge- and information-based services (often counted as part of the tertiary sector) | research and development, IT, consulting, media |

The **three-sector hypothesis** by Jean Fourastié (1949) describes **structural change**: in agrarian societies most people work in the primary sector. With industrialisation the secondary sector grows, because machines make agriculture more productive and release workers. With rising prosperity and further automation of industry, the tertiary sector becomes dominant (**tertiarisation**). The share of the sectors in employment is therefore also an indicator of development.

In Austria in 2023 about **3.1 %** of employed persons worked in agriculture and forestry, about **25.7 %** in industry and trades and about **71.2 %** in the service sector (Statistics Austria). Austria has a comparatively strong industrial core.

## Location factors and location theories

**Location factors** are all conditions that influence the choice of a company's location.

| Hard location factors (measurable, affecting costs) | Soft location factors (hard to measure) |
| --- | --- |
| availability and price of land | image of the region, business climate |
| transport links (motorway, rail, airport, port) | quality of life, cultural and leisure facilities |
| labour: number, qualifications, wage costs | nearby education and research institutions |
| proximity to raw materials, suppliers and markets | proximity to other companies in the industry (networks) |
| energy and water supply, broadband | political stability, efficiency of administration |
| taxes, charges, subsidies | environmental quality, housing supply |

For the IT industry, skilled workers, broadband, universities and quality of life are decisive; proximity to raw materials hardly matters. Companies in the same industry often concentrate spatially in **clusters**, because there they share skilled workers, suppliers and knowledge (**agglomeration advantages**). Examples are Silicon Valley, the automotive cluster in Styria and the electronics and semiconductor location Villach.

Classical **location theories**:

- **Johann Heinrich von Thünen** (1826) explained the arrangement of agriculture in **rings** around a city: the further from the market, the higher the transport costs and the more extensive the land use (inside vegetables and milk, further out grain, furthest out livestock grazing).
- **Alfred Weber** (1909) determined the optimal industrial location as the point with the lowest transport and labour costs between raw materials and market. Weight-losing materials such as ore and coal draw industry to the raw material.
- **Walter Christaller** (1933) described a hierarchy of places in his **central place theory**: higher-order centres offer less frequently demanded goods and services (university, medical specialist) for a larger catchment area. The system of central places is still a basis of spatial planning today.

## Agriculture

| Distinction | Forms |
| --- | --- |
| goal | **subsistence farming** (self-sufficiency, widespread in the Global South) and **market production** |
| input per area | **intensive** (much labour, capital, fertiliser on a small area) and **extensive** (large areas, low input) |
| farming method | conventional and **organic** (without synthetic pesticides and fertilisers) |
| type of holding | family farm, **plantation** (monoculture for export, such as coffee, bananas, palm oil), agribusiness corporation |

Agriculture in industrialised countries is highly mechanised and specialised. Its problems are soil compaction, nitrate in groundwater, loss of biodiversity and dependence on subsidies. In the Global South, export plantations compete with feeding the country's own population, and international investors buy up large areas of farmland (**land grabbing**). The **Green Revolution** of the 1960s raised yields in Asia with high-yield varieties, irrigation and fertiliser, but also with ecological costs.

In Austria agriculture is small-scale, and a large part of it lies in **mountain areas**, where grassland, dairy and livestock farming and alpine pastures dominate. Arable farming is concentrated in the Alpine foreland, the Weinviertel, the Marchfeld and northern Burgenland; wine in Lower Austria, Burgenland, Styria and Vienna. Austria has one of the highest shares of organic farming in the EU, about a quarter of its agricultural area. The EU Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) supports farms with direct payments and environmental programmes (in Austria ÖPUL).

## Industry

**Industry** produces goods in large quantities using machines and division of labour. Historically, industrial regions developed where coal and ore were found (Ruhr, Upper Silesia, the English Midlands). With the decline of coal and steel from the 1960s, these **old industrial regions** went through painful structural change with unemployment and out-migration.

Industrial locations in Austria:

| Region | Industries |
| --- | --- |
| **Linz and Upper Austria** | steel (voestalpine), chemicals, mechanical engineering, automotive suppliers; Upper Austria is the industrial province |
| **Styria** (Graz, Mur-Mürz valley) | automotive cluster (Magna Steyr in Graz), steel and stainless steel (Donawitz, Kapfenberg), Erzberg; formerly a typical crisis region of structural change |
| **Vienna and the Vienna Basin** | electrical engineering, pharmaceuticals, vehicle manufacturing; Wiener Neustadt and the Industrieviertel |
| **Vorarlberg** | historically textiles, today metalworking, food, fittings; strongly export-oriented |
| **Carinthia** (Villach) | microelectronics and semiconductors (Infineon), research at Silicon Austria Labs |

Today **globalisation** and **automation** shape industry: labour-intensive manufacturing has been relocated to low-wage countries, while research, development and highly automated production (**Industry 4.0**) remain in Europe. Industry and industry-related services are closely linked.

## Services

The **tertiary sector** is very diverse: simple personal services (hairdressing, cleaning), trade, public services (schools, hospitals) and highly qualified business services (banks, insurance, legal and management consulting, software). Highly qualified services cluster in large cities, because customers, skilled workers and personal contacts are available there. Digitalisation, however, also allows services to be relocated (**offshoring**), for example call centres and software development to India or Eastern Europe.

## Energy and raw materials

Energy and raw materials are unevenly distributed on Earth. This shapes trade flows, political dependencies and conflicts.

- **Fossil fuels** (oil, natural gas, coal) still cover about 80 % of global primary energy consumption. Large oil reserves lie in the Persian Gulf, Venezuela, Canada and Russia; natural gas mainly in Russia, Iran, Qatar and the USA. Fossil fuels are finite and cause most CO₂ emissions.
- **Renewable energies** (hydro, wind, solar, biomass, geothermal) depend on natural conditions: hydropower needs a gradient and precipitation, solar energy insolation, wind power open locations.
- **Mineral raw materials** such as copper, lithium, cobalt and rare earths are needed for electronics and the energy transition. Their extraction is concentrated in a few countries (see [Sustainability and Resources](/en/geography/sustainability-and-resources/#raw-materials-for-it)).

**Austria** generates most of its electricity from renewable sources: hydropower supplied about 60 % of electricity generation in 2024, wind and photovoltaics together about 20 %. Oil and natural gas, on the other hand, are mostly imported; the heavy dependence on Russian natural gas became a political problem with the Russian attack on Ukraine in 2022. Domestic raw materials include iron ore (Erzberg), magnesite, gypsum, salt (Salzkammergut), timber and water.

## Tourism in Austria

**Tourism** is one of Austria's most important economic sectors. According to Statistics Austria, 2024 was a record year:

| Indicator 2024 | Value |
| --- | --- |
| overnight stays | 154.29 million (+2.1 % compared with 2023) |
| arrivals | 46.71 million |
| of which overnight stays by foreign guests | 114.04 million |
| most important source market | Germany with 58.45 million overnight stays |

Tyrol and Salzburg together account for more than half of all overnight stays. A distinction is made between the **winter season** (ski tourism in the Alps) and the **summer season** (hiking, lakes, cities); Vienna is the centre of **city tourism** and **conference tourism**.

Tourism brings income and jobs to mountain regions that would otherwise have few alternatives, and so keeps peripheral valleys populated. At the same time it has downsides:

- **Ecological**: land take by hotels and ski lifts, interference with glaciers and slopes, artificial snow with its water and energy demand, traffic from arrivals and departures by car.
- **Social**: high housing prices due to second homes, seasonal jobs with demanding working hours, **overtourism** at hotspots such as Hallstatt.
- **Climate change**: snow reliability is decreasing at low and medium altitudes. Ski areas below about 1500 m are coming under pressure, and resorts are increasingly turning to **year-round tourism**.

**Sustainable (soft) tourism** tries to receive guests with as little burden on nature and residents as possible, for example through arrival by train, regional products and limits on expansion.

## Economic regions

Worldwide there are three large economic core regions, the **Triad**: North America, Europe (EU) and East Asia (Japan, South Korea, China). Production, trade, research and corporate headquarters are concentrated in them. In addition there are emerging economies, such as the **BRICS** group (originally Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa, enlarged since 2024).

Within states, a distinction is made between **active regions** (economically strong centres with in-migration) and **passive regions** (structurally weak periphery with out-migration). The **core-periphery model** describes this disparity at every scale: between town and country, between regions of a country and between industrialised and developing countries. States join together in **economic alliances** to remove trade barriers: free trade area (such as USMCA in North America), customs union, single market (EU) and monetary union (euro area). The European economic areas are covered on the page [Europe](/en/geography/europe/#economic-areas-and-alliances).
