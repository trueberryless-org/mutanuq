Since the 20th century, human life has been shifting from the countryside into cities. Cities offer jobs, education and services in a small space, but they also cause traffic, land take and social tensions. **Spatial planning** tries to steer the use of scarce land so that housing, business, transport and nature all have room side by side.

## Terms

- **Urbanisation** in the narrow sense means the growth in the number, area and population of cities, measured for example by the **level of urbanisation** (share of the urban population in the total population).
- In the wider sense it also means the spread of urban ways of living, working and behaving, including into rural areas.
- What counts as a **city** is defined differently by different states: by population, density, function or legal status. Austria knows the urban municipality as a legal term independent of size.

In the **World Urbanization Prospects 2025**, the UN therefore used an internationally uniform method for the first time, the **Degree of Urbanisation**. Based on grid cells with population density it distinguishes **cities** (densely populated areas with at least 50,000 inhabitants), **towns and semi-dense areas** and **rural areas**. According to this method, about **45 %** of the 8.2 billion people lived in cities in 2025 (1950: 20 %) and about 36 % in towns. Two thirds of population growth up to 2050 is expected in cities. Older figures based on national definitions (such as "55 % urban in 2018") are not directly comparable because of the different method.

## Megacities

A **megacity** has at least 10 million inhabitants. According to the UN, their number rose from 8 in 1975 to **33 in 2025**, 19 of them in Asia. According to the new method, **Jakarta** with almost 42 million inhabitants is the largest city on Earth, ahead of Dhaka (almost 40 million) and Tokyo (33 million).

Megacities in the Global South grow through migration from the countryside and high birth numbers faster than infrastructure can be built. Typical problems are **informal settlements** (slums, favelas, bidonvilles) without secure water and electricity supply, traffic congestion, air pollution, a large **informal sector** (work without contracts or social security) and exposure to natural events, such as flooding in coastal cities. At the same time, megacities concentrate the economic power of their country. A city that far outstrips all other cities of a country in size and importance is called a **primate city** (for example Bangkok, Lima; in Austria, Vienna has a similar position).

**Global cities** (world cities) such as New York, London, Tokyo or Paris, by contrast, are defined not by population but by their **command function** in the world economy: headquarters of corporations, stock exchanges, banks, international organisations and business services.

## Urban models

Urban models simplify the internal structure of cities and make them comparable.

| Model | Author | Statement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Concentric zone model** | Burgess, 1925 (Chicago) | the city grows in rings around the business core; social status rises outwards |
| **Sector model** | Hoyt, 1939 | land uses spread in wedges along transport axes (industry along railway lines, villa districts in favoured locations) |
| **Multiple nuclei model** | Harris and Ullman, 1945 | cities have several centres with their own functions (port, university, shopping centre) |

Other cultural regions have their own models. The **European city** has a historic core with church, town hall and market, often a former city wall (in Vienna the Ringstrasse in its place), dense late-19th-century buildings and a strong role for public planning. The **North American city** has a grid pattern, a high-rise central business district (CBD) and extensive suburbs of detached houses. The **Latin American city** has a colonial core with a central square (plaza), upper-class districts along a grand avenue and marginal settlements on the outskirts. The **Oriental city** is organised around the mosque and bazaar (souk) with cul-de-sacs and residential quarters by religion or origin.

## Stages of urban development

1. **Urbanisation**: the core city grows strongly through migration from the countryside, in Europe during industrialisation.
2. **Suburbanisation**: population and later retail and business move to the **surrounding area**. Reasons are the wish for a house of one's own and greenery, cheaper land and the car. Consequences are urban sprawl, soil sealing, commuter traffic and shopping centres on greenfield sites, while town centres decline. Typical in Austria are the area surrounding Vienna in Lower Austria and the commuter belt around Graz.
3. **Counterurbanisation** (deurbanisation): the urban region as a whole also loses inhabitants to rural areas.
4. **Reurbanisation**: inner cities gain inhabitants again, for example through immigration, young households and urban renewal. Vienna has been growing again since the 1990s.

Reurbanisation is often accompanied by **gentrification**: an inexpensive district close to the centre first becomes attractive to students and creative people, then is renovated and upgraded. Rents rise, and the original, poorer residents are displaced. **Social housing** (in Vienna municipal housing and subsidised housing) dampens this development.

Guiding principles of today's urban planning are the **city of short distances** (housing, work, services and leisure within walking distance, also called the "15-minute city"), densification instead of expansion onto green land, greenery against heat islands and the **smart city**, which uses data and sensors to manage traffic, energy and administration.

## Spatial planning in Austria

**Spatial planning** is the forward-looking planning of land use. Under the Austrian Federal Constitution it is essentially a **matter for the federal provinces**: each province has its own **spatial planning act** (in Vienna the building code regulates urban planning). The federal government is responsible for sectoral planning such as railways, federal roads, forestry and water law.

| Level | Instruments |
| --- | --- |
| **Federal government and provinces together** | **ÖROK** (Austrian Conference on Spatial Planning, founded in 1971) draws up the **Austrian Spatial Development Concept** (currently ÖREK 2030), a non-binding guideline |
| **Province** (regional planning) | provincial development programmes, regional spatial planning programmes, definition of settlement boundaries and green zones |
| **Municipality** (local planning) | **local development concept** (long-term goals), **land-use zoning plan** (binding for every plot: building land, green land, traffic area, with subcategories), **development plan** (building height, building lines, density) |

The municipalities therefore decide themselves where building is permitted. This brings local knowledge but also conflicts of interest: a municipality benefits from municipal tax and new inhabitants, while the whole region bears the follow-up costs such as traffic and loss of land.

A central problem is **land take**: according to surveys by the Environment Agency Austria, on average about 11 to 12 hectares per day have recently been newly taken for settlements, business and transport, a large part of it sealed. The Austrian sustainability strategy of 2002 set a target of 2.5 hectares per day; the soil strategy adopted by ÖROK aims to reach this value by 2030. Reasons for the high consumption are detached-house estates, business parks and retail parks on the edge of towns, car parks and roads. Sealed soil is lost for agriculture and water absorption, which aggravates floods and heat in settlements.

## Mobility and transport

Transport connects places of residence, work and services; accessibility determines the value of a location. The **modal split** describes how trips are divided between modes of transport. In Vienna a large share of trips are made on foot, by bicycle and by public transport; in rural municipalities the car dominates because public transport is sparse.

| Mode of transport | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Road** (cars, lorries) | flexible, door to door | high energy consumption and CO₂ emissions, congestion, land requirement, accidents |
| **Rail** | energy-efficient, high capacity, safe | expensive infrastructure, tied to lines |
| **Waterway** (Danube) | very cheap for bulk goods | slow, low and high water |
| **Air transport** | fast over long distances | highest energy consumption per passenger kilometre, noise |
| **Cycling and walking** | emission-free, healthy, space-saving | short range, weather-dependent |

In Austria transport is the sector whose greenhouse gas emissions have risen most since 1990. Approaches to a **transport transition** are:

- Expanding the railway: with the **Koralm Tunnel** (about 33 km, Austria's longest railway tunnel), the Koralm Railway opened in December 2025 shortens the travel time between Graz and Klagenfurt to about 41 minutes. The Semmering Base Tunnel and the Brenner Base Tunnel are under construction.
- The **KlimaTicket Ö** (since October 2021) is valid on almost all public transport in Austria.
- **Shifting freight transport** to rail, especially transit traffic over the Brenner.
- Electromobility, car sharing, cycle paths and traffic-calmed zones in cities.
- Spatial planning that enables short distances: developing settlements around stops instead of scattering them.
