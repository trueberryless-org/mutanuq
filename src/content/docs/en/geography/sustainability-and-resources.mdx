Humanity consumes more natural resources than the Earth can renew in the same period. At the same time, resources and the burdens of using them are very unequally distributed in space: raw materials for smartphones and laptops are often mined in countries of the Global South under difficult conditions, while the devices are used mainly in the North. Geography asks **where** resources occur, **who** uses them and **who** bears the consequences.

## Sustainability

In 1987 the United Nations **Brundtland Report** defined **sustainable development** as development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. The term originally comes from forestry (Hans Carl von Carlowitz, 1713): no more wood should be felled than grows back.

The **three-pillar model** combines ecological, economic and social sustainability. Because the economy and society depend on functioning ecosystems, the model of **strong sustainability** stresses the priority of ecological limits. The concept of **planetary boundaries** (Rockström and others, 2009) describes nine limits of the Earth system, such as climate change, biodiversity loss and the nitrogen cycle, several of which have already been exceeded. The biological foundations of ecosystems are covered in biology.

## Resources and scarcity

| Type of resource | Examples | Problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **non-renewable** | oil, natural gas, coal, metals, phosphate | finite deposits; extraction becomes more costly and environmentally damaging over time |
| **renewable but over-exploitable** | groundwater, fish stocks, forests, soils | only renew themselves if use stays below the regeneration rate |
| **renewable, practically inexhaustible** | solar radiation, wind, geothermal heat | limited by land, storage and grids |

Scarcity is often not absolute but **economic** or **political**: a raw material is available but expensive to extract, concentrated in a few countries or hard to obtain because of conflicts and export restrictions. **Reserves** are the known deposits that can be extracted economically with today's technology; they grow when prices rise or technology improves. The **static range** (reserves divided by annual production) is therefore only a snapshot.

## Water

Only a small part of fresh water is easily accessible to people (see [water cycle](/en/geography/landscapes-and-vegetation-zones/#water-cycle-and-rivers)). According to WHO and UNICEF, about 2 billion people lack access to safely managed drinking water. **Agriculture** uses about 70 % of freshwater withdrawals worldwide, mainly for irrigation.

Water scarcity results from low precipitation, over-exploitation of groundwater (falling water tables in India, California or northern China), pollution and climate change. The **Aral Sea** in Central Asia is the best-known example: because its tributaries have been diverted for cotton fields since the 1960s, it has largely dried up. Transboundary rivers such as the Nile, Euphrates and Tigris or Mekong lead to conflicts between upstream and downstream states when dams are built.

The concept of **virtual water** captures the water used to make a product. According to calculations by the Water Footprint Network, about 15,000 litres are needed on global average for 1 kg of beef. Anyone who imports products from dry regions also imports their water. Data centres and semiconductor factories also need a lot of water for cooling and cleaning.

Austria is very rich in water: drinking water comes almost entirely from groundwater and spring water, which can usually be used without treatment. Vienna obtains its water via two mountain spring pipelines from the Limestone Alps.

## Energy transition

The **energy transition** is the conversion of the energy supply from fossil and nuclear sources to renewable energy combined with higher efficiency. It changes the geography of energy:

- Generation becomes **decentralised** (photovoltaics on roofs, wind farms) and depends on wind and sun. Grids are needed that transport electricity over long distances (for example wind power from northern to southern Germany), as well as **storage** (pumped storage, batteries, hydrogen).
- Dependencies shift from oil- and gas-producing countries to countries with raw materials for batteries, generators and solar modules and with the corresponding factories, above all China.
- Land-use conflicts arise over wind farms, ground-mounted photovoltaics, power lines and hydroelectric power stations (nature conservation, landscape, agriculture).

In the **Renewable Energy Expansion Act** (EAG, 2021), Austria set itself the goal of covering 100 % of its electricity consumption from renewable sources on a national balance from 2030. According to E-Control, the share was about 94 % in 2024; hydropower supplied about 60 % of generation. Replacing fossil energy in transport, space heating and industry, where oil and natural gas still dominate, is more difficult.

## Raw materials for IT

A smartphone contains dozens of chemical elements, including copper, gold, silver, tin, tantalum, cobalt, lithium, gallium, indium and rare earths. Their extraction is concentrated in a few countries:

| Raw material | Use | Main producing countries | Problems |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cobalt** | cathodes of lithium-ion batteries | **DR Congo** (2024 about 220,000 of 290,000 t worldwide, about three quarters; USGS), Indonesia | artisanal mining with child labour and dangerous conditions, corruption |
| **Rare earths** (17 elements such as neodymium, dysprosium) | permanent magnets in loudspeakers, vibration motors, hard disks, electric motors, wind turbines | **China** (2024 about 270,000 of 390,000 t worldwide, about 69 %; USGS), plus most of the processing | not rare but costly to separate; toxic and partly radioactive waste; dependency, Chinese export restrictions |
| **Lithium** | batteries | Australia, Chile, China, Argentina | high water consumption when extracted from salt flats in the "lithium triangle" (Chile, Argentina, Bolivia) |
| **Tantalum** (from coltan) | capacitors | DR Congo, Rwanda, Brazil | financing of armed groups in eastern Congo |
| **Gallium, germanium** | semiconductors, LEDs, optical fibre | almost exclusively China | export controls since 2023 |
| **Gold, tin, copper** | contacts, solder, conductor tracks | worldwide | use of mercury in artisanal mining, rainforest destruction |

### Conflict minerals

**Conflict minerals** are raw materials whose extraction or trade finances armed conflicts, particularly in the east of the Democratic Republic of the Congo. The **EU Conflict Minerals Regulation** (Regulation (EU) 2017/821, applicable since 1 January 2021) requires importers of **tin, tantalum, tungsten and gold** (3TG) from conflict-affected and high-risk areas to carry out due diligence on their supply chain. Cobalt is not covered; for cobalt, lithium, nickel and graphite in batteries, the EU Batteries Regulation (2023) provides its own due diligence obligations.

With the **Critical Raw Materials Act** (2024), the EU wants to reduce dependency: by 2030 at least 10 % of the demand for strategic raw materials is to be mined in the EU, 40 % processed and 25 % obtained from recycling, and no single third country is to supply more than 65 % of any strategic raw material.

### Electronic waste

According to the **Global E-waste Monitor 2024**, about **62 million tonnes** of electrical and electronic waste were generated worldwide in 2022. Only 22.3 % of it was documented as properly collected and recycled. Part of it is exported illegally to countries of the Global South, where it is dismantled or burned under conditions harmful to health to recover copper and other metals. At the same time, electronic waste is an **urban mine**: a tonne of old mobile phones contains many times the gold of a tonne of gold ore.

## Circular economy

In the **linear economy**, raw materials are extracted, processed into products, used and thrown away ("take, make, dispose"). The **circular economy** keeps products and materials in use for as long as possible.

The **waste hierarchy** of the EU Waste Framework Directive sets the order:

1. **Prevention**
2. **Preparing for re-use** (repair, refurbishment)
3. **Recycling** (material recovery)
4. **Other recovery**, such as energy recovery through incineration
5. **Disposal** (landfill)

The **R strategies** arrange measures in a similar way: _refuse_, _reduce_, _reuse_, _repair_, _refurbish_ and _remanufacture_, _recycle_ (recover material) and _recover_ (obtain energy). The higher a strategy ranks, the more material and energy it saves.

For IT this means: durable, repairable devices with replaceable batteries, software updates over many years, reuse of company hardware (refurbished devices), modular design and take-back schemes. Among other things, the EU has adopted the common **USB-C charging port** (mandatory for new smartphones and many other devices since the end of 2024), the **right to repair** (directive of 2024) and ecodesign requirements for smartphones and tablets. Austria subsidises repairs of electrical devices with the **repair bonus**.

## Ecological footprint

The **ecological footprint** (Mathis Wackernagel and William Rees, 1990s) converts the consumption of food, energy, timber, living space and the absorption of CO₂ into the biologically productive area needed for it. The unit is the **global hectare** (gha), a hectare with globally average productivity. It is compared with the **biocapacity**, the available productive area.

According to calculations by the **Global Footprint Network**, humanity currently consumes as much as if it had about 1.8 Earths at its disposal. **Earth Overshoot Day**, the day on which consumption exceeds the Earth's annual capacity to regenerate, fell on **24 July** in 2025. If everyone lived like the population of Austria, this day would already have been reached on **29 March** 2025. The largest share of the footprint of industrialised countries is the carbon footprint from the use of fossil energy.

The footprint is a vivid model, but it simplifies greatly: it captures, for example, the loss of biodiversity or water pollution only inadequately. The **carbon footprint** (greenhouse gas emissions in CO₂ equivalents) and the **water footprint** are used in addition. Personally, the footprint can be reduced above all in mobility (flights, car), diet (meat consumption), housing (heating, living space) and consumption (buying durable products); political frameworks and infrastructure have the greater effect.
