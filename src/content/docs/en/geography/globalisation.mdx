**Globalisation** is the increasing worldwide interconnection of the economy, politics, culture, the environment and communication. It is not a new phenomenon (the Silk Road and colonial trade already connected continents), but it has accelerated strongly since the 1990s. In IT it is particularly tangible: a smartphone is designed in California, assembled in China or India from raw materials from Africa and chips from Taiwan, and its data travel through submarine cables to data centres in Ireland.

## Causes

- **Falling transport costs**: the standardised **container** (from 1956) made sea transport cheap and quick to handle; large container ships carry more than 20,000 standard containers (TEU).
- **Information and communication technology**: the internet, mobile networks and fibre-optic cables allow companies to be managed worldwide in real time.
- **Liberalisation**: removal of tariffs and capital controls (GATT, WTO, free trade agreements, EU single market).
- **Political upheavals**: the opening of China from 1978, the end of the Cold War from 1989 to 1991 and the integration of Eastern Europe into the world economy.
- **Differences in labour costs** and companies' search for new sales markets.

## Dimensions

| Dimension | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **economic** | world trade, direct investment, global financial markets, international division of labour |
| **political** | international organisations (UN, WTO, IMF), supranational unions (EU), global agreements (climate) |
| **cultural** | global brands, films, music, streaming, English as a world language; counter-movements to preserve regional identity |
| **social** | migration, tourism, international students |
| **ecological** | global climate change, spread of invasive species, export of environmental burdens (electronic waste) |
| **technological** | worldwide spread of the internet, software and standards |

The **KOF Globalisation Index** of ETH Zurich measures economic, social and political globalisation for almost all states. Small, open economies such as Switzerland, the Netherlands or Austria are among the most globalised countries.

## World trade

Since 1950 world trade has grown many times faster than world production. According to the **WTO**, commercial services worth about **US$ 8.7 trillion** were traded in 2024; trade in goods is considerably larger still. Most trade takes place within and between the economic regions of the **Triad** (North America, Europe, East Asia). China is the world's largest exporter of goods.

Two kinds of trade are distinguished: **inter-industry trade** (different goods, such as raw materials for manufactured goods, typical between North and South) and **intra-industry trade** (similar goods, such as cars for cars, typical between industrialised countries). Free trade is justified theoretically by **comparative advantage** (David Ricardo, 1817): if countries specialise in what they can produce relatively most cheaply, everyone's prosperity increases.

Austria is an export-oriented economy: exports of goods and services correspond to more than half of GDP. By far the most important trading partner is **Germany**; other important partners are the USA, Italy, Switzerland, China and the neighbouring countries to the east.

## Global value chains: the smartphone

A **value chain** comprises all steps from extracting raw materials to sale and disposal. In **global value chains** these steps are distributed to the locations that are cheapest or most capable.

| Step | Typical locations | Share of value added |
| --- | --- | --- |
| research, design, software, brand | USA (California), South Korea, China | high |
| raw materials: cobalt, lithium, rare earths, tin, tantalum, gold | DR Congo, Chile, Australia, China, Indonesia | low, but with large social and ecological consequences |
| chips | Taiwan (TSMC), South Korea, USA; machines from the Netherlands (ASML) | high |
| displays, memory, camera modules | South Korea, Japan, China | medium |
| final assembly | China, increasingly India and Vietnam | low |
| logistics, marketing, sales | worldwide | high |
| disposal and recycling | partly legally in recycling plants, partly illegally in countries such as Ghana or Nigeria | low |

The **smile curve** (after Stan Shih, founder of Acer) shows this distribution: at the beginning (research, design) and at the end (brand, distribution, service) of the chain value added is high, in the middle (assembly) it is low. Countries therefore try to "move up" the chain, as South Korea, Taiwan and China have done.

Global chains are efficient but **vulnerable**: the COVID-19 pandemic, the blockage of the Suez Canal in 2021 and the chip shortage from 2020 brought production to a standstill worldwide. Since then states and companies have been trying to reduce dependencies, for example through stockpiling, multiple suppliers, relocation (_reshoring_) and support programmes such as the **European Chips Act** (2023).

## Transnational companies

**Transnational companies** (TNCs, also multinational corporations) have production or sales sites in several states and manage them from a headquarters. They organise a large part of world trade, and much of it takes place as **intra-firm trade** between subsidiaries. Through **foreign direct investment** they build plants or take over companies abroad.

TNCs bring capital, technology and jobs, but they can also play states off against each other (locational competition over taxes and environmental regulations) and shift profits to low-tax countries. The OECD has therefore agreed a global **minimum tax** of 15 % for large corporations, which the EU has applied since 2024. The world's largest companies by market capitalisation today are technology corporations.

## The World Trade Organization

The **WTO** (_World Trade Organization_), based in Geneva, was founded in 1995 as the successor to the **GATT** (1947) and has 166 members. Austria has been a member since its foundation but is represented by the EU in trade matters.

Basic principles:

- **Most-favoured-nation treatment**: a trade advantage that a member grants to one country must be granted to all members (exceptions for free trade areas and developing countries).
- **National treatment**: once tariffs have been paid, imported goods must not be treated worse than domestic ones.
- **Reduction of tariffs and non-tariff barriers to trade**.
- **Dispute settlement**: a procedure decides trade disputes. The Appellate Body has been unable to function since the end of 2019 because the USA blocks the appointment of new judges.

Besides the WTO, bilateral and regional **free trade agreements** shape trade, such as the EU's agreement with Canada (CETA) or the agreement with the Mercosur states. In recent years protective tariffs and trade conflicts between the major economic powers have been increasing again.

## Digital globalisation

Digital globalisation rests on a very physical infrastructure.

### Submarine cables

More than **99 %** of intercontinental data traffic runs not via satellites but through **fibre-optic submarine cables**. According to TeleGeography, about **570 submarine cable systems** were in service in 2025, with another 81 planned. The cables are only about as thick as a garden hose and end at **landing stations** on the coast. Hubs include Marseille, Cornwall, Singapore, Egypt (the Europe-Asia link via the Red Sea) and the US East Coast.

New cables are increasingly financed by large technology corporations such as Google, Meta, Microsoft and Amazon. Submarine cables are vulnerable: many are damaged every year by ship anchors and fishing, and incidents in the Baltic Sea in 2023 and 2024 have heightened concerns about deliberate sabotage. Satellite internet such as Starlink supplements the cables for remote areas but cannot replace their capacity.

### Internet exchanges and data centres

Networks of different providers exchange data at **Internet Exchange Points** (IXPs). One of the largest in the world is DE-CIX in Frankfurt; in Austria the **VIX** (Vienna Internet Exchange) is the most important hub.

**Data centres** cluster where fibre connectivity, secure and affordable power supply, cooling, skilled workers and legal certainty come together: in Europe mainly around Frankfurt, London, Amsterdam, Paris and Dublin (the "FLAP-D" markets), in the USA in Northern Virginia. Their energy demand is a new location factor and is growing strongly because of AI applications: according to the International Energy Agency (IEA), data centres consumed about 415 TWh of electricity in 2024, about 1.5 % of global consumption. In Ireland data centres already take up a large share of the electricity grid. Data protection law (GDPR) and questions of **digital sovereignty** also influence where data are stored.

The **digital divide** describes unequal access to the internet: in industrialised countries almost everyone is online, in the least developed countries only a small proportion.

## Criticism and counter-movements

| Arguments for globalisation | Criticism |
| --- | --- |
| gains in prosperity through specialisation and cheaper products | gains unequally distributed, within and between states |
| hundreds of millions of people in Asia have escaped poverty | working conditions in low-wage countries (textile industry, Rana Plaza collapse in Bangladesh in 2013) |
| spread of knowledge and technology | loss of industrial jobs in old industrialised countries |
| cooperation on global problems | locational competition pushes down taxes and environmental standards ("race to the bottom") |
| cultural exchange | cultural homogenisation, dominance of a few corporations |
| | environmental burden from transport, outsourcing of emissions and waste |

Responses include **fair trade** (Fairtrade labels guarantee minimum prices and standards), supply chain laws that make companies responsible for human rights and the environment in their supply chains (in the EU the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive of 2024), and **regionalisation** with shorter supply chains. Since about 2008 world trade has hardly grown faster than the world economy; some therefore speak of a phase of **deglobalisation** or "slowbalisation".
