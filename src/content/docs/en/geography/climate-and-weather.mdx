Weather and climate determine where people live, what they grow and how they build. Climate change is currently shifting these conditions at a pace unprecedented in human history. To understand its consequences you need the fundamentals: how the sun heats the Earth, why there is wind and how climate zones arise.

## Structure of the atmosphere

The **atmosphere** is the Earth's envelope of gas. Dry air consists of 78.1 % nitrogen (N₂), 20.9 % oxygen (O₂) and 0.9 % argon. Carbon dioxide (CO₂) makes up only about 0.04 %, but as a greenhouse gas it is decisive for the heat balance. In addition there is a varying share of water vapour (up to about 4 %).

| Layer | Altitude | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Troposphere** | up to about 8 km (poles) or 17 km (equator) | contains about 80 % of the air mass and almost all water vapour; this is where **weather** happens; temperature falls with altitude by on average 0.65 °C per 100 m |
| **Stratosphere** | up to about 50 km | the **ozone layer** (mainly 15 to 35 km) absorbs UV radiation, so the temperature rises again |
| **Mesosphere** | up to about 85 km | temperature falls below −80 °C; meteors burn up |
| **Thermosphere** | above | very thin air; auroras; International Space Station at about 400 km |

## Weather, spell of weather and climate

- **Weather** is the state of the atmosphere at a place at a particular time: temperature, precipitation, air pressure, wind, cloud cover, humidity.
- A **spell of weather** (German _Witterung_) is the character of the weather over a few days to weeks ("a rainy summer").
- **Climate** is the statistics of weather over a long period, according to the definition of the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) at least **30 years**. The current reference period is 1991 to 2020.

A single hot day or a cold winter therefore says nothing about climate change; only the accumulation over decades shows the trend.

**Climate factors** determine the climate of a place: latitude (angle of incidence of sunlight), altitude, distance from the sea (**maritime** climate with mild winters and cool summers compared with **continental** climate with large temperature ranges), ocean currents (the Gulf Stream warms Western Europe), relief (windward and leeward sides of mountains) and land cover.

## Radiation balance and the greenhouse effect

Outside the atmosphere the sun delivers about 1361 W/m² (solar constant). About 30 % of the incoming radiation is reflected straight back by clouds, air and the surface; this reflectivity is called **albedo**. Fresh snow has an albedo of up to 0.9, oceans only about 0.06. The rest is absorbed by the surface and the atmosphere and emitted again as long-wave thermal radiation (infrared).

In the **natural greenhouse effect**, water vapour, CO₂, methane (CH₄), nitrous oxide (N₂O) and ozone absorb part of this thermal radiation and partly radiate it back to the surface. Without this effect the mean surface temperature would be about −18 °C instead of about +15 °C. The **anthropogenic greenhouse effect** amplifies it: since the beginning of industrialisation the CO₂ concentration has risen, mainly through burning fossil fuels and deforestation, to a global average of 420 ppm in 2023 (WMO). That is 151 % of the pre-industrial value of 1750.

Because sunlight strikes the equator steeply and the poles at a shallow angle, the tropics receive an energy surplus and the polar regions a deficit. Air and ocean currents balance this out. This is the engine of the global circulation.

## Air pressure and wind

Warm air expands and rises; at the ground a **low-pressure area** (L) forms. Cold air sinks; at the ground a **high-pressure area** (H) forms. **Wind** is the compensating flow from high to low. Because of the Earth's rotation every movement is deflected (**Coriolis force**): to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. That is why in the Northern Hemisphere air flows anticlockwise into a low and clockwise out of a high.

Rising air cools, the water vapour condenses, and clouds and precipitation form. Lows therefore usually bring changeable weather, highs usually dry, sunny weather (in winter often with low stratus).

## Global circulation

The **planetary circulation** consists of three cells in each hemisphere:

1. At the equator heated air rises. This creates the **Intertropical Convergence Zone** (ITCZ) with daily heavy rainfall (zenithal rain).
2. The air flows towards the poles at high altitude, sinks at about 30° latitude and forms the **subtropical high-pressure belt**. Sinking air warms up and becomes drier, which is why the great deserts lie here (Sahara, Arabia, Australia).
3. At the ground the air flows back to the equator as **trade winds**, in the Northern Hemisphere as the north-east trades. This circulation is the **Hadley cell**.
4. In the middle latitudes (about 40° to 60°) the **westerlies** prevail with migrating lows and highs. Austria lies here: the weather usually comes from the west, changes frequently and is hard to forecast far ahead. Further towards the poles follow the **Ferrel cell** and the **polar cell** with the polar high.

The ITCZ moves north and south over the year with the zenith of the sun. This produces the **rainy seasons** and **dry seasons** of the seasonally wet tropics. The **monsoon** in South and South-East Asia is a seasonal reversal of wind: in summer the heat low over the continent draws in moist sea air (rainy season), in winter dry air blows from the land to the sea.

## Climate zones and the Köppen classification

In simplified form, **solar climate zones** are distinguished by insolation: tropics, subtropics, temperate zone, cold zone (polar zone). The most widely used scientific classification is the **effective climate classification of Köppen and Geiger**, which links temperature and precipitation to vegetation:

| Main climate | Criterion | Vegetation | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A** tropical | all months above 18 °C | rainforest, savanna | Manaus, Lagos |
| **B** dry | evaporation exceeds precipitation | desert, steppe | Cairo, Riyadh |
| **C** warm temperate | coldest month between −3 °C and 18 °C | deciduous forest, sclerophyllous vegetation | London, Rome |
| **D** boreal (snow climate) | coldest month below −3 °C, warmest above 10 °C | coniferous forest (taiga) | Moscow, Winnipeg |
| **E** polar | warmest month below 10 °C | tundra, ice | Greenland, Antarctica |

Further letters describe the distribution of precipitation (f: always moist, s: dry summer, w: dry winter) and summer temperature (a: hot, b: warm, c: cool). The Mediterranean countries have a **Csa** climate, that is hot, dry summers. Austria's lowlands lie in the transition zone between the **Cfb** and **Dfb** climates, depending on the data set and reference period; in the mountains follow **Dfc** and at the highest altitudes **ET** (tundra climate). With warming the boundaries are shifting: newer analyses already assign parts of eastern Austria to a climate with hot summers (Cfa).

## Reading climate diagrams

A **climate diagram** (after Walter and Lieth) shows the long-term monthly means of a station: **temperature** as a red line (left axis in °C) and **precipitation** as a blue line or bars (right axis in mm). The header gives the station, its altitude, the mean annual temperature and the annual precipitation total.

The axes are scaled so that **10 °C corresponds to 20 mm** of precipitation. This lets you read the water balance directly:

- If the precipitation curve lies **above** the temperature curve, the month is **humid**, because more precipitation falls than can evaporate.
- If it lies **below**, the month is **arid**.

Precipitation above 100 mm is usually compressed at a scale of 1:10 and shaded dark (perhumid). For stations in the Southern Hemisphere the diagram often starts with July so that summer is in the middle.

Procedure for the analysis:

1. Determine the **location**: station, latitude, altitude.
2. Describe the **temperature**: annual mean, warmest and coldest month, **annual range** (difference). A small range indicates the tropics or proximity to the sea, a large one a continental location. If the maximum is in January, the station is in the Southern Hemisphere.
3. Describe the **precipitation**: annual total, distribution, rainy and dry seasons.
4. Count the **humid and arid months**.
5. Assign the **climate zone** and derive possible vegetation and land use.

| Pattern in the diagram | Interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| temperature around 25 °C all year, every month above 150 mm | ever-wet tropics, tropical rainforest |
| high temperature, one rainy season and a long dry season | seasonally wet tropics, savanna |
| precipitation curve below the temperature curve all year | desert |
| hot, arid summers, mild, humid winters | Mediterranean climate, sclerophyllous vegetation |
| annual range of 20 °C or more, winters below 0 °C, summer rain | continental climate, for example Eastern Europe |

## Weather phenomena

- **Fronts**: at a **warm front** warm air slowly glides up over cold air; there is long-lasting steady rain. At a **cold front** cold air pushes under warm air and lifts it quickly; showers, thunderstorms, gusts and a sharp drop in temperature follow. When the cold front catches up with the warm front, an **occlusion** forms.
- **Föhn**: moist air rises on the windward side of a mountain range, cools (after cloud formation by only about 0.5 °C per 100 m) and rains out. On the leeward side it descends dry and warms by about 1 °C per 100 m. The result is a warm, dry downslope wind. In Austria the south föhn in Tyrol and Salzburg and the north föhn in Carinthia are well known.
- **Inversion**: normally it gets colder with altitude. During an inversion, cold air lies in basins and valleys beneath warmer air. Pollutants accumulate in the ground layer, typically in winter in the Klagenfurt Basin or the Graz Basin (particulate matter).
- **Thunderstorms and hail**: in warm, humid, unstably layered air, tall thunderclouds (cumulonimbus) form. In summer Austria is one of the regions of Central Europe with the most thunderstorms and hail, especially the Alpine foreland and Styria.
- **Tropical cyclones** (hurricane in the Atlantic, typhoon in the western Pacific, cyclone in the Indian Ocean) form over seas with a water temperature of at least about 26.5 °C and draw their energy from the condensation of evaporated water. Over land they weaken rapidly.

Weather forecasts are based on **numerical weather models**: supercomputers solve the physical equations of the atmosphere on a three-dimensional grid. In Austria **GeoSphere Austria** (ZAMG until 2022) is the national weather service.

## Climate change

### Observations

- According to the WMO, the global mean temperature in 2024 was about **1.55 °C** above the pre-industrial level (1850 to 1900). 2024 was therefore the warmest year since records began.
- The **Alpine region** is warming about twice as fast as the global average. According to GeoSphere Austria, 2024 was the warmest year on record in Austria, in the lowlands about 3.1 °C warmer than the 1961 to 1990 mean.
- Glaciers and permafrost are shrinking, sea level is rising through the thermal expansion of water and the melting of glaciers and ice sheets, and the oceans are acidifying through dissolved CO₂.

### Consequences

| Area | Consequences worldwide | Consequences in Austria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| extreme weather | more heatwaves, droughts and heavy rain | more hot days, heavy rain and floods (for example September 2024) |
| water | water shortage in dry regions, glacier melt threatens water supply in high mountains | glacier retreat, less snow at low altitudes, drought in the east |
| sea level | threat to coastal cities and low-lying island states (Bangladesh, Maldives) | no direct consequences |
| agriculture | crop losses in many tropical regions | longer growing season, but drought and hail damage, new pests |
| ecosystems | coral bleaching, species extinction | spruce forests suffer from drought and bark beetles, species move to higher altitudes |
| society | climate-related migration, conflicts over water | heat stress in cities, pressure on winter tourism |

### Responses

- **Mitigation** reduces the causes: phasing out fossil fuels, energy efficiency, expanding renewable energy, protecting forests. In the **Paris Agreement** (2015) states committed to keeping warming well below 2 °C and to pursuing efforts towards 1.5 °C. Under the European Climate Law, the EU aims to be climate-neutral by 2050.
- **Adaptation** reduces the damage: flood protection and retention areas, heat-adapted urban planning (greenery, shading, cooling surfaces), drought-resistant mixed forests instead of spruce monocultures, early warning systems, adapted crop varieties. Austria has a national **strategy for adaptation to climate change** for this.

How strong warming will be by 2100 depends mainly on future emissions. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (**IPCC**) summarises the state of research in its assessment reports (the sixth was published from 2021 to 2023).
