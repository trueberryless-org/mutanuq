The Earth's surface is not rigid. Continents move a few centimetres per year, oceans open and close, mountains are folded up and worn down again. These processes are driven by the heat in the Earth's interior and explain where earthquakes and volcanoes occur, how the Alps were formed and where raw materials are found.

## Layers of the Earth

The Earth has a mean radius of about 6371 km. Its structure is known from the propagation of **seismic waves**: waves change speed and direction at boundaries between materials, and shear waves (S waves) cannot pass through liquid material.

| Layer | Depth | State and composition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Crust** | continental about 30 to 70 km, oceanic about 5 to 10 km | solid; continental crust granitic (lighter), oceanic crust basaltic (denser) |
| **Mantle** | down to about 2900 km | solid, but plastically deformable over long periods; silicate rock (peridotite) |
| **Outer core** | 2900 to about 5100 km | liquid; iron and nickel; its movements generate the Earth's magnetic field |
| **Inner core** | 5100 to 6371 km | solid because of the high pressure; iron and nickel, temperature about 5000 to 6000 °C |

For plate tectonics a second, mechanical division matters more: the **lithosphere** (crust and uppermost mantle, about 100 km thick) is rigid and broken into plates. Below it lies the **asthenosphere**, a partly plastic zone of the upper mantle on which the plates move.

## Plate tectonics

In 1912 Alfred Wegener proposed the theory of **continental drift**. His evidence was the matching coastlines of South America and Africa, identical rocks and fossils on continents that are now separated, and traces of glaciation in regions that are now tropical. He assumed a supercontinent **Pangaea** that broke apart about 200 million years ago. He could not explain the driving force. Only the exploration of the ocean floors in the 1950s and 1960s (mid-ocean ridges, symmetrical magnetic stripe patterns, oceanic crust that becomes older away from the ridges) led to today's **theory of plate tectonics**.

The lithosphere consists of seven large plates (Pacific, North American, South American, Eurasian, African, Indo-Australian, Antarctic) and numerous smaller ones. They are driven mainly by **slab pull** (the heavy, sinking plate pulls the rest along), **ridge push** at the mid-ocean ridges and **convection currents** in the mantle.

### Plate boundaries

| Boundary type | Movement | Consequences | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **divergent** (constructive) | plates move apart | magma rises, new crust forms; mid-ocean ridge, rift valley | Mid-Atlantic Ridge with Iceland, East African Rift |
| **convergent: subduction** | an oceanic plate sinks beneath another | ocean trench, volcanic chains, strong earthquakes | Andes, Japan, Mariana Trench |
| **convergent: collision** | two continental plates collide | fold mountains, earthquakes, hardly any volcanism | Himalayas, Alps |
| **transform fault** (conservative) | plates slide past each other | strong earthquakes, no crust formation | San Andreas Fault (California), North Anatolian Fault |

During subduction the sinking, water-bearing plate partly melts at a depth of about 100 km. The magma rises and forms volcanic chains parallel to the ocean trench. The **Pacific Ring of Fire** around the Pacific is such a zone: most of the Earth's active volcanoes lie there, and most strong earthquakes occur there.

**Hotspots**, in contrast, lie in the middle of a plate: a stationary upwelling of hot mantle material (_mantle plume_) melts through the plate moving above it. This produces a chain of increasingly older volcanic islands such as Hawaii.

## Earthquakes

An **earthquake** occurs when stresses in the crust are released suddenly along a fracture surface (fault). The point inside the Earth is called the **hypocentre** (focus), the point directly above it on the surface the **epicentre**. Waves spread out from there: fast compressional waves (P waves), slower shear waves (S waves) and, at the surface, the destructive surface waves. The epicentre can be located from the time difference between P and S waves at several measuring stations (seismographs).

### Magnitude and intensity

| | Magnitude | Intensity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| measures | the **energy** released by the earthquake | the **effects** at a location |
| scales | Richter scale (local magnitude, 1935), today mostly **moment magnitude** $M_w$ | **EMS-98** (European Macroseismic Scale, 12 levels), Mercalli scale |
| value per earthquake | one value | depends on distance, ground and building construction |

Magnitude scales are **logarithmic**: one step higher means about ten times the amplitude of the trace and about **32 times the energy**. An earthquake of magnitude 7 therefore releases about $32^2 \approx 1000$ times as much energy as one of magnitude 5. The strongest earthquake ever recorded was the Valdivia earthquake in Chile in 1960 with $M_w = 9.5$.

The consequences of an earthquake depend less on its magnitude than on population density, building construction and preparedness. **Tsunamis** occur when a submarine earthquake suddenly lifts or lowers the sea floor; the wave is low and fast in the open ocean and only builds up at the coast (Indian Ocean 2004, Tōhoku in Japan 2011).

Earthquakes in Austria are mostly weak. Areas with higher seismic hazard are the Vienna Basin, the Mur-Mürz valley and the Inn valley. Earthquakes are recorded by the Austrian Seismological Service of **GeoSphere Austria**. Seismic hazard is taken into account in the building standard Eurocode 8 (ÖNORM EN 1998).

## Volcanism

Volcanoes form where **magma** (molten rock) reaches the surface; at the surface it is called **lava**. Its behaviour depends mainly on the silica content (SiO₂) of the magma:

| Volcano type | Magma | Eruption | Shape | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Shield volcano** | basaltic, low in SiO₂, runny, gases escape easily | effusive: calm lava flows | flat, very wide | Mauna Loa (Hawaii), Iceland |
| **Stratovolcano** (composite volcano) | andesitic to rhyolitic, rich in SiO₂, viscous, gas-rich | explosive: ash clouds, pyroclastic flows | steep cone of layers of lava and ash | Vesuvius, Fuji, Mount St. Helens |

Stratovolcanoes typically lie above subduction zones and are more dangerous because gas pressure builds up inside them. Particularly deadly are **pyroclastic flows** (hot avalanches of gas and ash, which made the eruption of Vesuvius in AD 79 so devastating for Pompeii) and **lahars** (mudflows of ash and water). Large eruptions can cool the global climate because sulphur aerosols in the stratosphere reflect sunlight (Pinatubo 1991).

Despite the danger, volcanic areas are densely populated: volcanic soils are very fertile, and the Earth's heat is used for **geothermal energy** (Iceland covers most of its space heating this way). In Austria the extinct volcanoes of the **Styrian Volcano Land** (Riegersburg) and the Pauliberg in Burgenland are evidence of earlier volcanism.

## Mountain building: the Alps

The Alps are young **fold mountains**. They were formed by the convergence of the **African plate** (more precisely the Adriatic microplate) and the **Eurasian plate**. From the Cretaceous onwards, the ocean in between (the Penninic Ocean, part of the Tethys) was subducted. From the Tertiary onwards the continents collided: rock masses were folded, broken and thrust over each other as **nappes** for many kilometres. The uplift continues (about 1 mm per year in parts of the Eastern Alps), while glaciers, rivers and frost wear the mountains down at the same time.

The structure of the Eastern Alps appears in Austria as three longitudinal zones:

| Zone | Rocks | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Northern Limestone Alps** | limestone and dolomite, deposits of a former sea; karstified with caves and high plateaus | Dachstein, Totes Gebirge, Rax, Karwendel |
| **Central Alps** | crystalline rocks: gneiss, mica schist, granite; the highest peaks | Hohe Tauern with the Grossglockner (3798 m), Ötztal Alps |
| **Southern Limestone Alps** | limestone and dolomite south of the Periadriatic Seam | Karawanken, Carnic Alps (border with Italy and Slovenia) |

Between the Limestone Alps and the Central Alps lies the **Greywacke Zone** with slates and ore deposits (the Erzberg in Styria). This structure explains the landforms: limestone forms sheer walls and karst plateaus, crystalline rocks rather jagged ridges with glaciers.

## The rock cycle

Rocks are divided into three groups by their origin, which change into one another in the **rock cycle**:

| Group | Formation | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Igneous rocks** | solidification of magma: slowly at depth (intrusive, coarse-grained) or quickly at the surface (extrusive, fine-grained) | granite, basalt |
| **Sedimentary rocks** | weathering, erosion, deposition and consolidation of material, or precipitation | sandstone, limestone, conglomerate, coal |
| **Metamorphic rocks** | transformation of existing rocks by high pressure and high temperature, without melting | gneiss, marble, schist |

Rocks at the surface **weather** (physically through frost, chemically through solution) and are **eroded** (by water, ice, wind). The material is deposited and consolidated into sedimentary rock. If plate movement carries it to depth, it is transformed into metamorphic rock or melts into magma, from which igneous rocks form again. Mountain building brings it back to the surface, and the cycle begins anew.
