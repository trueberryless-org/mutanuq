How many people live on Earth, how old they are and where they migrate to influences almost every other area of geography: the demand for food, housing and energy, the financing of pensions, the labour market and the development of cities. **Population geography** describes these structures and explains how they change.

## Key indicators

| Indicator | Calculation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Birth rate** (crude birth rate) | live births per year per 1000 inhabitants (‰) | simple, but depends on the age structure |
| **Death rate** | deaths per year per 1000 inhabitants (‰) | high even in old societies with good care |
| **Rate of natural increase** | birth rate − death rate | natural balance without migration |
| **Total fertility rate** (TFR) | average number of children per woman at current age-specific birth rates | independent of the age structure; about **2.1** children keep a population constant in the long term without migration (**replacement level**) |
| **Life expectancy** at birth | average lifespan at current probabilities of death | indicator of health care and standard of living |
| **Infant mortality** | deaths in the first year of life per 1000 live births | very sensitive development indicator |
| **Population density** | inhabitants per km² | spatial distribution |
| **Net migration** | immigration − emigration | population change through migration |

**Population change** is made up of natural increase and net migration. With constant growth of p % per year, a population doubles after about $70 / p$ years (rule of thumb from $\ln 2 \approx 0.69$). A country with 2 % growth therefore doubles its population in about 35 years.

## Development of world population

| Year | World population |
| --- | --- |
| around 1804 | 1 billion |
| 1927 | 2 billion |
| 1960 | 3 billion |
| 1974 | 4 billion |
| 1987 | 5 billion |
| 1999 | 6 billion |
| 2011 | 7 billion |
| 15 November 2022 | 8 billion (UN estimate) |

Humanity needed its entire history for the first billion, but only 12 to 15 years for each billion since 1960. The cause was not a rise in births but the **decline in mortality** through hygiene, medicine and better nutrition.

According to the United Nations **World Population Prospects 2024**, about **8.2 billion** people lived on Earth in 2024. World population continues to grow, but ever more slowly: the global fertility rate was about 2.25 children per woman in 2024 (1990: 3.1). The UN expects a peak of about **10.3 billion in the mid-2080s**, followed by a slight decline to about 10.2 billion by 2100. Growth now takes place almost only in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia. In 63 countries, including China, Germany and Japan, population has already passed its peak. Since 2023 **India** has been the most populous country on Earth, ahead of China.

## The demographic transition model

The **demographic transition model** describes how birth and death rates change as a country develops from an agricultural to an industrial and service society. It was derived from the development of European countries.

![Demographic transition model with five stages: birth rate and death rate fall with a time lag, and the population grows strongly in between](/images/geography/demographic_transition_en.svg)

| Stage | Birth rate | Death rate | Growth | Causes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1 high stationary** | high | high, strongly fluctuating | low | epidemics, famines, wars; children as workers and provision for old age |
| **2 early expanding** | high | falls rapidly | rises strongly | hygiene, vaccinations, better nutrition |
| **3 late expanding** | falls | continues to fall slowly | very high, then decreasing | urbanisation, schooling, equal rights for women, contraception |
| **4 low stationary** | falls sharply | low | decreasing | children become more expensive, pension systems replace the family as provision for old age |
| **5 declining** (post-transitional) | low | low, rises slightly through ageing | low, zero or negative | prosperity, late parenthood, individualisation |

The gap between the two curves in stages 2 and 3 is called the **population gap** (demographic gap): this is when the population grows fastest. In Europe the transition took roughly from 1800 to 1950. Many countries of the Global South are going through it **faster** today, because medicine and vaccinations could be adopted from outside, so the death rate fell abruptly. The model describes but does not predict: it states neither the duration of the stages nor the level of growth, and it does not take migration into account.

## Reading population pyramids

A **population pyramid** (age pyramid) shows the **age structure** of a population: the vertical axis shows the age groups, horizontally the males to the left and the females to the right, as absolute numbers or as a share of the total population.

![Three basic shapes of age structure: pyramid for growing, bell for stagnant and urn for shrinking populations](/images/geography/population_pyramids_en.svg)

| Shape | Characteristics | Population | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Pyramid** | wide base, narrow top | growing, high birth and death rates | Niger, Nigeria |
| **Bell** | evenly wide, narrowing only in old age | stagnant, births at replacement level | France, USA (approximately) |
| **Urn** | narrow base, wide middle | shrinking and ageing, few births | Japan, Italy, Germany, Austria |

To interpret a pyramid, proceed as follows:

1. Determine the **shape** and assign it to a basic type.
2. Look for **indentations** and **bulges** and explain them historically: missing births during wars and crises, the **baby boom** in the 1950s and 1960s, the drop after the introduction of the pill.
3. Check the **sex ratio**: in old age women predominate because of their higher life expectancy. A surplus of men of working age indicates labour migration (for example in the Gulf states).
4. Derive the **consequences**: many children mean demand for schools and future jobs; many old people mean demand for care and rising pension costs.

The **dependency ratio** relates the people under 15 and aged 65 and over to the 15 to 64-year-olds. It shows how many people outside the typical working age there are per person of working age.

## Migration

**Migration** is the permanent or long-term relocation of one's place of residence. According to UN estimates, about **304 million** people lived outside their country of birth in 2024, about 3.7 % of the world population. According to UNHCR, **123.2 million** people were forcibly displaced at the end of 2024, of whom about 73.5 million were internally displaced within their own country.

### Types of migration

| Criterion | Types |
| --- | --- |
| spatial | **internal migration** (within a state, for example rural-urban migration) and **international migration** |
| temporal | permanent, temporary (seasonal work), **commuting** (not migration in the strict sense) |
| decision | **voluntary** (labour, education, family migration) and **forced** (flight from war, persecution, environmental disasters) |
| scale | individual, group and mass migration |

Under the **Geneva Refugee Convention** (1951), a **refugee** is a person who is outside their country of origin owing to a well-founded fear of persecution for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion. Economic hardship or climate impacts alone do not establish refugee status.

### Push and pull factors

The model by **Everett S. Lee** (1966) explains migration through factors in the area of origin and the destination and through **intervening obstacles** in between (distance, costs, borders, laws, language).

| Push factors (repelling, in the area of origin) | Pull factors (attracting, at the destination) |
| --- | --- |
| unemployment, low wages, poverty | jobs, higher wages |
| war, persecution, oppression | security, democracy, rule of law |
| poor education and health care | education and health services |
| drought, soil erosion, natural disasters | relatives and compatriots already living there (networks) |

Migration has effects in both directions: countries of origin often lose young, well-educated people (**brain drain**) but receive **remittances**, which in many countries amount to more than development aid. Destination countries gain workers against skills shortages and face the task of integration.

## Population of Austria

| Indicator | Value | Date and source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inhabitants | 9,197,213 | 1 January 2025, Statistics Austria |
| Inhabitants | 9,219,113 | 1 January 2026, Statistics Austria (provisional) |
| Live births | 77,238 | 2024 |
| Total fertility rate | 1.31 children per woman | 2024 (2023: 1.44) |
| Life expectancy at birth | men 79.8 years, women 84.3 years | 2024 |
| Foreign nationals | 20.2 % | 1 January 2025 |
| People with a migration background | 27.8 % | annual average 2024 |

Features of population development:

- **Growth only through immigration**: the natural balance is negative; more people die than are born. The population grows because more people move in than move out, mainly from other EU states (Germany, Romania, Hungary) and through flight (Syria, Afghanistan, Ukraine).
- **Ageing**: the baby-boom cohorts of the 1950s and 1960s are retiring. The age structure is urn-shaped. This puts pressure on the pay-as-you-go pension system and increases the need for care.
- **Spatial differences**: Vienna (2,028,289 inhabitants on 1 January 2025) and the urban regions around Vienna, Graz, Linz, Salzburg and Innsbruck are growing, while peripheral regions, such as parts of Upper Styria, southern Carinthia and the Waldviertel, are shrinking as young people move away. The share of foreign nationals ranges from 11.5 % in Burgenland to 36.4 % in Vienna (1 January 2025).
