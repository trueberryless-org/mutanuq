## Money

Without money, goods would have to be exchanged directly for each other (**barter**): someone who wants bread and offers software would have to find a baker who happens to need software. Money solves this problem because everyone accepts it in return.

### Functions of money

| Function | Meaning |
| -------- | ------- |
| **Medium of exchange** (means of payment) | Money is generally accepted for goods and services. |
| **Store of value** | Money can be saved and spent later. Inflation impairs this function. |
| **Unit of account** (measure of value) | The values of different goods can be compared and added in one unit, for example in a balance sheet. |
| **Means of credit** | Money can be lent; debts are expressed in money. |

For something to work as money, it must be **generally accepted**, **scarce**, **divisible**, **durable**, easily **portable** and **hard to counterfeit**.

### Forms of money

- **Commodity money:** objects with their own value (shells, salt, livestock, later gold and silver coins).
- **Cash:** banknotes and coins. The only **legal tender** in the euro area. Euro banknotes are issued by the European Central Bank, coins by the member states.
- **Book money** (deposit money): balances on current accounts, which are used by transfer, debit card or credit card. Most money today exists only as book money.
- **Digital euro:** the ECB is preparing a digital form of central bank money which, like cash, comes from the central bank but is used electronically. Whether and when it arrives depends on EU legislation.

Today's money is **fiat money**: it is not backed by gold; its value rests on the confidence that it will remain scarce and be accepted everywhere. Because of strong price fluctuations and low acceptance, cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin (see [blockchain](/en/decentralised-systems/blockchain/)) fulfil the functions of money only to a limited extent.

### Money creation and money supply

**Central bank money** (cash and banks' balances at the central bank) is created when the central bank lends to commercial banks or buys securities. **Deposit money** is created mainly when a **commercial bank grants a loan**: it credits the amount to the customer's account and thereby creates new book money. When the loan is repaid, this money disappears again. Money creation is limited by the demand for loans, banks' capital requirements and the central bank's interest rates.

The ECB measures the **money supply** in three aggregates:

| Aggregate | Content |
| --------- | ------- |
| **M1** | currency in circulation and overnight deposits (current accounts) |
| **M2** | M1 + deposits with an agreed maturity of up to 2 years and savings deposits with a notice period of up to 3 months |
| **M3** | M2 + short-term marketable instruments (e.g. money market fund shares) |

## Inflation

**Inflation** is a sustained increase in the general price level. When prices rise, the same amount of money buys less: the **purchasing power** of money falls. If only the price of individual goods rises, for example coffee after a crop failure, that is not yet inflation.

### Measurement: CPI and HICP

**Statistics Austria** measures inflation monthly with the **consumer price index** (CPI, German VPI). It is based on a **basket** of several hundred goods and services bought by an average Austrian household (food, rent, energy, fuel, clothing, mobile and internet tariffs, package holidays ...). Each item is weighted according to its share of spending; the weights come from the household budget survey and are updated regularly. Prices are collected throughout Austria.

The index refers to a **base year** that is given the value 100 (e.g. CPI 2020 = 100). An index value of 129.5 means that the basket costs 29.5 % more than in the base year. The **inflation rate** is the change in the index compared with the previous year or the same month of the previous year:

$$
\text{inflation rate} = \left(\frac{\text{index}_{\text{new}}}{\text{index}_{\text{old}}} - 1\right) \cdot 100\,\%
$$

**Example:** if the annual average CPI rises from 125.0 to 129.5, the inflation rate is $\left(\frac{129.5}{125.0} - 1\right) \cdot 100\,\% = 3.6\,\%$.

| Index | Purpose |
| ----- | ------- |
| **CPI** (consumer price index, VPI) | national index, basis for **indexation clauses** (rents, maintenance, contracts), wage negotiations and the annual pension adjustment |
| **HICP** (harmonised index of consumer prices, HVPI) | according to an EU-wide uniform method so that inflation can be compared between countries; basis of ECB monetary policy. It also includes, for example, the spending of foreign tourists in Austria. |
| **Mini and micro basket** | price development of the weekly or daily shop; closer to "perceived" inflation |

Inflation in Austria (CPI, annual average) was 8.6 % in 2022, 7.8 % in 2023, 2.9 % in 2024 and 3.6 % in 2025. In 2022 and 2023 it was driven mainly by the sharp rise in energy prices after the Russian attack on Ukraine, in 2025 among other things by the rise in electricity prices after the electricity price cap expired.

**Perceived inflation** often differs from measured inflation because people notice frequently bought goods (food, fuel) more than rarely bought ones (electronics, which often become cheaper), and because every household has its own personal basket.

### Causes

| Type | Cause | Example |
| ---- | ----- | ------- |
| **Demand-pull inflation** | aggregate demand exceeds supply (consumer boom, high government spending, strong export demand) | catch-up consumption after the COVID lockdowns in 2021 |
| **Cost-push inflation** | production costs rise and are passed on to customers: energy and commodity prices, wages, taxes | energy price shock of 2022 |
| **Imported inflation** | imported goods become more expensive, for example because of a weaker euro | oil is traded in US dollars |
| **Monetary inflation** | the money supply grows faster than the volume of goods | hyperinflation in Austria 1921/22, Zimbabwe 2008 |

The relationship between money supply and price level is described by the **quantity equation** of money:

$$
M \cdot V = P \cdot Y
$$

with the money supply $M$, the velocity of circulation $V$, the price level $P$ and the real volume of transactions $Y$. If $M$ grows faster than $Y$ and $V$ stays the same, $P$ must rise.

A **wage-price spiral** is dangerous: higher prices lead to higher wage demands, which raise firms' costs, which in turn raise prices. **Inflation expectations** are also self-reinforcing, which is why the credibility of the central bank is so important.

### Consequences

- **Losers:** savers (savings lose value), creditors, people on fixed incomes, low-income households who spend a large share on food and energy.
- **Winners:** debtors (debts lose real value), including the state as a large debtor, and owners of real assets (property, gold).
- Uncertainty makes planning and investment harder, and prices lose their signalling function.
- With a progressive tax scale, incomes that only rise with inflation slip into higher tax brackets (**bracket creep**, German _kalte Progression_). In Austria, the tax brackets have therefore been adjusted to inflation every year since 2023 (see [taxes](/en/economy_and_law/accounting/taxes/)).

Moderate, stable inflation, on the other hand, is considered unproblematic and even desirable, because it leaves room for adjustments and keeps a distance from deflation.

### Deflation and stagflation

**Deflation** is a sustained fall in the price level. It sounds tempting for consumers but is dangerous: those who expect goods to become cheaper postpone purchases. Firms sell less, cut wages and lay off staff, and demand falls further (**deflationary spiral**). At the same time, the real burden of debt rises. Japan suffered from deflation for a long time from the 1990s.

**Stagflation** is a combination of stagnation (no economic growth, rising unemployment) and inflation, as after the oil price shocks of the 1970s. It is particularly difficult for economic policy, because measures against inflation weaken the economy further.

## Interest

**Interest** is the price for leaving money with someone for a period of time. Banks pay **deposit interest** on savings and charge **lending interest** on loans. The difference (interest margin) covers their costs and the risk of loan defaults.

With **simple interest**, interest does not itself earn interest; with **compound interest** it does:

$$
Z = K_0 \cdot \frac{p}{100} \cdot n \qquad\qquad K_n = K_0 \cdot \left(1 + \frac{p}{100}\right)^n
$$

**Example:** €10,000 is invested for 5 years at 2.5 %. With simple interest, the interest is $€10{,}000 \cdot 0.025 \cdot 5 = €1{,}250$. With compound interest, the capital grows to $€10{,}000 \cdot 1.025^5 \approx €11{,}314.08$. In Austria, the bank withholds capital gains tax of 25 % on interest from savings books and accounts.

What matters is the **real interest rate**, i.e. the interest after deducting inflation:

$$
\text{real interest rate} \approx \text{nominal interest rate} - \text{inflation rate}
$$

At 2.5 % interest and 3.6 % inflation, the real interest rate is about $-1.1\,\%$. Despite the interest, the savings lose purchasing power.

## The European Central Bank

The **euro** has been the common currency since 1999, and also as cash since 2002. With Bulgaria joining on 1 January 2026, 21 of the 27 EU member states use the euro. Monetary policy in the euro area is the responsibility of the **European Central Bank** (ECB) in Frankfurt am Main.

| Term | Meaning |
| ---- | ------- |
| **Eurosystem** | the ECB and the national central banks of the euro countries, in Austria the **Oesterreichische Nationalbank** (OeNB) |
| **European System of Central Banks** (ESCB) | the ECB and the national central banks of all 27 EU member states |
| **Governing Council** | highest decision-making body: the six members of the Executive Board and the governors of the euro area national central banks; decides on the key interest rates |
| **Executive Board** | President (Christine Lagarde since 2019), Vice-President and four other members; manages day-to-day business |

The ECB is **independent**: neither governments nor EU institutions may give it instructions, and it may not finance states directly. Its primary objective under the EU Treaty is **price stability**. The ECB defines this as an inflation rate (HICP) of **2 % over the medium term**, with deviations above and below equally undesirable (symmetric target). Without prejudice to this objective, it supports the general economic policies of the EU.

The **OeNB** implements monetary policy in Austria, supplies the economy with cash, manages currency reserves, runs payment systems, compiles statistics and supervises banks together with the Financial Market Authority (FMA).

### Monetary policy instruments

**Key interest rates:** the ECB sets three interest rates at which commercial banks borrow money from or deposit money with the central bank:

| Rate | Meaning | Since 16 September 2026 |
| ---- | ------- | ----------------------- |
| **Deposit facility rate** | interest on balances that banks park overnight at the central bank; currently the most important key rate because banks hold a lot of liquidity | 2.50 % |
| **Main refinancing operations rate** | interest on the central bank's weekly loans to banks against collateral | 2.65 % |
| **Marginal lending facility rate** | interest on short-term emergency overnight loans | 2.90 % |

Other instruments:

- **Open market operations:** the central bank provides liquidity to banks against collateral (main refinancing operations, longer-term operations).
- **Minimum reserves:** banks must hold 1 % of certain deposits at the central bank.
- **Asset purchases** (_quantitative easing_, QE): the central bank buys government and corporate bonds on a large scale to lower long-term interest rates (programmes APP from 2015 and PEPP during the COVID pandemic). Since 2023 it has been reducing its holdings again (_quantitative tightening_).
- **Forward guidance:** the ECB announces how it is likely to set interest rates and thereby influences expectations.

### Effect of monetary policy

When the ECB raises key interest rates (**restrictive** monetary policy), the following happens (**transmission mechanism**):

1. Banks pass on the higher costs; loans for firms and households become more expensive, and saving becomes more attractive.
2. Investment and credit-financed consumption (such as buying a flat) decline.
3. Higher interest rates make the euro more attractive to investors; the euro strengthens and imports become cheaper.
4. Aggregate demand falls and price pressures ease.

With an **expansionary** monetary policy (rate cut), everything works the other way round: it is meant to boost the economy and inflation. The full effect only occurs with a delay of one to two years.

Recent years show both directions: from 2014 to 2022 the deposit rate was negative (at times -0.5 %) to raise inflation, which was very low at the time. When inflation rose sharply in 2022, the ECB raised rates to 4 % by 2023. As inflation fell, it gradually cut the deposit rate to 2 % in 2024 and 2025. After a renewed energy price shock caused by the conflict in the Middle East, it raised it to 2.25 % in June 2026 and to 2.50 % in September 2026.
