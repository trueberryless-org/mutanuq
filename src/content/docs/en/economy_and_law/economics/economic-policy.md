**Economic policy** covers all measures by which the state influences the course of the economy. In Austria, it is shaped mainly by the federal government and the National Council, the federal provinces and municipalities, the social partners and the EU. Monetary policy is the responsibility of the European Central Bank (see [money and monetary policy](/en/economy_and_law/economics/money-and-monetary-policy/)).

## Goals: the magic polygon

The German Stability and Growth Act of 1967 named four macroeconomic goals, known as the **magic square**. Extended by two more goals, people speak of the **magic hexagon** (polygon):

| Goal | Indicator | Guideline value |
| ---- | --------- | --------------- |
| **adequate, steady economic growth** | change in real GDP | about 1.5 to 2 % per year |
| **price stability** | inflation rate (CPI or HICP) | 2 % (ECB target) |
| **high employment** (full employment) | unemployment rate | as low as possible, close to the natural rate |
| **external equilibrium** | current account | roughly balanced |
| **fair distribution of income and wealth** | Gini coefficient, wage share | |
| **environmental protection** (sustainable economy) | greenhouse gas emissions, resource consumption | climate neutrality (EU by 2050) |

The polygon is called "magic" because not all goals can be fully achieved at the same time. Some goals support each other (**complementary goals**: growth creates jobs), others conflict (**goal conflict**):

- **Price stability and full employment:** a policy that stimulates demand lowers unemployment but often increases inflation. Conversely, an interest rate rise against inflation also dampens growth and employment. This relationship is described by the **Phillips curve**.
- **Growth and environmental protection:** without technological change, more production means more energy and resource consumption.

## Gross domestic product

**Gross domestic product** (GDP) is the market value of all **final products** (goods and services) produced **within the country** in one year. Intermediate goods, i.e. goods that go into other goods (flour for bread, chips for the laptop), are not counted, otherwise they would be counted twice. GDP is the most important indicator of a country's economic output. In Austria, Statistics Austria calculates it in the **national accounts**.

### Three methods of calculation

| Method | Question | Calculation |
| ------ | -------- | ----------- |
| **Production approach** | Where was it produced? | sum of the **value added** (output minus intermediate consumption) of all industries plus taxes on products minus subsidies on products |
| **Expenditure approach** | What was it used for? | $GDP = C + I + G + (X - M)$: private consumption + investment + government consumption + exports minus imports |
| **Income approach** | Who received the income? | compensation of employees + business and property income (+ depreciation and taxes on production and imports minus subsidies) |

In theory, all three approaches lead to the same result, because every euro spent is at the same time someone else's income.

**Example of value added:** a farmer sells wheat for €100 to a mill. The mill sells the flour for €250 to a bakery, which turns it into bread sold to consumers for €600. The values added are €100, €150 and €350, a total of €600. This corresponds exactly to the value of the final product, the bread.

### Nominal, real, per capita

- **Nominal GDP** is calculated at the prices of the respective year. It rises even if only prices rise.
- **Real GDP** removes price changes (at the constant prices of a base year). Only real GDP shows whether more has actually been produced. **Economic growth** is the change in real GDP.
- **GDP per capita** divides GDP by the population and is suitable for comparisons between countries.

Approximately: real growth ≈ nominal growth minus price increase. Austria's nominal GDP in 2025 was around **€518 billion** (+3.6 %), real growth was **0.7 %**, and GDP per capita was around €56,300. In the two preceding years (2023 and 2024), the economy had shrunk in real terms.

**Gross national income** (GNI), by contrast, counts the incomes of all **residents** (national concept), even if earned abroad, and leaves out incomes of foreigners earned in the country.

### Limits of GDP

GDP is not a measure of prosperity or quality of life:

- Unpaid work (housework, raising children, caring for relatives, volunteering), leisure and undeclared work are not included.
- Environmental damage is not deducted. Repairing damage after a storm even increases GDP.
- The distribution of income is not taken into account.

Complementary measures are the UN's **Human Development Index** (income, life expectancy, education) and in Austria Statistics Austria's indicator set **"How's Austria?"**, which looks at material prosperity, quality of life and the environment together.

## Business cycle

The economy does not grow evenly but in waves. These fluctuations around the long-term growth trend are called the **business cycle**. A cycle typically lasts several years and has four phases:

| Phase | Output, capacity use | Employment | Prices, interest rates | Mood |
| ----- | -------------------- | ---------- | ---------------------- | ---- |
| **Upswing** (expansion) | rises, capacities are better utilised | rises | rise slightly | optimistic, investment increases |
| **Boom** | capacity limit reached | full employment, shortage of skilled workers | rise significantly | euphoric, supply bottlenecks |
| **Downswing** (recession) | falls | falls with a delay | price increases ease, interest rates fall | pessimistic, investment is postponed |
| **Trough** (depression) | low, much unused capacity | high unemployment | stagnate or fall | depressed |

A **technical recession** is when real GDP shrinks compared with the previous quarter in two consecutive quarters. A **depression** is a particularly deep and long downturn, such as the Great Depression from 1929.

Causes of cyclical fluctuations are swings in investment and consumption, external shocks (pandemic, energy crisis, wars), interest rate changes and moods (expectations are self-reinforcing). In Austria, forecasts are produced by **WIFO** (Austrian Institute of Economic Research) and **IHS** (Institute for Advanced Studies). **Economic indicators** help:

- **Leading indicators** show future developments: incoming orders, sentiment surveys among firms and consumers (purchasing managers' index), building permits, share prices.
- **Coincident indicators** show the current situation: production, capacity utilisation, sales.
- **Lagging indicators** react with a delay: unemployment rate, inflation, tax revenue.

## Fiscal policy

**Fiscal policy** means using public revenue and expenditure to influence the economy.

**Countercyclical (demand-side) fiscal policy** according to John Maynard Keynes:

- In a **downswing**, the state increases spending (infrastructure projects, investment premiums, short-time work) or cuts taxes, even if it has to borrow to do so (**deficit spending**). The additional demand is meant to boost the economy.
- In an **upswing**, the state should save and repay debt to prevent overheating.

Part of this happens without any decision through **automatic stabilisers**: in a crisis, tax revenue falls and spending on unemployment benefit rises; in a boom it is the other way round.

Criticism: measures often take effect too late (time for recognition, decision and impact), in an upswing the state rarely really saves, so that debt keeps rising, and high government borrowing can raise interest rates and crowd out private investment (**crowding out**).

**Supply-side economic policy** (Milton Friedman, monetarism), by contrast, relies on better conditions for firms: lower taxes and non-wage labour costs, less bureaucracy, deregulation, investment in education and research, and a stable monetary policy.

## Unemployment

### Types

| Type | Cause | Example | Measures |
| ---- | ----- | ------- | -------- |
| **Frictional unemployment** (search unemployment) | temporary, when changing jobs | a graduate looking for their first job | quick placement, job portals |
| **Seasonal unemployment** | seasonal fluctuations | construction and tourism in winter or the off-season | year-round employment, working time models |
| **Cyclical unemployment** | insufficient aggregate demand in a downswing | layoffs in industry during a recession | economic policy, short-time work |
| **Structural unemployment** | qualifications, industries or regions do not match demand (_mismatch_), e.g. due to technological change or relocation abroad | print workers after digitalisation | retraining, further training, regional support |

Frictional unemployment and part of structural unemployment together form the **natural rate of unemployment**, which remains even during a boom.

### Measurement: AMS and Eurostat

There are two unemployment rates in Austria, which must not be confused:

| | National method (AMS) | International method (ILO/Eurostat) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Data source | register data: unemployed persons registered with the **Public Employment Service** (AMS) and dependent employees registered with the umbrella association of social insurance institutions | **sample survey** (Statistics Austria's microcensus labour force survey) |
| Unemployed is anyone who ... | is registered as unemployed with the AMS (people in training courses are counted separately) | is aged 15 to 74, did not work even one hour in the reference week, is actively looking for work and is available within two weeks |
| Calculation | $\frac{\text{registered unemployed}}{\text{registered unemployed} + \text{dependent employees}}$ | $\frac{\text{unemployed}}{\text{labour force (employed including self-employed + unemployed)}}$ |
| Use | current monthly data for Austria | comparison between EU member states |
| Value 2025 | **7.4 %** (around 318,000 people on annual average) | around **5.7 %** |

The international rate is lower because just one hour of work per week counts as employment and because the self-employed are included in the denominator. In 2025, Austria was slightly below the EU average of around 6 % according to the international method.

The consequences of unemployment are loss of income and risk of poverty, psychological strain, loss of skills (especially with **long-term unemployment** of more than one year), lower tax and contribution revenue and higher government spending.

## State budget and public debt

Every year the federal government adopts a **budget**: with the **Federal Finance Act**, the National Council approves revenue and expenditure for the following year, embedded in a **federal financial framework** for four years. Provinces and municipalities have their own budgets.

- The most important **revenue** comes from taxes (above all VAT, wage and income tax, corporation tax, see [taxes](/en/economy_and_law/accounting/taxes/)) and social security contributions. The ratio of all taxes and social contributions to GDP is called the **tax ratio**. In Austria it is over 40 %, which is high by EU standards.
- The largest **expenditure** goes on social security (above all pensions), health, education and interest.

If expenditure exceeds revenue, there is a **budget deficit**, which is financed by new borrowing. The sum of all debts is the **public debt**.

The EU rules (Maastricht criteria, Stability and Growth Pact) require

- the **deficit** to be at most **3 % of GDP** and
- **public debt** to be at most **60 % of GDP** or to fall sufficiently quickly.

Since the reform of the EU fiscal rules in 2024, each state also agrees a multi-year path for its net expenditure. In 2025, Austria had a deficit of **4.2 % of GDP** (around €21.5 billion) and debt of around **€418 billion**, just over 80 % of GDP. Because of the excessive deficit, the Council of the EU opened an **excessive deficit procedure** against Austria on 8 July 2025. Austria must bring the deficit back below 3 % by 2028 and report on its measures every six months. Compliance with the fiscal rules is monitored in Austria by the **Fiscal Advisory Council**.

High debt is problematic because **interest payments** take up a growing share of the budget, leaving less room for investment, education or crisis aid, because rating agencies may downgrade creditworthiness, and because future generations bear the burden.

## The Austrian social security system

Austria is a **welfare state**: the state protects people against the major risks of life and partly evens out income differences. At its heart is **social insurance**, a **compulsory insurance** for everyone in employment.

| Branch | Benefits |
| ------ | -------- |
| **Health insurance** | medical treatment, hospital, medicines (against a prescription fee), sick pay, maternity benefits; dependants are often insured free of charge |
| **Accident insurance** | accidents at work and on the way to work, occupational diseases, rehabilitation, accident pensions; prevention; financed by the employer alone |
| **Pension insurance** | old-age, disability and survivors' pensions, rehabilitation |
| **Unemployment insurance** | unemployment benefit (basic amount 55 % of daily net income), then unemployment assistance; training courses; administered by the AMS |

Since 2020 there have been five social insurance institutions: the **Austrian Health Insurance Fund** (ÖGK, for dependent employees), the **Social Insurance Institution for the Self-Employed** (SVS), the **Insurance Institution for Public Servants, Railways and Mining** (BVAEB), the **Pension Insurance Institution** (PVA) and the **General Accident Insurance Institution** (AUVA). They are organised in the **umbrella association of Austrian social insurance institutions** and are self-governed by representatives of the insured and the employers.

Social insurance is financed by contributions from employees and employers, calculated as a percentage of gross income up to the maximum contribution basis (see [payroll](/en/economy_and_law/accounting/payroll/)), and by federal subsidies. Pensions are financed on a **pay-as-you-go** basis: the contributions of today's workers pay the pensions of today's pensioners (**generational contract**). Because people live longer and fewer children are born, there are fewer and fewer contributors per pensioner. The statutory retirement age is 65 for men. For women, it has been raised gradually from 60 to 65 since 2024 (until 2033).

Other social benefits are **family allowance**, **childcare allowance**, **long-term care allowance** (in seven levels depending on care needs), the provinces' **social assistance** as a last safety net and the equalisation supplement for low pensions.
