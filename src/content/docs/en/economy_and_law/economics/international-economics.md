No country produces everything itself. Austria imports oil, natural gas, smartphones and bananas and exports machinery, vehicle parts, medicines and holidays. **International economics** studies these relationships between economies. It is particularly important for a small, open country like Austria: exports of goods and services amount to more than half of GDP.

## Why countries trade

- **Availability:** some goods are not available at home or not in sufficient quantities (raw materials, tropical fruit).
- **Absolute cost advantages** (Adam Smith): a country can produce a good with less effort than another.
- **Comparative cost advantages** (David Ricardo, 1817): trade pays off even if one country can produce **everything** more cheaply.
- **Economies of scale:** those who produce for the world market can make larger quantities more cheaply.
- **Variety and competition:** more choice for consumers, pressure on domestic suppliers to become more efficient.

### Comparative advantage

Two countries produce machines and wine. The labour required in hours is:

| | 1 machine | 1 unit of wine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Country A | 10 h | 20 h |
| Country B | 30 h | 30 h |

Country A is more productive in **both** goods (absolute advantage). What matters, however, are the **opportunity costs**: for one unit of wine, country A has to give up 2 machines, country B only 1 machine. Country B therefore has a **comparative advantage in wine**, country A a **comparative advantage in machines** (a machine costs A only 0.5 wine, but B 1 wine).

- **Without trade**, each country produces one unit of each good. A needs 30 hours for this, B 60 hours. Together there are 2 machines and 2 units of wine.
- **With specialisation**, A produces 3 machines in its 30 hours and B 2 units of wine in its 60 hours. Together there are 3 machines and 2 units of wine, i.e. one machine more for the same labour input.

Through exchange, both countries can consume more than without trade. This explains why the international division of labour increases prosperity overall, including for less productive countries. However, the gains are not automatically distributed evenly, and individual industries and workers can lose out.

## Austria's foreign trade

- By far the most important trading partner is **Germany** (around 30 % of goods exports), followed by Italy, the USA, Switzerland and the eastern neighbouring countries. Around two thirds of goods trade takes place within the EU.
- Important exports are machinery and equipment, motor vehicles and vehicle parts, pharmaceutical products, metals and metal goods, and wood and paper. Many exporters are specialised medium-sized firms that are world market leaders in their niche ("hidden champions").
- Important imports are energy (oil, natural gas), vehicles, electronics, chemicals and consumer goods.
- In **services**, **tourism** plays a major role: spending by foreign guests in Austria counts as a service export.

## Protectionism and free trade

**Protectionism** means protecting the domestic economy from foreign competition through trade barriers.

| Instrument | Description |
| ---------- | ----------- |
| **Tariffs** | duties on imported goods; make imports more expensive |
| **Quotas** | quantitative limits on imports |
| **Subsidies** | state grants to domestic producers, including exporters |
| **Non-tariff barriers** | different technical standards, complex approvals, rules of origin, bureaucracy |
| **Export restrictions** | e.g. on high technology or critical raw materials |

Protectionism is justified by protecting young industries (infant industry tariffs), jobs, security of supply (food, medicines, chips) or by unfair practices of other countries (dumping). The disadvantages: higher prices for consumers and for firms that import intermediate products, less competition and countermeasures by trading partners up to a **trade war**. One example is the tariffs that the USA introduced on imports from many countries in 2025. The EU and the USA then agreed on a tariff rate of at most 15 % for most EU goods.

The **World Trade Organization** (WTO), based in Geneva, works to reduce trade barriers and settles trade disputes. Important principles are **most-favoured-nation treatment** (a tariff advantage for one member applies to all) and **national treatment** (imported goods may not be treated worse than domestic ones once they have crossed the border). In addition, states and the EU conclude **free trade agreements**, for example the EU with Canada (CETA), Japan or Mercosur.

## Balance of payments

The **balance of payments** records all economic transactions between residents and the rest of the world over a period. In Austria, it is compiled by the Oesterreichische Nationalbank.

| Sub-account | Content |
| ----------- | ------- |
| **Current account** | |
| Goods account (trade balance) | goods exports minus goods imports |
| Services account | travel (tourism), transport, IT services, licence fees, consulting |
| Primary income | cross-border wages (commuters), interest, dividends |
| Secondary income | current transfers without consideration: remittances by migrant workers to their home countries, contributions to the EU, development aid |
| **Capital account** | one-off capital transfers, e.g. debt forgiveness, EU investment grants |
| **Financial account** | direct investment (buying companies, founding subsidiaries abroad), portfolio investment, loans, changes in currency reserves |

According to the principle of double-entry bookkeeping, the balance of payments always balances: a **current account surplus** (more exported than imported) means that the country builds up claims on the rest of the world to the same extent, i.e. invests abroad or lends money. A residual item ("errors and omissions") offsets recording errors. Austria usually has a surplus in the services account, mainly thanks to tourism, and in most years also in the overall current account.

## Exchange rates

The **exchange rate** is the price of one currency expressed in another. For the euro, the **indirect quotation** is used: how many units of the foreign currency do you get for 1 euro (e.g. €1 = 1.10 US dollars)?

- With **flexible exchange rates**, the rate is formed on the foreign exchange market by supply and demand. This is the case for the euro against the dollar, yen or pound.
- With **fixed exchange rates**, a country pegs its currency to another and has to defend the rate through central bank purchases and sales. The Danish krone, for example, is closely pegged to the euro.

The exchange rate depends on interest rate differentials (higher interest rates attract capital), inflation differentials, the current account, the economic situation, political stability and speculation.

**Effects:** an Austrian manufacturer sells a machine for €100,000 to the USA.

| Exchange rate | Price in US dollars | Effect |
| ------------- | ------------------- | ------ |
| €1 = 1.10 USD | 110,000 USD | |
| €1 = 1.20 USD (**appreciation** of the euro) | 120,000 USD | exports become more expensive and harder to sell; imports (e.g. oil priced in dollars) and holidays in the USA become cheaper; imported inflation falls |
| €1 = 1.00 USD (**depreciation** of the euro) | 100,000 USD | exports become cheaper and more competitive, imports more expensive; imported inflation rises |

In the long run, exchange rates should reflect **purchasing power parity**: a basket of goods should cost the same everywhere once converted. A well-known, not entirely serious measure of this is The Economist's Big Mac index.

Companies protect themselves against exchange rate risks, for example by invoicing in euros, using forward exchange contracts or producing in the sales country.

## The European single market and the euro

Austria has been a member of the European Union since **1 January 1995**. The **EU single market** has existed since 1 January 1993 and is based on the **four freedoms**:

| Freedom | Meaning | Example |
| ------- | ------- | ------- |
| **free movement of goods** | no tariffs or border controls for goods within the EU, mutual recognition of standards | a machine approved in Austria may be sold throughout the EU (CE marking) |
| **free movement of persons** | EU citizens may live, work and study in any member state | an HTL graduate takes a job in Munich |
| **freedom to provide services** | companies may offer their services throughout the EU | a Viennese IT company supports clients in Milan |
| **free movement of capital and payments** | money may flow freely across borders | buying shares in a French company, SEPA transfers |

The EU is also a **customs union**: a common tariff applies at the external borders, and the European Commission negotiates trade agreements for all member states. In the **Schengen area**, there are in principle also no checks on persons at internal borders. The **European Economic Area** (EEA) extends the single market to Norway, Iceland and Liechtenstein.

With **economic and monetary union**, Austria introduced the euro as book money in 1999 and as cash in 2002 (see [money and monetary policy](/en/economy_and_law/economics/money-and-monetary-policy/)).

| Advantages of the euro | Disadvantages of the euro |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------- |
| no exchange costs and no exchange rate risk within the euro area | no monetary policy of one's own: the key interest rate does not always suit all countries equally well |
| prices are directly comparable | countries can no longer devalue to become more competitive |
| stable, internationally important currency, lower interest rates | problems of one country (debt crisis) affect everyone |

## Globalisation

**Globalisation** is the increasing worldwide interconnection of economies, politics, culture and communication. Economically, it shows in rapidly growing world trade, international capital flows, globally active corporations and global **value chains**: a smartphone is designed in the USA, contains chips from Taiwan and South Korea and raw materials from Africa and South America, and is assembled in China or India.

Its drivers are falling transport costs (container shipping), information and communication technology, the removal of trade barriers and the opening up of countries such as China and the former Eastern bloc.

| Opportunities | Risks |
| ------------- | ----- |
| higher prosperity through specialisation, cheaper products | relocation of jobs to low-wage countries, pressure on wages and social standards |
| larger sales markets for exporters | dependence on supply chains and individual countries (energy from Russia, chips from Asia) |
| transfer of knowledge and technology, development of poorer countries (less extreme poverty worldwide) | rising inequality within countries |
| more competition and innovation | environmental burden from transport, shifting of environmental damage |
| exchange between cultures | tax avoidance by multinational corporations, concentration of power |

Crises such as the COVID pandemic, the blockage of the Suez Canal in 2021 and the Russian attack on Ukraine in 2022 have shown how vulnerable global supply chains are. Many companies therefore rely on several suppliers, higher stocks and moving production back closer to home (**nearshoring**, **reshoring**). The EU also promotes domestic production of strategically important goods, for example with the Chips Act.
