**Business administration** examines how an individual company is managed well. **Economics** looks at the economy of an entire country or of the world. A distinction is made between:

- **Microeconomics:** the behaviour of individual households and companies and price formation in individual markets (see [markets and prices](/en/economy_and_law/economics/markets-and-prices/)).
- **Macroeconomics:** aggregate quantities such as economic growth, inflation, unemployment and public debt (see [economic policy](/en/economy_and_law/economics/economic-policy/)).

The starting point of every economy is a contradiction: people's wants are almost unlimited, but the means of satisfying them are scarce. **Economic activity** means dealing with scarce means in a planned way in order to satisfy needs as well as possible.

## Needs, demand and effective demand

A **need** is the feeling of a lack, combined with the wish to remove it (hunger, the wish for a new smartphone). Needs can be classified in different ways:

| Criterion | Types | Examples |
| --------- | ----- | -------- |
| Urgency | **existential needs** (essential for life), **cultural needs** (depend on society's standard of living), **luxury needs** | food, housing; education, internet access; sports car |
| Bearer | **individual needs** (satisfied by the individual), **collective needs** (satisfied by the community) | clothing; security, roads, schools |
| Object | **material** and **immaterial** needs | car; recognition, friendship |
| Awareness | **open** (conscious) and **latent** (unconscious, e.g. awakened by advertising) needs | |

A ranking of needs is described by [Maslow's hierarchy of needs](/en/economy_and_law/leadership/#maslows-hierarchy-of-needs). The boundary between cultural and luxury needs shifts over time: a mobile phone was a luxury in the 1990s, today a smartphone is part of the normal standard of living.

A need for which purchasing power (money) is available is called **demand in the narrow sense**. When this demand actually becomes effective in the market, i.e. something is bought, it is called **effective demand**.

$$
\text{need} + \text{purchasing power} = \text{demand} \quad \xrightarrow{\text{purchase decision}} \quad \text{effective demand}
$$

## Goods

**Goods** are means that satisfy needs.

- **Free goods** are available in such large quantities that they cost nothing, for example air or sunlight.
- **Economic goods** are scarce. They have to be produced and have a price. Almost all goods are economic goods, and even goods that used to be free, such as clean air and clean water, are becoming scarce because of pollution.

Economic goods are further subdivided:

| Classification | Types |
| -------------- | ----- |
| Nature | **tangible goods** (material, e.g. a laptop), **services** (immaterial, e.g. software development, a haircut), **rights** (e.g. licences, patents) |
| Use | **consumer goods** (used directly by households) and **capital goods** or production goods (used to produce other goods, e.g. servers, machines) |
| Period of use | **non-durable goods** (used once, e.g. food, fuel) and **durable goods** (used repeatedly, e.g. a fridge) |
| Relationship | **complementary goods** (used together, e.g. printer and toner) and **substitute goods** (replace each other, e.g. butter and margarine, train and car) |

A laptop is a consumer good for a student and a capital good for a software company. What matters is the purpose for which it is used.

### Private and public goods

Two properties decide whether a good can be provided through a market:

- **Excludability:** can people who do not pay be excluded from using it?
- **Rivalry:** does one person's use restrict use by others?

| | rival | non-rival |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **excludable** | **private goods** (pizza, clothing) | **club goods** (streaming service, toll road) |
| **non-excludable** | **common goods** (fish stocks in the sea, pasture land) | **public goods** (national defence, street lighting, flood protection) |

Public goods are hardly offered by private companies because anyone can use them without paying (**free-rider problem**). They are therefore usually provided by the state and financed through taxes. Without rules, common goods are overused (**tragedy of the commons**), for example through overfishing.

## Scarcity and opportunity cost

Because goods and time are scarce, choices have to be made. Every decision for something is at the same time a renunciation of the best alternative. The benefit of this forgone alternative is called the **opportunity cost**.

**Example:** after her Matura, an HTL graduate can work as a developer for €2,800 gross a month or start a three-year degree. The opportunity cost of studying is not only tuition fees and books but above all the forgone income of around 3 · 14 · €2,800 ≈ €117,600 gross. Whether studying pays off depends on whether the later income and satisfaction make up for this.

A whole economy also has to choose: with its workers, machines and raw materials, it can only produce more of one good by giving up some of another. The **production possibility frontier** shows all combinations that are possible at full capacity. Points inside the curve mean unused resources (such as unemployment), points outside it are currently unattainable. Technical progress and investment shift the curve outwards (economic growth).

## The economic principle

The **economic principle** (efficiency principle) describes rational behaviour under scarcity. It has two forms:

- **Maximum principle:** with **given means**, the **greatest possible result** should be achieved. Example: with a marketing budget of €10,000, as many new customers as possible should be won.
- **Minimum principle:** a **given goal** should be achieved with the **smallest possible means**. Example: a website with defined features should be completed with as few development hours as possible.

The demand "the greatest possible result with the smallest possible effort", by contrast, is **nonsensical** because it cannot be met at the same time: zero effort brings no result. One quantity must always be held fixed.

Economic activity is measured with indicators such as **productivity** (output per input, e.g. units per working hour), **efficiency** (revenue divided by expenses) and **return on investment** (profit in relation to the capital employed, see [annual accounts and ratios](/en/economy_and_law/accounting/annual_accounts/)).

## Factors of production

**Factors of production** are all the means used to produce goods.

**Economic factors of production:**

| Factor | Description | Income |
| ------ | ----------- | ------ |
| **Labour** | any physical and mental activity aimed at earning income | wages or salary |
| **Land** (nature) | arable land, location, mineral resources, energy, environment | lease, rent (ground rent) |
| **Capital** | produced means of production such as machines, buildings, servers (**real capital**); money capital is only the means of financing real capital | interest |

Labour and land are **original** factors, capital is a **derived** factor because it first has to be produced using labour and land. Capital arises through **forgoing consumption**: those who save make investment possible. Today, **knowledge** (education, know-how, technology, also called human capital) is often named as a fourth factor. For a high-wage country with few raw materials such as Austria, it is the most important.

The **business factors of production** according to Erich Gutenberg are **executive labour**, **operating resources** (machines, buildings, software), **materials** (raw materials, semi-finished goods) and the **dispositive factor** (planning, organisation and control by management), which combines the other factors.

## Division of labour

**Division of labour** means that a task is not done by one person alone but by many specialists. In 1776, in "The Wealth of Nations", Adam Smith described a pin factory: ten workers, each of whom carried out only a few of the roughly 18 work steps, together produced about 48,000 pins a day. Alone, each of them would hardly have managed 20.

| Form | Description | Example |
| ---- | ----------- | ------- |
| **Emergence of occupations** | different occupations develop | farmer, smith, trader |
| **Splitting of occupations** | an occupation splits into specialist occupations | doctor into GP, surgeon, radiologist; IT specialist into front-end and back-end developer, network technician |
| **Breakdown of work** | a work process is broken down into individual steps, often on the assembly line | car manufacturing |
| **Division within a company** | division into departments | purchasing, production, sales (see [organisation](/en/economy_and_law/organisation/)) |
| **Division within the economy** | specialisation of companies and economic sectors | suppliers and manufacturers |
| **International division of labour** | specialisation of countries | chip manufacturing in Taiwan, mechanical engineering in Austria (see [international economics](/en/economy_and_law/economics/international-economics/)) |

The economy is divided into three **economic sectors**: the **primary** sector (agriculture and forestry, mining), the **secondary** sector (industry, trades, construction) and the **tertiary** sector (services such as retail, tourism, banking, IT, public administration). In Austria, around 70 % of all employed people now work in the service sector. Some economists count the information and knowledge sector as a separate **quaternary** sector.

| Advantages of the division of labour | Disadvantages of the division of labour |
| ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------- |
| higher productivity through specialisation and practice | dependence on others (supply chains, e.g. during a chip shortage) |
| use of machines becomes possible | monotonous work, alienation from the end product |
| better use of talents | one-sided strain, damage to health |
| cheaper products, higher prosperity | more coordination and transport effort |

## Circular flow model

The **circular flow model** is a simplified representation of the flows between the participants (sectors) in the economy.

### Simple circular flow

In the simplest model there are only **households** and **firms**. Households provide their factors of production (mainly labour) to firms and receive income in return. Firms produce consumer goods, which households buy with their income. Two opposing flows result:

- **Real flow:** factor services from households to firms, consumer goods from firms to households.
- **Money flow:** income from firms to households, consumer spending from households to firms.

If households spend all their income and save nothing, the circuit is closed (**stationary economy**).

### Extended circular flow

The extended model adds three sectors:

- **Banks** (capital formation): households save part of their income with banks, which lend it to firms for investment.
- **State:** levies taxes and contributions from households and firms, pays salaries to civil servants, buys goods, pays transfers (pensions, family allowance) and subsidies.
- **Rest of the world:** exports bring money into the country, imports take money out.

In a closed economy without a state, the following always holds after the fact: **saving = investment** ($S = I$), because what households do not spend on consumption is available for investment via the banks.

## Economic systems

How a society decides **what, how and for whom** to produce is governed by its **economic system**. The two theoretical basic models are:

| Feature | Free market economy | Centrally planned economy |
| ------- | ------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Planning | decentralised, by households and firms | central, by a state planning authority |
| Coordination | prices on markets (supply and demand) | quantity plans |
| Ownership of the means of production | private | state or collective |
| Goal of firms | profit maximisation | fulfilling the plan |
| Role of the state | only protection of property and contracts ("night-watchman state") | directs the entire economy |
| Strengths | efficiency, innovation, consumer sovereignty | equality of incomes, predictability |
| Weaknesses | social inequality, monopolies, economic crises, environmental damage | shortages, lack of incentives, inefficiency, restriction of freedom |
| Theorists | Adam Smith (1776) | Karl Marx (critique of capitalism) |

In reality there are only mixed forms. Austria and Germany have a **social market economy**: in principle, markets coordinate the economy, but the state intervenes to ensure social balance (social insurance, progressive income tax), competition (competition law) and environmental protection. A special feature of Austria is the **social partnership**: the Economic Chamber, the Chamber of Agriculture, the Chamber of Labour and the Trade Union Federation cooperate voluntarily, negotiate the [collective agreements](/en/economy_and_law/law/labour_law/#social-partners-and-collective-agreements) and contribute to legislation.
