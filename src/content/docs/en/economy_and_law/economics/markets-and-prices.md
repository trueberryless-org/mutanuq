A **market** is any place where supply and demand meet and goods are exchanged for money. It can be a farmers' market, an online shop, the Vienna Stock Exchange or a job portal. The **price** forms on the market and coordinates the actions of buyers and sellers without a central authority having to intervene.

| Market | What is traded | Price |
| ------ | -------------- | ----- |
| Goods market | consumer and capital goods | price of goods |
| Labour market | work | wage |
| Capital and credit market | savings, loans, securities | interest, share price |
| Foreign exchange market | foreign currencies | exchange rate |
| Property market | land, flats | purchase price, rent |

## Demand

**Demand** is the quantity of a good that buyers want and are able to buy at a certain price. According to the **law of demand**, the quantity demanded falls when the price rises, because some buyers do without the good or switch to substitutes. The **demand curve** therefore slopes down from top left to bottom right (x-axis: quantity, y-axis: price).

Apart from the price, demand depends on:

- household **income**: for **normal goods**, demand rises with income; for **inferior goods** it falls (those who earn more buy fewer cheap noodles and more branded products),
- the **prices of other goods**: if the price of a substitute (butter) rises, demand for the good (margarine) rises; if the price of a complement (printer) rises, it falls (toner),
- **preferences** (fashion, advertising, health awareness),
- **expectations** (expected price rises lead to purchases being brought forward),
- the number of buyers.

If the price changes, you **move along** the demand curve. If one of the other factors changes, the whole curve **shifts**: to the right for higher demand, to the left for lower demand.

Exceptions to the law of demand are **prestige goods** (Veblen effect: luxury watches are bought precisely because of their high price) and speculation (rising prices create the expectation of further rises).

## Supply

**Supply** is the quantity of a good that sellers want to sell at a certain price. According to the **law of supply**, the quantity supplied rises with the price, because production becomes worthwhile for more firms at higher prices. The **supply curve** therefore slopes up from bottom left to top right.

The supply curve shifts due to changes in **production costs** (wages, energy, raw materials), **technology** (automation lowers costs), the **number of suppliers**, **taxes and subsidies**, and external influences such as crop failures.

## Market equilibrium

The price at which the quantity supplied equals the quantity demanded is called the **equilibrium price**. At this price the market **clears**: everyone who wants to buy or sell at this price can do so.

- If the price is **higher**, more is supplied than demanded (**excess supply**). Suppliers are left with unsold goods and lower their prices.
- If the price is **lower**, more is demanded than supplied (**excess demand**). Queues form and suppliers raise their prices.

In this way the price moves towards equilibrium by itself. Adam Smith called this mechanism the "invisible hand" of the market.

### Worked example

For a good, the demand function is $x_D = 100 - 2p$ and the supply function $x_S = -20 + 4p$ ($p$ in euros, $x$ in units).

In equilibrium $x_D = x_S$:

$$
100 - 2p = -20 + 4p \quad \Rightarrow \quad 120 = 6p \quad \Rightarrow \quad p^* = €20, \quad x^* = 100 - 2 \cdot 20 = 60 \text{ units}
$$

| Price | Demand | Supply | Situation |
| ----- | ------ | ------ | --------- |
| €15 | 70 | 40 | excess demand of 30 units |
| €20 | 60 | 60 | equilibrium |
| €25 | 50 | 80 | excess supply of 30 units |

Many buyers would also have been willing to pay more than €20. This advantage is called **consumer surplus**: the area between the demand curve and the equilibrium price. Demand is zero at $p = €50$, so:

$$
\text{consumer surplus} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot (50 - 20) \cdot 60 = €900
$$

Similarly, suppliers who would also have sold at a lower price receive a **producer surplus**. Supply is zero at $p = €5$:

$$
\text{producer surplus} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot (20 - 5) \cdot 60 = €450
$$

### Functions of the price

| Function | Meaning |
| -------- | ------- |
| **Balancing function** | The price brings supply and demand into balance. |
| **Signalling function** | Rising prices indicate scarcity, falling prices an oversupply. |
| **Allocation function** | Factors of production flow to where high prices promise high profits. |
| **Selection function** | Only suppliers who can produce at the market price without a loss stay in the market; only buyers who can or want to pay the price are served. |

### Perfect market

Strictly speaking, the model only applies to a **perfect market**:

- **homogeneous** (identical) goods,
- no **preferences** for particular suppliers (spatial, personal, temporal),
- complete **market transparency**: everyone knows all prices,
- immediate **reaction** to price changes,
- many suppliers and buyers.

A perfect market does not exist in reality. Stock exchanges come closest. On imperfect markets, different prices can be charged for the same good. Price comparison websites increase market transparency and thereby intensify competition.

## Price elasticity of demand

The **price elasticity of demand** states by how many percent the quantity demanded changes when the price changes by one percent:

$$
\varepsilon = \frac{\text{percentage change in quantity}}{\text{percentage change in price}} = \frac{\Delta x / x}{\Delta p / p}
$$

Because price and quantity usually move in opposite directions, $\varepsilon$ is negative. Often the absolute value is considered:

| Absolute value | Name | Meaning | Examples |
| -------------- | ---- | ------- | -------- |
| $\lvert \varepsilon \rvert > 1$ | **elastic** | quantity reacts more strongly than the price | goods with many substitutes, luxury goods, individual brands |
| $\lvert \varepsilon \rvert = 1$ | unit elastic | quantity changes to the same extent | |
| $\lvert \varepsilon \rvert < 1$ | **inelastic** | quantity reacts less strongly than the price | staple foods, electricity, fuel in the short term, medicines, cigarettes |
| $\varepsilon = 0$ | perfectly inelastic | quantity does not change at all | vital medicines |

**Example:** a bakery raises the price of a bread roll from €0.50 to €0.55 (+10 %). Sales fall from 1,000 to 950 rolls per day (-5 %):

$$
\varepsilon = \frac{-5\,\%}{+10\,\%} = -0.5
$$

Demand is inelastic. Revenue rises from €500 to $950 \cdot €0.55 = €522.50$.

This leads to an important rule for pricing policy (see [marketing](/en/economy_and_law/marketing/)): with **inelastic** demand, revenue rises with price increases; with **elastic** demand, it rises with price cuts. That is why the state likes to tax goods with inelastic demand such as tobacco and fuel: tax revenue is high and reliable.

Elasticity is greater the more **substitutes** there are, the less **urgent** the need, the larger the **share of income** and the **longer** the period under consideration (in the long run you can switch to a more economical car).

Other elasticities:

- **Income elasticity:** percentage change in quantity due to a percentage change in income (positive for normal goods, negative for inferior goods, greater than 1 for luxury goods).
- **Cross-price elasticity:** percentage change in the quantity of one good due to a change in the price of another (positive for substitutes, negative for complements).

## Market forms

The **market form** results from the number of suppliers and buyers:

| Suppliers / buyers | many buyers | few buyers | one buyer |
| ------------------ | ----------- | ---------- | --------- |
| **many suppliers** | **polypoly** (perfect competition): fruit and vegetables, hairdressers | oligopsony: dairy farmers and a few dairies | monopsony: defence equipment and the armed forces |
| **few suppliers** | **oligopoly**: mobile telephony, fuel, operating systems | bilateral oligopoly: car part suppliers and car manufacturers | restricted monopsony |
| **one supplier** | **monopoly**: electricity grid in a region, patent holder | restricted monopoly | bilateral monopoly |

- In a **polypoly**, the individual supplier has no influence on the price (**price taker**). It can only decide how much to offer at the market price. Strong competition leads to low prices and innovation.
- In an **oligopoly**, the few suppliers watch each other: if one cuts the price, the others follow (price war). That is why oligopolists often compete through advertising, service and product differences instead, or illegally agree on prices (**cartel**).
- The **monopolist** can set the price itself, but is bound by demand: a higher price means lower sales. It chooses the combination with the highest profit. Monopolies usually lead to higher prices, lower quantities and less innovation than competition.

A **natural monopoly** exists when a single supplier can serve a market most cheaply because fixed costs are very high (electricity and gas grids, rail networks). Such networks are regulated by the state, in Austria for example by E-Control. In IT, **network effects** easily give rise to dominant platforms: a social network or an app store is all the more valuable the more people use it.

### Competition policy

Because competition protects consumers, it is protected by the state. In Austria, the **Cartel Act 2005** applies, enforced by the **Federal Competition Authority** (BWB) and the Cartel Court at the Higher Regional Court of Vienna, and in cross-border cases by the European Commission:

- **Ban on cartels:** agreements on prices, quantities or territories between competitors are prohibited.
- **Ban on abuse:** dominant companies must not abuse their position, for example through excessive prices or by obstructing competitors.
- **Merger control:** larger mergers must be notified and can be prohibited if they would significantly impede competition.

For very large digital platforms (gatekeepers such as Alphabet, Apple, Meta, Microsoft), the EU's **Digital Markets Act** also applies.

## State intervention in price formation

Sometimes the state considers the market price socially undesirable and sets prices.

**Maximum price:** a maximum price **below** the equilibrium price is meant to make goods affordable for consumers, for example capped rents for older flats (reference and category rents in Austria). In the worked example above, a maximum price of €15 would lead to **excess demand** of 30 units. The consequences are queues, allocation by other criteria (connections, "first come, first served"), black markets and too little supply because production becomes less worthwhile (for rents: less new construction and renovation).

**Minimum price:** a minimum price **above** the equilibrium price is meant to secure suppliers' incomes, for example the former intervention prices for agricultural products in the EU. At €25 in the example, there is **excess supply** of 30 units. The state has to buy up and store or destroy the surpluses (the "butter mountains" in the EU of the 1980s). A statutory minimum wage has a similar effect on the labour market to a minimum price. Austria has no statutory minimum wage; minimum wages are set in the collective agreements.

Instead of setting prices, the state can also intervene through **taxes** (making things more expensive, e.g. tobacco tax) and **subsidies** (making things cheaper, e.g. support for photovoltaic systems) without suspending the market mechanism.

## Market failure

**Market failure** occurs when the market on its own does not deliver a good overall economic outcome.

| Cause | Description | Example | Possible countermeasure |
| ----- | ----------- | ------- | ----------------------- |
| **Negative externalities** | costs borne not by those who cause them but by third parties or society | CO₂ emissions, noise, water pollution | taxes on the polluters (Pigouvian tax), CO₂ pricing and EU emissions trading, limit values, bans |
| **Positive externalities** | benefits to third parties that are not paid for | vaccinations, education, basic research | subsidies, state provision, compulsory schooling |
| **Public goods** | nobody can be excluded from use, so there is no private supply (see [basic concepts](/en/economy_and_law/economics/basics/#private-and-public-goods)) | national defence, flood protection | provision by the state |
| **Asymmetric information** | one side knows more than the other | used car market (the seller knows the defects), insurance | warranty obligations, information duties, compulsory insurance |
| **Market power** | monopolies and cartels | | competition policy, regulation |

A famous example of asymmetric information is George Akerlof's "Market for Lemons" (1970): because buyers cannot tell good used cars from bad ones, they only pay an average price. Sellers of good cars withdraw, and the quality on the market keeps falling (**adverse selection**). With insurance, **moral hazard** also occurs: people who are insured often behave less carefully.

The state can fail too (**government failure**), for example through lack of information, lobby interests or slow bureaucracy. An intervention therefore only makes sense if it actually improves the outcome.
