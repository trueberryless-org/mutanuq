According to the definition in the United Nations' Brundtland Report (1987), **sustainable development** is development "that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs". The term originally comes from forestry: no more wood should be felled than grows back.

## Three dimensions

| Dimension | Goal | Examples |
| --------- | ---- | -------- |
| **ecological** | preserve the natural foundations of life | climate protection, biodiversity, careful use of raw materials and energy |
| **economic** | economic activity that is viable in the long term | long-term competitiveness, sound public finances |
| **social** | fair living conditions | fair wages, occupational safety, education, equal opportunities, also along the supply chain |

In 2015, the United Nations adopted the **2030 Agenda** with 17 **Sustainable Development Goals** (SDGs), from "no poverty" through "quality education" to "climate action".

## Climate policy

Environmental damage is a classic example of **negative externalities** (see [market failure](/en/economy_and_law/economics/markets-and-prices/#market-failure)): those who emit greenhouse gases do not bear the costs of climate change themselves. The market on its own therefore produces too many emissions.

- In the **Paris Agreement** (2015), almost all states committed to limiting global warming to well below 2 °C, ideally 1.5 °C, above pre-industrial levels.
- With the **Green Deal**, the EU aims to become **climate-neutral** by 2050 and to cut net greenhouse gas emissions by at least 55 % compared with 1990 by 2030. In government programmes, Austria has set itself the goal of becoming climate-neutral as early as 2040.

The most important market-based instrument is a **price on CO₂**, so that those who cause emissions bear the costs:

- In the **EU Emissions Trading System** (EU ETS, since 2005), power plants, industrial installations and intra-European aviation need an allowance for every tonne of CO₂. The total number of allowances shrinks every year, and they are traded on an exchange. Those who can avoid emissions cheaply do so and sell surplus allowances. Emissions are thus cut where it is cheapest.
- For buildings and road transport, Austria introduced a **national CO₂ price** in October 2022, which raises the price of heating oil, natural gas and fuel. It is to be transferred into a second EU emissions trading system for these sectors (ETS 2), whose start has been postponed to 2028.

In addition, policy relies on subsidies (photovoltaics, replacing heating systems, e-mobility), regulations (limit values for vehicles, building regulations) and investment in public transport.

## Circular economy

The conventional **linear economy** follows the pattern "extract raw materials, produce, use, throw away". A **circular economy** keeps products and materials in use for as long as possible: through durable and repairable design, reuse, sharing, refurbishment and finally recycling. Examples from IT are modular smartphones, refurbished business laptops, the common USB-C charging port (an EU requirement for many devices since the end of 2024) and the right to repair.

## Sustainability in companies

Sustainability is not only an ethical question for companies: customers, employees, banks and investors increasingly pay attention to it. People speak of **ESG** criteria (_environmental, social, governance_).

- Under the EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (**CSRD**), large companies must report on their impact on the environment and society. With the simplification omnibus package of 2025 and 2026, the EU significantly reduced the number of companies covered, so that mainly very large companies are affected.
- **Greenwashing**, i.e. unsubstantiated environmental claims in advertising ("climate-neutral" only through purchased offsets), is increasingly restricted by EU rules.

### Green IT

IT also contributes to resource consumption: data centres, networks and end devices need electricity, and manufacturing devices consumes raw materials and energy. Sustainable IT includes efficient software and hardware, using devices for longer, choosing data centres powered by renewable energy and a conscious approach to compute-intensive applications such as [AI](/en/artificial-intelligence/ai-ethics-and-law/#environmental-impact).
