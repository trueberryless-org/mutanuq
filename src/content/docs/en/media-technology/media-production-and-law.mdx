Whether it is a corporate film, a podcast, a website or a diploma thesis video: every media production is a [project](/en/project_management/) with a client, a budget, a deadline and a team. Good planning before the first day of shooting saves time and money later. The legal side is just as important, because almost every media product uses other people's works (photos, music, typefaces) or shows people. Anyone who does not clear the rights risks injunctions, damages and having to take down the finished product.

## Media production workflow

A production is divided into three phases. The more is clarified in the first phase, the more smoothly the other two run.

| Phase | Tasks | Results |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pre-production** | briefing, idea, concept, script, storyboard, planning, rights clearance | concept, script, storyboard, shooting schedule, budget, contracts |
| **Production** | recording: shoot, photo shoot, sound recording | raw material (_footage_), sound recordings, notes |
| **Post-production** | reviewing, editing, sound, colour, graphics, export | finished media product in all required formats |

### Pre-production

1. **Briefing**: the client describes the goal, target group, core message, purpose (website, social media, trade fair), budget, deadline and requirements from the [corporate design](/en/media-technology/layout-and-design/#corporate-design). In the **rebriefing**, the agency summarises how it has understood the brief and clarifies open questions.
2. **Idea and concept**: ideas are collected with [creativity techniques](/en/project_management/basics/creativity_techniques/) and condensed into a concept. An **outline** (synopsis) describes the story on one page, a **treatment** describes it in more detail, scene by scene, in prose.
3. **Script**: describes each scene with a scene heading (interior or exterior, location, time of day), action and dialogue. For explainer and corporate films, a two-column script is often used: picture on the left, sound and voice-over on the right.
4. **Storyboard**: draws each shot as a sketch, similar to a comic, with information on [shot size](/en/media-technology/video/#shot-sizes), camera movement, duration, sound and text. Stick figures are enough: the point is that the whole team has the same picture in mind, and problems become visible on paper instead of on the day of shooting.
5. **Planning**: **shot list** (list of all shots), **shooting schedule** (sorted by location and people, not by the order in the film), casting, **location scouting**, filming permits, equipment, **budget** and timetable.
6. **Rights clearance**: consent forms from the participants, music and image licences, permission from property owners.

### Production

- On set there are clear **roles**: director, camera, sound, lighting, production management, often a person for **continuity** who notes which clothing, props and positions were visible in each shot.
- The **clapperboard** at the beginning of each take shows scene, shot and take and produces a visual and audio cue used to synchronise separately recorded sound.
- More is shot of every set-up than needed: a few seconds of lead-in and lead-out, several takes and cutaway material (_B-roll_).
- **Backup** on the day of shooting according to the **3-2-1 rule**: three copies on two different media, one of them at another location.

### Post-production

1. **Reviewing** and **logging**: watch the material, mark the best takes, name and organise the files.
2. **[Editing](/en/media-technology/video/#editing)**: rough cut, fine cut, approval by the client, picture lock.
3. **Sound**: dialogue editing, sound design, music, voice-over recording, mixing to the target [loudness](/en/media-technology/audio/#loudness).
4. **Colour correction and grading**, graphics, titles, animations, visual effects.
5. **Subtitles** (for accessibility and for watching without sound on social media).
6. **Export** in all required formats (16:9 for YouTube, 9:16 for Reels) and **archiving** of the project data.

Each phase ends with **approval** by the client. Change requests after approval cost considerably more than before, which is why the quote specifies how many rounds of revisions are included.

## Copyright

The general principles of the Austrian **Copyright Act** (UrhG) are covered in the chapter [Internet Law](/en/economy_and_law/law/internet_law/#copyright). This section is about what matters in media production.

**Works** are protected: original intellectual creations in the fields of literature, music, fine arts and cinematography (§ 1 UrhG). In media production, these include texts, scripts, music, photos, graphics, illustrations, logos (if they are original), films and software. **Ideas**, concepts, styles and facts are not protected, only their concrete expression.

- Protection arises **automatically** upon creation. A © notice is not required.
- It ends **70 years after the death** of the author. After that the work is in the **public domain** and may be used by everyone. However, a new recording of a public-domain piece of music or a photo of a public-domain painting may itself be protected.
- Photos are either **photographic works** (with original design, protected like other works) or simple **photographs** (§ 74 UrhG). The latter are also protected; the photographer's right expires 50 years after the photo was taken or published. So there is practically no photo that may be used freely just because it is "simple".

### Rights of the author

| Type | Rights |
| --- | --- |
| **Exploitation rights** | adaptation, reproduction, distribution, broadcasting, performance and screening, **making available** (uploading to the internet) |
| **Moral rights** | recognition of authorship, determining the author's designation (attribution), protection against distortion of the work |

Besides authors, the law also protects **neighbouring rights** (related rights): the rights of performers (musicians, actors, voice artists), producers of sound recordings and films, broadcasters and photographers.

### Licence to use and exclusive right of use

Copyright itself is **not transferable** (except by inheritance). The author can, however, permit others to use the work (§ 24 UrhG):

| | Licence to use | Exclusive right of use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Effect | simple permission to use the work in a certain way | **exclusive** right: only the holder may use the work in this way, not even the author any more |
| Comparison | simple, non-exclusive licence | exclusive licence |
| Example | stock photo bought by many customers | a logo that an agency designs for a client |

A licence agreement should specify exactly: which **types of use** (print, web, social media, TV), which **territory**, which **duration**, whether it is **exclusive**, whether **adaptations** are allowed and how the author must be credited. Anything not expressly granted is, in case of doubt, considered not permitted. For logos and corporate designs, the client therefore usually has a comprehensive, unlimited exclusive right of use granted.

For commercially produced films, the exploitation rights belong to the **film producer** under § 38 UrhG. For works created by employees in the course of their duties, the employment contract governs the rights of use (for software, § 40b UrhG applies).

### Permitted uses

Some uses are allowed without consent. Particularly important for media productions:

- **Quotation** (§ 42f UrhG): parts of a work or individual images may be included with attribution if they serve as evidence in a separate work of your own, for example in a film review. An image used merely for illustration or decoration is not a quotation.
- **Incidental inclusion** (§ 42e UrhG): a work that appears accidentally and incidentally in the frame (a poster in the background of a street scene) may be filmed along with it.
- **Freedom of panorama** (§ 54 para. 1 no. 5 UrhG): buildings and works of art permanently located in public places may be depicted and the images used.
- **Teaching**: certain uses to a limited extent are permitted for teaching. However, this no longer applies once a school project is published on the internet.

### Consequences of infringement

Anyone who uses other people's works without permission must expect **injunctions**, **removal** (deletion, destruction), **appropriate remuneration** (the usual licence fee) and, if at fault, **damages**. Without proof of specific damage, the rights holder can demand **double** the appropriate remuneration in the case of culpable infringement (§ 87 para. 3 UrhG). Lawyers' fees come on top. "I found it on Google" is no defence.

## Creative Commons

**Creative Commons** (CC) is a non-profit organisation that provides standardised licences with which authors permit certain uses in advance. The current version 4.0 is made up of four elements:

| Element | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **BY** (attribution) | author, title, source and licence must be credited; part of all six licences |
| **SA** (ShareAlike) | adaptations must be published under the same licence |
| **NC** (NonCommercial) | no use primarily intended for commercial advantage or monetary compensation |
| **ND** (NoDerivatives) | the work may only be shared unchanged |

This results in six licences, from the most permissive to the most restrictive:

| Licence | commercial | adaptation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **CC BY** | yes | yes |
| **CC BY-SA** | yes | yes, under the same licence |
| **CC BY-ND** | yes | no |
| **CC BY-NC** | no | yes |
| **CC BY-NC-SA** | no | yes, under the same licence |
| **CC BY-NC-ND** | no | no |

In addition there is **CC0**: the author waives all rights as far as legally possible. In Austria a complete waiver of copyright is not possible; CC0 then acts as a comprehensive, unconditional licence.

Attribution follows the rule of thumb **TASL** (title, author, source, licence), for example: "Sunset over Krems" by Anna Huber, licensed under CC BY 4.0 (with links to the original and the licence text). For adaptations you also state that the work has been modified.

:::caution
A website with advertising, a corporate film for a company or a video on a monetised YouTube channel counts as commercial use. NC works are off limits there. CC licences are also irrevocable: anyone who publishes a work under CC BY cannot withdraw the licence later.
:::

## Stock media

**Stock agencies** (such as Adobe Stock, Getty Images, Shutterstock) sell rights to use photos, videos, music and graphics.

| Model | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Royalty-free** | pay once, then use multiple times without time limit within the licence terms; "royalty-free" therefore does not mean free of charge |
| **Rights managed** | price depends on the specific use: medium, print run, territory, duration |
| **Editorial use only** | only for reporting, not for advertising, usually because no releases from people or brands shown are available |
| **Extended licence** | needed for example for merchandise intended for resale or for very large print runs |

Platforms such as Unsplash, Pexels or Pixabay offer free images under **their own licences** (not Creative Commons). They usually allow commercial use as well, but forbid, for example, reselling the images unchanged. They do not guarantee that releases from the people shown are available.

For images generated with **artificial intelligence**: copyright only protects human creations, so purely machine-generated images are generally not protected. The service's terms of use still govern what is allowed, and depicted real people, brands or imitated works may infringe the rights of third parties.

## Image rights

**Protection of likeness** under **§ 78 UrhG** protects people depicted: images of persons may neither be exhibited publicly nor made available to the public in any other way if this would harm the **legitimate interests** of the person depicted.

Unlike in Germany, Austria therefore has no general ban on publishing images of people without their consent. It depends on a **balancing of interests** in the individual case. According to case law, legitimate interests are typically harmed if the image

- **exposes**, demeans or ridicules the person,
- reveals their **private life** (such as their home, illness, family),
- can give rise to **misinterpretation** (a photo of an uninvolved person in a report on crime),
- is used for **advertising** without consent, because nobody has to appear as an advertising figure against their will.

The protection applies to **publication**, not to taking the photo. Independently of this, photos and videos of identifiable people are **personal data** within the meaning of the [GDPR](/en/economy_and_law/law/internet_law/#data-protection).

:::tip[In practice]
For every production with identifiable people, you obtain a written **consent form** (_model release_). It states the purpose, media, duration and any payment. For minors, the parents or guardians also sign. People who can only be seen by chance in a crowd usually do not need to consent, as long as they are not singled out or exposed.
:::

## Music rights

Music is particularly tricky in media productions because many rights are attached to a single recording:

| Rights holder | Rights | administered by (Austria) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| composer, lyricist, music publisher | performance, broadcasting and making-available rights | **AKM** (state-approved society of authors, composers and music publishers) |
| composer, lyricist, music publisher | mechanical rights: reproduction and distribution (CD, download, storage media) | **austro mechana** |
| performers, record producers (label) | neighbouring rights in the specific recording | **LSG** and the label directly |

**Collecting societies** such as AKM license entire repertoires, collect the fees and distribute them to the authors. Anyone who runs, say, a school party with music or a café with background music reports this to AKM. The German counterparts are GEMA and GVL, in Switzerland SUISA.

For a video, an AKM licence alone is usually not enough: combining music with pictures (**synchronisation right**) usually has to be acquired directly from the **music publisher**, and the use of the specific recording (**master right**) from the **label**. For small productions this is rarely affordable. Practical alternatives are:

- **production music** from music libraries (library music) with a simple licence for videos
- music under **Creative Commons licences** (observe the conditions; NC is excluded for commercial projects)
- the **YouTube Audio Library** for videos on YouTube
- **your own music** or a commissioned composition with contractually granted rights

On platforms such as YouTube, **Content ID** automatically detects protected music. As a result, revenue goes to the rights holder, or the video is blocked or muted.
