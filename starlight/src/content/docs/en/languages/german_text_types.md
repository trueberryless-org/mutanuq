## Stylistic devices

Stylistic devices and rhetorical devices are linguistic elements that make texts more effective. Stylistic devices are special linguistic expressions that deliberately deviate from the norm in order to achieve effects. Rhetorical devices are techniques that increase the persuasiveness of statements. From metaphors to irony, they offer many ways to refine communication and to express thoughts more intensely. Authors and speakers use these tools to present their messages impressively. Since this page is about German texts, the examples are given in German.

| Term                        | Description                                                                                                    | Example (German)                                                                                                                                    |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Allegorie** (allegory)    | representation of an abstract concept as a person                                                             | Frau Sonne, Gerechtigkeit tritt im Theaterstück auf                                                                                     |
| **Alliteration** (Stabreim) | identical initial sounds of the stem syllables of successive words                                             | Zimt und Zucker, über Stock und Stein                                                                                                               |
| Anadiplose (anadiplosis)    | repetition of the last word(s) at the end of a sentence at the beginning of the following sentence            | Die Nacht ist dunkel. Dunkel ist der Wald. Wald voller Geheimnisse.                                                                                 |
| **Anapher** (anaphora)      | repetition of words or phrases at the beginning                                                                | Tatsache ist, ich habe verloren. Tatsache ist, ich werde daraus Konsequenzen ziehen.                                                                |
| **Antithese** (antithesis)  | juxtaposition of opposites                                                                                     | Groß und Klein, Arm und Reich                                                                                                                       |
| **Assonanz** (assonance)    | the same or a similar vowel occurs frequently in successive words or sentences                                 | Der Mond thront ober dem Wolkenhügel.                                                                                                               |
| **Chiasmus**                | crosswise arrangement of sentence elements                                                                     | Die Kunst ist lang und kurz ist unser Leben.                                                                                                        |
| Dysphemismus (dysphemism)   | derogatory term instead of a neutral expression                             | Glotze (für Fernseher), abkratzen (für sterben)                                                                                   |
| **Ellipse** (ellipsis)      | incomplete sentence                                                                                            | Was nun?                                                                                                                                            |
| **Epipher** (epiphora)      | repetition at the end of a sentence                                                                            | Ich fordere mehr Mut. Ich lebe mehr Mut.                                                                                                            |
| **Euphemismus** (euphemism) | veiling, glossing over of a negative expression                                                                | die Radieschen von unten ansehen, das Zeitliche segnen, Raumpflegerin                                                                               |
| **Häufung** (accumulation)  | enumeration / repetition of the same part of speech                                                            | Amsel, Drossel, Fink und Star                                                                                                                       |
| **Hyperbel** (hyperbole)    | exaggeration                                                                                                   | Hundertmal hab‘ ich es dir gesagt.                                                                                                                  |
| **Hypotaxe** (hypotaxis)    | complex sentence with subordinate clauses                                                                      | Um zu beweisen, dass er unschuldig war, legte er seine Handyrechnung vor, aus der hervorging, wen er zum fraglichen Zeitpunkt angerufen hatte.      |
| Ironie (irony)              | the opposite of what is expressed is meant                                                                     | Das hast du ja mal wieder toll gemacht!                                                                                                             |
| **Klimax** (climax)         | gradual intensification within a sentence                                                                      | Ich kam, sah und siegte. (Cäsar)                                                                                                                    |
| Litotes                     | affirmation through negation                                                                                   | Nicht schlecht!                                                                                                                                     |
| **Metapher** (metaphor)     | figurative expression                                                                                          | Du bist meine Rose. Die Wüstenschiffe ziehen weiter. Füll den Tiger in den Tank.                                                                    |
| Metonymie (metonymy)        | replacing a term with another one that is related to it                                                        | Die Krone hat entschieden. (the crown stands for royal authority)                                                   |
| **Neologismus** (neologism) | newly coined word                                                                                              | downloaden                                                                                                                                          |
| Onomatopoesie (onomatopoeia) | imitation of natural sounds through language                                                                  | Der Hund bellte laut. Die Vögel zwitscherten im Baum. Der Wind zischte, pfiff, blies.                                                                           |
| **Oxymoron**                | combination of two parts that logically exclude each other                                                     | Minuswachstum, schwarze Milch                                                                                                                       |
| **Parallelismus** (parallelism) | repeated word order / sentence construction                                                                | Sie hören weit, sie sehen fern.                                                                                                                     |
| **Parataxe** (parataxis)    | sequence of main clauses                                                                                       | Er sah, er reagierte.                                                                                                                               |
| **Parenthese** (parenthesis) | insertion                                                                                                     | Er ist – wie gesagt – nicht dumm.                                                                                                                   |
| Paronomasie (paronomasia)   | play on words through similar sounds with different meanings                                                   | Wer rastet, der rostet.                                                                                                                             |
| **Personifikation** (personification) | attributing human qualities                                                                          | Die Sonne lacht. Die Finsternis sieht mit hundert schwarzen Augen.                                                                                  |
| Pleonasmus (pleonasm)       | unnecessary use of words whose meaning is already contained in the context                                     | Der nasse Regen.                                                                                                                |
| **Rhetorische Frage** (rhetorical question) | pseudo-question that answers itself                                                            | Das ist doch nicht dein Ernst? Bin ich deine Putzfrau?                                                                                              |
| **Symbol**                  | figurative expression that points beyond itself                                                                | Wasser (für Leben), Herz (für Liebe), Feuer (für Leidenschaft)                                                                                      |
| Synekdoche (synecdoche)     | a part stands for the whole or vice versa                                                                      | Alle Hände an Deck! (hands stand for the people)                                                                                                    |
| **Vergleich** (simile)      | equating two things with the comparison word "wie" (like)                                                     | Er kämpft wie ein Löwe.                                                                                                                             |
| **Wiederholung** (repetition) | repetition of identical words                                                                                | Geliebte, o meine Geliebte!                                                                                                                         |
| Zeugma                      | one word refers to two others, but in different meanings                                                       | Er schlug die Tür zu und sein Herz in Angst.                                                                                     |

## Summary (Zusammenfassung)

The summary is a neutral, objective text type written in the present tense and the present perfect.

### Characteristics

A summary has the following characteristics:

-   no opinion
-   only summarise the most essential information
-   put it in your own words
-   no conclusion
-   do not address the reader
-   stick exactly to the word count
-   heading (optional): `Zusammenfassung von ...`
-   short to medium-length text type

### Structure

#### Heading

The heading consists of `Zusammenfassung von` (summary of) and the title of the source text and is optional.

#### Introduction

In the introduction of a summary, you mention the meta information about the source text. It should contain at least the title, author, place and date of publication as well as the topic. Because of the heading, you do not necessarily have to mention the title a second time.

It is best to use the basic sentence (Basissatz), which is demonstrated here with an example:

> Der Artikel „grüne Wolle“ von Arthur Wilderöm, welcher am 26.10.2023 in „Der Standard“ erschienen ist, beschäftigt sich mit dem Thema Umweltbelastung in Bezug auf Polyester.

#### Main part

In the main part of a summary, the most important positions, information and facts of the source text are summarised in your own words. The order of the information does not have to follow the source text. Ideally, deal with one fact or argument in detail per paragraph. However, you do not have to argue yourself; rather, you should take over the content of the arguments from the source text.

To be able to extract the most important information easily, read through the source text and only mark the passages whose content you really want to include in the summary. Aim to have marked at most **four passages** in total after reading. Keep in mind that the specified word count must not be exceeded.

## Letter to the editor (Leserbrief)

The letter to the editor is an opinion-oriented, subjective text type written in the present tense and the present perfect.

### Characteristics

A letter to the editor has the following characteristics:

-   establish a connection to the source text and especially to the author
-   formulate your own arguments
-   address the reader
-   stick exactly to the word count
-   special structure with header, salutation and closing (+ signature)
-   short text type

### Structure

#### Header

So that the letter to the editor looks like a letter, the place and date where and when the letter was written belong in the _top right_ corner.

> Stockholm, 26. Oktober 2023

#### Salutation

You write a letter to the editor either directly to the author of the source text or to the editorial team of the newspaper / magazine.

> Sehr geehrter Herr Wilderöm!

> Sehr geehrtes Redaktionsteam,

:::note[Keep in mind!]
Keep in mind that in German, after a `,` (comma) in the salutation the following sentence starts with a lowercase letter, while after a `!` (exclamation mark) the first sentence must start with a capital letter.
:::

#### Introduction

In the introduction of a letter to the editor, you mention the meta information about the source text. It should contain at least the title, author, place and date of publication as well as the topic.

It is best to use the basic sentence, which is demonstrated here with an example:

> Der Artikel „grüne Wolle“ von Arthur Wilderöm, welcher am 26.10.2023 in „Der Standard“ erschienen ist, beschäftigt sich mit dem Thema Umweltbelastung in Bezug auf Polyester.

#### Main part

In the main part, you refer to all passages in the source text to which you want to add something or which you want to correct. If, for example, you are of a different opinion, argue why your opinion is more justifiable than the author's. Support your opinion concretely with examples. You can use the 3B method or the 5-step method to help with your arguments.

Divide the main part into sections that belong together in terms of content and separate these paragraphs with blank lines.

#### Conclusion

In the conclusion, summarise the most important point of criticism of your letter once more. If the task asks for an appeal, the conclusion is perfect for it. So appeal to all readers or a specific group in the conclusion, or write a simple summary of the letter.

#### Closing

End your letter with a closing formula.

> Mit freundlichen Grüßen

#### Signature

If you send the text digitally, your name below the closing is sufficient. If you print the text, however, actually sign it by hand below the closing.

## Discussion essay (Erörterung)

The discussion essay is a neutral, objective text type with sophisticated argumentation, written in the present tense and the present perfect.

### Characteristics

A discussion essay has the following characteristics:

-   factual wording
-   neutral tone
-   creative introduction
-   summarising (and appealing) conclusion with your own opinion, final verdict
-   write at most 50 words more than required
-   heading possible
-   medium-length to long text type

### Structure

#### Introduction

The introduction of a discussion essay should be as creative as possible. As an author, you want the readers to be captivated by the topic right away and to want to read the entire text. There are various methods for writing and starting a creative introduction:

-   start with a quote, something current or an experience
-   ask a rhetorical question to make the readers think

> Jeder kennt den Moment, wenn der Toast vom Tellerrand kippt und man weiß bereits vorher, dass er 100%ig auf der Marmeladenseite aufklatschen wird. Aber haben Sie sich jemals gefragt, warum dieses Ereignis so unvermeidlich ist?

> Als Aristoteles sagte, man könne den Wind nicht ändern, aber die Segel richtig setzen, ahnte er sicher nicht, dass sein Zitat einmal auf hunderten von Webseiten im Internet und in Büchern in Bibliotheken kursieren würde. Genauso wenig wissen wir heute, was in 200 Jahren sein wird.

#### Main part

In the main part, the most important arguments relating to the topic are dealt with. Depending on whether you are supposed to write a pro and contra discussion or a supporting discussion (Belegerörterung), you deal with pro and contra arguments or only with arguments that reinforce each other. These arguments must always remain factual and must not include any personal judgement. It is also important to establish a clear reference to the source text.

#### Conclusion

In the conclusion of a discussion essay, you briefly summarise the most important statements once more and discuss connections with possible other topics. In addition, you may be asked to write an appeal to the readers, which must also be done in the conclusion.

## Commentary (Kommentar)

The commentary is an opinion-based, critical, satirical, internally coherent, subjective text type with sophisticated, linguistically excellent argumentation, written in the present tense and the present perfect. All arguments must support your own opinion. Counter-arguments must be worded weakly and presented as unconvincing.

### Characteristics

A commentary has the following characteristics:

-   do not use `Ich` (I)
-   put it in your own words or quote correctly (only indirect quotes)
-   address the readers
-   creative introduction that mentions the title and author
-   the main part argues fluently (use many [stylistic devices](#stylistic-devices))
-   opinion-oriented conclusion with suggested solutions
-   write at most 50 words more than required
-   opinionated, creative heading
-   short to medium-length text type

### Structure

#### Heading

Since the commentary is a very creative text type, the heading should also be extremely creative and captivating and make the reader curious about the text.

#### Introduction

A commentary has a creative introduction that should at least mention the author and the title of the source text. However, not all data of the source text has to appear in the introduction. For example, the date or place of publication is not required. Overall, the introduction should comprise about 50 words.

#### Main part

In the main part, several paragraphs follow one another, each of which refers to an argument that supports your own opinion. If a counter-argument is to be included, your linguistic skill must convey to the reader that this point carries little weight compared to your own opinion.

Avoid using `Ich` (I). Some passive constructions can help with this:

> ~Ich finde, dass ...~

> Für viele steht außer Debatte, dass ...

In addition, emphasise the importance of quotes, arguments or claims in general by using special stylistic devices that are otherwise rarely used.

> **Keine Frage:** Die Matura ist kein Honiglecken.

> **Obwohl:** Milch kann auch keine Laktose beinhalten, wenn man es bevorzugt, Produktersatzstoffe zu konsumieren.

:::note[Note]
You should **not** mark the phrases in bold in your text. The stylistic devices are only in bold here to make clear which parts of the sentence the previous paragraph refers to.
:::

#### Conclusion

In the conclusion, the most important of your argumentative positions should be summed up once more **without using `Ich` (I)**. If you use the word `Ich` once, that is completely okay. However, always aim to write subjectively without using subjective words.

## Text analysis (Textanalyse)

The text analysis is a factual, analytical text type that analyses the source text according to many criteria. Because of these criteria, the text analysis always follows the same scheme, which you only have to learn by heart. The text analysis is written in the present tense and the present perfect.

### Characteristics

A text analysis has the following characteristics:

-   do not use `Ich` (I)
-   standard introduction with title, author, text type, place and date of publication of the source text
-   do not interpret, only quote (direct quotes)
-   be able to recognise [stylistic devices](#stylistic-devices)
-   use technical terms (if the topic is `IT`, you can include such technical vocabulary)
-   factual wording
-   write at most 50 words more than required
-   no heading
-   medium-length to long text type

### Structure

#### Introduction

In the introduction of a text analysis, you only briefly mention the author, the title, the place and date of publication and the type (report, gloss, commentary, column, ...) of the source text. You can use a basic introduction for this:

> Die Glosse „Online einkaufen war gestern - heute gehen wir Echtzeitshoppen“ von Sybille Hamann aus „der Presse“ beschäftigt sich auf satirische Weise mit dem Thema Onlineshopping und dem damit verbundenen Riesenaufwand.

#### Main part

The main part summarises the content of the source text in two to three sentences and then moves directly on to the **formal** (structure and organisation), **linguistic** (language, sentence structures, style, rhetorical devices, choice of words) and **content-related** (content, argumentation, intention) analysis. The following table shows which features must always be analysed:

| Criterion                                    | Possible results |
| -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Content                                      | reproduce the content of the source text in your own words in about 75 words |
| Sections / structure                         | list the sections that are separated by content, using line numbers |
| Sentence structures (+ examples)             | <ul style="margin-top: 1rem"><li><p><strong>paratactic</strong><br>mostly main clauses (parataxis)</p></li><li><p><strong>hypotactic</strong><br>many subordinate clauses and complex sentences (hypotaxis); many commas</p></li></ul> |
| Language + examples                          | standard language, educated language, colloquial language, youth language |
| Argumentation + examples                     | <ul style="margin-top: 1rem"><li><p><strong>factual arguments</strong><br>justification by scientific facts</p></li><li><p><strong>value arguments</strong> / normative arguments<br>justification by social <em>norms / values / rules</em></p></li><li><p><strong>comparative arguments</strong> / arguments by analogy<br>comprehensibility through comparison with another field</p></li><li><p><strong>arguments from authority</strong><br>justification by statements of experts or recognised organisations</p></li><li><p><strong>indirect arguments</strong><br>refutation of alternative positions</p></li><li><p><strong>plausibility argument</strong><br>logical conclusions</p></li></ul> |
| Word fields / vocabulary                     | does the author use the technical terms of the topic? |
| Parts of speech + examples                   | many nouns (nominal style), verbs, adjectives |
| [Stylistic devices](#stylistic-devices) + many examples | Which stylistic devices are used? Always name the type of rhetorical device with as many examples as possible. You can also list which stylistic devices are not used in the entire source text. |
| Intention                                    | analysis of attention, information and appeal + successful / not successful? |

Work through these criteria in your text and divide them logically into linguistic analysis, content analysis and stylistic analysis.

#### Conclusion

In the conclusion, you share your own opinion, but without using `Ich` (I). You mainly focus on evaluating the author's intention. Usually, you ask yourself the question: _How well did the author succeed in convincing you of_ her / his _opinion?_ Justify all your statements!

## Text interpretation (Textinterpretation)

The text interpretation can be written for three different types of source texts. The structure of the interpretation differs mainly in the last points of the text. Speaking of structure: just like with the text analysis, it is clearly defined, so you can work according to the same scheme every time.

### Characteristics

A text interpretation has the following characteristics:

-   do not use `Ich` (I) (possible in the conclusion)
-   standard introduction with title, author, text type, place and date of publication of the source text
-   quote when giving examples (direct quotes)
-   be able to recognise and interpret [stylistic devices](#stylistic-devices)
-   use technical terms (if the topic is `IT`, you can include such technical vocabulary)
-   factual wording
-   do not fall below the word count; no upper limit (longest text type)
-   heading possible
-   always a long text type

### Structure

At the beginning of the text interpretation, you have to carry out all tasks of the text analysis in a somewhat shorter form (see [text analysis](#main-part-4)). In addition, you have to interpret why certain stylistic devices were used and identify some genre-specific features – epic, lyric, dramatic.

#### Heading

The heading of a text interpretation is optional. If a heading is required, it must look like this:

> Textinterpretation: „Titel“ von Autor

#### Introduction

For the text interpretation, you can write either a creative or a standard introduction. Either you briefly mention the author, the title, the place and date of publication and the type (short story, novella, poem, tragedy, ...) of the source text, or you come up with a creative opening. For the former option, you can use a basic introduction:

> Die Glosse „Online einkaufen war gestern - heute gehen wir Echtzeitshoppen“ von Sybille Hamann aus „der Presse“ beschäftigt sich auf satirische Weise mit dem Thema Onlineshopping und dem damit verbundenen Riesenaufwand.

#### Main part

In a text interpretation, you have to cover all the analysis points that also have to be carried out in the text analysis (see [text analysis](#main-part-4)). Then, depending on the type of source text (epic, lyric, dramatic), you also analyse the specific features (see [types](#types)). The interpretation of the stylistic devices (Why are these stylistic devices used? What does _the author_ want to express with them?) and the intention of the author are also important. Do not separate the interpretation from the analysis and do not write a separate paragraph with the interpretation. Instead, group the content by topics and features. `Ich` (I) must not be used in the entire main part.

##### Approaches to interpretation

Since interpreting an epic, lyric or dramatic text is not always the easiest thing, here are a few approaches to help you get started.

-   **Contextualisation**  
    Based on the analysis of time and place, consider in which historical context the text can be placed. Which epoch / period of literary history does the text belong to?

-   **Effect on the reader**  
    What effects does the text have on the reader? Which impressions and feelings are created? _The author_ certainly did not write the text at that time without reason, but rather wanted to make the reader think about something. Perhaps this intention is also related to historical events.

-   **Sociology**  
    Did _the author_ criticise social conditions? If so, what effect did social reality have on _the author_?

-   **Psychology**  
    Interpret the actions of the characters and analyse their motives. Why did _the author_ think that the characters would act in a certain way in certain situations?

-   **Communication theory**  
    In some poems, the characters talk past each other. Analyse whether _the author_ has also built such features into your source text!

-   **Text types**  
    _The author_ chose to write the story as a gloss, song, ballad, poem, tragedy, novel, legend, imagery, etc. for a particular reason. This choice is unlikely to have been made without reason. Many authors are aware of the effects, strengths and weaknesses of these text types and use them to (un)consciously convey their intentions to the reader. Here, too, the connection with the context has to be made, since at some times in history publishing certain content was forbidden and authors used text types to get around these bans. Why?

#### Conclusion

Every text interpretation must have a conclusion. Here, you are usually asked for your personal opinion. The main question is whether the author's intention and opinion match your own or whether certain formalities or customs in the text still apply today. Only in the conclusion can you use `Ich` (I) in the text.

### Types

Depending on the type of source text, different features must be analysed in the text interpretation.

#### Epic (Epik)

If the source text is an epic (narrative) text type, the following features must be analysed and interpreted:

##### Author and title

Research data about _the author_ and possibly establish references to literary history. Also interpret the title in relation to the topic and world events. Name the topic of the text.

##### Narrator, narrative perspective and narrative stance

The narrator is invented by the author and has a mediating function between what is narrated and the readers. In most cases, the narrator is not identical to _the author_ of a narrative text, but this is also possible.

-   **Narrative perspective (Erzählperspektive)**  
    The point of view from which a story is told is called the narrative perspective. Stories can be told from two different perspectives:

    -   _External perspective_  
        You look at events from the outside and narrate them.

    -   _Internal perspective_  
        The narrator is in the middle of the events.

-   **Narrative stance (Erzählhaltung)**

    -   _Authorial narrator_  
        This narrator knows everything: they know the thoughts and feelings of all characters. In addition, they comment on the events, for example:

        > Hans bewegt seine Hand zur Schere. Ich habe mir bereits gedacht, dass er dies trotz seiner Angst gegenüber dem Objekt tun wird. Er denkt ein letztes Mal an den potentiellen Tod und greift schließlich zu.

    -   _Neutral narrator_  
        This narrator does not interfere in the events and only reproduces the plot. You also learn little about the characters' thoughts.

        > Hans greift langsam zur Schere. Er zögert bei dieser Bewegung, jedoch fasst er schlussendlich das Objekt in seine Hand.

    -   _Personal narrator_  
        This narrator knows the feelings and thoughts of one person, but not the thoughts and feelings of other people. The story can be told in the third person or by means of a `first-person narrator`.

        Here are two examples (first a personal narrator and then the `first-person narrator`):

        > Hans greift langsam zur Schere. Er zögert bei dieser Bewegung, jedoch fasst er schlussendlich das Objekt in seine Hand.

        > Ist der Tod mir nahe? Ich spüre die Kälte des Objekts, als ich langsam zur Schere greife.

##### Space and time

-   **Space**  
    The surroundings, landscape or place where a story takes place.

    The space can be real (e.g. Vienna), symbolic (e.g. a big city, in the forest, by the sea, ...) or surreal (e.g. a living room inside a dromedary).

    In short texts, the space is only hinted at in general terms and not specified.

-   **Time**  
    The time in which a story takes place.

    -   _Period_  
        Analyse which period the text belongs to (Second World War, Roman Empire, present day).

    -   _Narrating time (Erzählzeit)_  
        The time needed to tell a story (reading time).

    -   _Narrated time (erzählte Zeit)_
        The time span that passes within the narrated plot.

        > Spielt die Geschichte über mehrere Jahre oder nur Minuten?

    -   _Handling of time_  
        If you put the _narrating time_ and the _narrated time_ in relation to each other, you get the handling of time in the text. Three types are distinguished:

        -   **Time stretching**  
            If $narrating\;time\;>\;narrated\;time$, we speak of stretched time. The events happen in slow motion, so to speak.

        -   **Time covering**  
            If $narrating\;time\;\approx\;narrated\;time$, we speak of covering time. The events happen as if you were living through them.

        -   **Time compressing**  
            If $narrating\;time\;<\;narrated\;time$, we speak of compressed time. The events happen very quickly one after another; sometimes only a few words describe a scene.

    -   _Sequence of time_  
        The order in which the events occur.

        -   **chronological**  
            The story starts with the events that happened earliest and ends with those that happened latest.

        -   **with flashbacks and flash-forwards**  
            Moments that occur either before or after the main timeline are inserted in the middle of the flow of time to symbolise memories or visions.

        -   **with complete dissolution of the usual concept of time**  
            Past, present and future flow into one another ("tumbling time").

##### Characters

-   **Characterisation**  
    How the characters are introduced to _the reader_.

    -   _direct characterisation_

        > brav, einfältig, vorlaut, muskulös, ...

    -   _indirect characterisation_  
        The reader infers the character from the behaviour and statements of the figure.

-   **Constellation of characters**  
    The relationship between the characters

-   **Forms of speech**

    -   _Narrator's report_  
        The narrator's contributions in different contexts (sections):

        -   reproduction of the plot
        -   scenic presentation (direct speech)
        -   description / depiction (places, characters)
        -   narrator's comments
        -   reflection (thoughts)
        -   summaries

    -   _Characters' speech_  
        Utterances of fictional characters

        -   direct speech
        -   indirect speech
        -   free indirect speech (erlebte Rede): reproduction of the thoughts, feelings etc. of a character in the third person singular, past tense:
            > Medina musterte den Mann misstrauisch. Sollte das wirklich ihr Bruder sein? Hatte der nicht eine kantigere Nase und eine Narbe am Hals?
        -   interior monologue: silent self-talk in the first person and in the present tense
            > [...] Zum wievielten Mal lauf' ich jetzt eigentlich um das Hotel herum? Also was jetzt? Da steh' ich vor dem Tor. In der Halle ist noch niemand. Natürlich -- sie sitzen ja noch alle beim Diner. Seltsam sieht die Halle aus so ganz ohne Menschen. Auf dem Sessel dort liegt ein Hut, ein Touristenhut, ganz fest. Hübscher Gemsbart. [...] (Arthur Schnitzler: Fräulein Else)
        -   stream of consciousness: associative sequence of the contents of a character's consciousness (sensations, memories, thoughts, perceptions, etc.)
            > [...] Eine Kugel kam geflogen, gilt sie mir oder gilt sie dir. Sie hat ihn weggerissen, er liegt mir zu den Füßen, als wärs ein Stück von mir. Da steht er: Franz Biberkopf, Arm hat er ab, Kriegsinvalide, der Kerl ist besoffen, oder nicht. Macht er eine Bewegung, knall ich ihn ab. [...] (Alfred Döblin: Berlin Alexanderplatz)

##### Forms and special techniques

-   **Forms**  
    Forms of narrative literature (novel, novella, short story, parable) follow certain rules. Practically all literary texts use such forms, but can also change them.

-   **Special techniques**

    -   _Frame story and embedded story_  
        Starting from one plot, one or more new stories are told.
        For example, a narrator could report that on a journey through a foreign country he heard the following story in an inn ... This story is then told. Frame stories are mainly found in longer texts, e.g. novellas.

    -   _Montage_  
        As in a film (editing technique), disparate (different) elements are inserted into or joined to one another. Such elements can also be of different origin (e.g. advertising, newspaper, recipe, law), including phrases, idioms and empty slogans.

#### Lyric poetry (Lyrik)

If the source text is a lyric (poetic) text type, the text interpretation has some special features. As always, the introduction must name the text type (ballad, sonnet, ...), title, author, year of publication, epoch and theme or motifs (loneliness, grief, ...). In the main part, the standard aspects (just like in the text analysis) and additionally all features (more on this in a moment) must be analysed and interpreted. A special feature of lyric texts is the `lyrical I` (lyrisches Ich). It is a first-person speaker in the poem, who does not have to be _the author_.

Lyric texts are usually structured on three levels. At the top level, there are the stanzas, which are separated by blank lines. Each stanza consists of several verses (the lines) and each verse of a number of syllables. Here is an example poem by Goethe, which consists of two stanzas of six verses each. Most verses have five syllables.

<blockquote>

**Feiger Gedanken bängliches Schwanken**  
_Johann Wolfgang von Goethe_

Feiger Gedanken  
Bängliches Schwanken,  
Weibisches Zagen,  
Ängstliches Klagen  
Wendet kein Elend,  
Macht dich nicht frei.

Allen Gewalten  
Zum Trutz sich erhalten,  
Nimmer sich beugen,  
Kräftig sich zeigen,  
Rufet die Arme  
Der Götter herbei!

</blockquote>

In addition, poems sometimes contain caesuras. A caesura is a metrical break within a verse. You pause slightly at this point. Caesuras are very common in sonnets, for example.

##### Formal analysis

-   **Form of the poem**  
    The form of the poem is the text type of the source text. In other words, it can be a ballad, elegy, hymn, ode, epigram, song, sonnet, ...

-   **Stanzas**  
    The stanza form can be determined by the length and number of stanzas in the poem.

    -   _Sonnet_  
        The sonnet consists of two quatrains (four-line stanzas) and two tercets (three-line stanzas). The quatrains have the rhyme scheme `ABBA`, the tercets, on the other hand, `CDC CDC` or `CCD EED`. The sonnet is common in the Baroque period.

        <blockquote>

        **Natur und Kunst**  
        _Johann Wolfgang von Goethe_

        Natur und Kunst, sie scheinen sich zu fliehen  
        Und haben sich, eh man es denkt, gefunden;  
        Der Widerwille ist auch mir verschwunden,  
        Und beide scheinen gleich mich anzuziehen.

        Es gilt wohl nur ein redliches Bemühen!  
        Und wenn wir erst in abgemeßnen Stunden  
        Mit Geist und Fleiß uns an die Kunst gebunden,  
        Mag frei Natur im Herzen wieder glühen.

        So ists mit aller Bildung auch beschaffen:  
        Vergebens werden ungebundne Geister  
        Nach der Vollendung reiner Höhe streben.

        Wer Großes will, muß sich zusammenraffen;  
        In der Beschränkung zeigt sich erst der Meister,  
        Und das Gesetz nur kann uns Freiheit geben.

        </blockquote>

    -   _Ode_  
        The ode is a solemn, stanzaic and rather long poem with a fixed metre. It is often unrhymed, but there are also rhymed odes such as Schiller's "An die Freude" (Ode to Joy).

        <blockquote>

        **An die Freude**  
        _Friedrich Schiller_

        Freude, schöner Götterfunken,  
        Tochter aus Elisium,  
        Wir betreten feuertrunken  
        Himmlische, dein Heiligthum.  
        Deine Zauber binden wieder,  
        was der Mode Schwerd getheilt;  
        Bettler werden Fürstenbrüder,  
        wo dein sanfter Flügel weilt.

        [...]

        </blockquote>

    -   _Hymn_  
        A hymn is a solemn song of praise. Literary hymns often have no formal regularities and do without structure, metre and rhymes. National and church hymns such as the following, on the other hand, are usually rhymed and divided into stanzas.

        <blockquote>

        **Bundeshymne der Republik Österreich**  
        _Paula Preradović_ (Text), möglicherweise _Johann Baptist Holzer_ oder _Paul Wranitzky_ (Melodie)

        Land der Berge, Land am Strome,  
        Land der Äcker, Land der Dome,  
        Land der Hämmer, zukunftsreich!  
        Heimat großer Töchter und Söhne,  
        Volk, begnadet für das Schöne,  
        vielgerühmtes Österreich.  
        Vielgerühmtes Österreich.

        Heiß umfehdet, wild umstritten,  
        liegst dem Erdteil du inmitten  
        einem starken Herzen gleich.  
        Hast seit frühen Ahnentagen  
        hoher Sendung Last getragen,  
        vielgeprüftes Österreich.  
        Vielgeprüftes Österreich.

        Mutig in die neuen Zeiten,  
        frei und gläubig sieh uns schreiten,  
        arbeitsfroh und hoffnungsreich.  
        Einig laß in Jubelchören,  
        Vaterland, dir Treue schwören,  
        vielgeliebtes Österreich.  
        Vielgeliebtes Österreich.

        </blockquote>

    -   _Ballad_  
        The ballad is divided into stanzas and has fixed metres and rhymes. It usually also has a narrative character.

        <blockquote>

        **Der Erlkönig**  
        _Johann Wolfgang von Goethe_

        Wer reitet so spät durch Nacht und Wind?  
        Es ist der Vater mit seinem Kind;  
        Er hat den Knaben wohl in dem Arm,  
        er fasst ihn sicher, er hält ihn warm.

        Mein Sohn, was birgst du so bang dein Gesicht? -  
        Siehst, Vater, du den Erlkönig nicht?  
        Den Erlenkönig, mit Kron' und Schweif! -  
        Mein Sohn, es ist ein Nebelstreif. -

        Du liebes Kind, komm geh mit mir!  
        Gar schöne Spiele spiel' ich mit dir,  
        Manch' bunte Blumen sind an dem Strand,  
        meine Mutter hat manch gülden Gewand. -

        Mein Vater, mein Vater, und hörest du nicht  
        was Erlenkönig mir leise verspricht? -  
        Sei ruhig, bleibe ruhig, mein Kind;  
        In dürren Blättern säuselt der Wind. -

        Willst, feiner Knabe, du mit mir gehn?  
        Meine Töchter sollen dich warten schön;  
        meine Töchter führen den nächtlichen Reihn,  
        und wiegen und tanzen und singen dich ein. -

        Mein Vater, mein Vater und siehst du nicht dort  
        Erlkönigs Töchter am düsteren Ort? -  
        Mein Sohn, mein Sohn! Ich seh es genau!  
        Es scheinen die alten Weiden so grau! -

        Ich liebe dich! Mich reizt deine schöne Gestalt;  
        und bist du nicht willig, so brauch' ich Gewalt. -  
        Mein Vater, mein Vater, jetzt fasst er mich an!  
        Erlkönig hat mir ein Leids getan! -

        Den Vater grauset's, er reitet geschwind,  
        er hält in den Armen das ächzende Kind,  
        erreicht den Hof mit Müh und Not;  
        in seinen Armen das Kind war tot.

        </blockquote>

-   **Metre**  
    The metre describes which syllables within the individual verses are stressed (Hebungen) or unstressed (Senkungen).

    -   _Names of the metres_

        -   **Iamb (Jambus)**  
            The iamb always consists of two syllables, the second of which is stressed: `-‘`. An iamb therefore consists of an unstressed and a stressed syllable.

            | Examples of iambs    |
            | -------------------- |
            | bemüht               |
            | verkehrt             |
            | Geduld               |

        -   **Trochee (Trochäus)**  
            The trochee always consists of two syllables, the first of which is stressed: `‘-`. A trochee therefore consists of a stressed and an unstressed syllable.

            | Examples of trochees   |
            | ---------------------- |
            | Sieger                 |
            | Rabe                   |
            | mähen                  |

        -   **Anapaest (Anapäst)**  
            The anapaest always consists of three syllables, the last of which is stressed: `--‘`. An anapaest therefore consists of two unstressed syllables and a stressed one.

            | Examples of anapaests |
            | --------------------- |
            | Elefant               |
            | reserviert            |
            | analog                |

        -   **Dactyl (Daktylus)**  
            The dactyl always consists of three syllables, the first of which is stressed: `‘--`. A dactyl therefore consists of a stressed syllable and two unstressed ones.

            | Examples of dactyls    |
            | ---------------------- |
            | untere                 |
            | jugendlich             |
            | eisige                 |

    -   _Number of stresses_  
        To determine the length of a verse, there is the analytical unit of stresses. Here, you simply count the occurrences of the respective metre in a verse. To illustrate this, let's take this beautiful verse by Schiller from "Das Mädchen aus der Fremde":

        > Doch nahte sich ein liebend Paar

        Try to say the verse out loud and recognise the metre from the stressed and unstressed syllables. Small spoiler: it is the `iamb`, since the stresses are on `nah`, `sich`, `lie` and `Paar`. These stresses can be symbolised like this: `x` `x́` `x` `x́` `x` `x́` `x` `x́`. Normal `x`s stand for unstressed syllables; a stressed syllable is marked with an acute accent (`x́`).

        From this sequence, you can easily see that the verse has **four stresses**. We therefore call the verse a `four-stress iamb` (iambic tetrameter). Since the verse also ends with a stressed syllable, we speak of a complete iamb. If an iambic verse has an additional unstressed syllable at the end, it is called hypercatalectic. If, on the other hand, a syllable is missing at the end, it is called incomplete or catalectic. The following example is a three-stress hypercatalectic iamb:

        > Da ist in meinem Herzen  
        > `x` `x́`&nbsp;&nbsp;`x` `x́`&nbsp;&nbsp;`x` `x́`&nbsp;&nbsp;`x`

    -   _Special forms_
        -   **masculine / blunt**: the line ends with a stressed syllable
        -   **feminine / sounding**: the line ends with an unstressed syllable
        ***
        -   **Blank verse**: unrhymed five-stress iamb (iambic pentameter)  
            (`x` `x́` `x` `x́` `x` `x́` `x` `x́` `x` `x́`)
        -   **Alexandrine**: six-stress iamb with a caesura (`/`), usually in the middle  
            (`x` `x́` `x` `x́` `x` `x́` `/` `x` `x́` `x` `x́` `x` `x́`)
        -   **Hexameter**: six-foot dactyl (the last syllable of the last dactyl is dropped)  
            (`x́` `x` `x` `x́` `x` `x` `x́` `x` `x` `x́` `x` `x` `x́` `x` `x` `x́` `x`)
        -   **Free rhythm**: no uniform rhythm

-   **Rhyme**

    -   _Rhyme scheme_  
        The rhyme scheme describes which verses within a stanza rhyme with each other. It is not always simply the two consecutive verses that rhyme.

        -   **Rhyming couplet (Paarreim)**  
            The rhyming couplet is probably the kind most people know, since the verses that follow each other always rhyme. There is a notation that describes this pattern: `AA BB CC`. The notation is read in such a way that each letter symbolises a verse and identical letters, i.e. verses, rhyme with each other.

            <blockquote>

            **Bewaffneter Friede** -- Wilhelm Busch  
            `a` Ganz unverhofft auf einem Hügel  
            `a` sind sich begegnet Fuchs und Igel.  
            `b` Halt! rief der Fuchs, du Bösewicht!  
            `b` Kennst du des Königs Order nicht!  
            `c` Ist nicht der Friede längst verkündigt,  
            `c` Und weißt du nicht, daß jeder sündigt,  
            `d` der immer noch gerüstet geht!  
            `d` Im Namen seiner Majestät,  
            `e` komm her und übergib dein Fell!

            </blockquote>

        -   **Alternate rhyme (Kreuzreim)**  
            The alternate rhyme has the scheme `ABAB CDCD EFEF`.

            <blockquote>

            **Zwei Segel** -- Conrad Ferdinand Meyer  
            `a` Zwei Segel erhellend  
            `b` Die tiefblaue Bucht!  
            `a` Zwei Segel sich schwellend  
            `b` Zu ruhiger Flucht!

            `c` Wie eins in den Winden  
            `d` Sich wölbt und bewegt,  
            `c` Wird auch das Empfinden  
            `d` Des anderen erregt.

            `e` Begehrt eins zu hasten,  
            `f` Das andre geht schnell,  
            `e` Verlangt eins zu rasten,  
            `f` Ruht auch sein Gesell.

            </blockquote>

        -   **Enclosed rhyme (umarmender Reim)**  
            The enclosed rhyme has the pattern `ABBA CDDC EFFE`.

            <blockquote>

            **Im Winter** -- Georg Trakl  
            `a` Der Acker leuchtet weiß und kalt.  
            `b` Der Himmel ist einsam und ungeheuer.  
            `b` Dohlen kreisen über dem Weiher  
            `a` Und Jäger steigen nieder vom Wald.

            `c` Ein Schweigen in schwarzen Wipfeln wohnt.  
            `d` Ein Feuerschein huscht aus den Hütten.  
            `d` Bisweilen schellt sehr fern ein Schlitten  
            `c` Und langsam steigt der graue Mond.

            `e` Ein Wild verblutet sanft am Rain  
            `f` Und Raben plätschern in blutigen Gossen.  
            `f` Das Rohr bebt gelb und aufgeschossen.  
            `e` Frost, Rauch, ein Schritt im leeren Hain.

            </blockquote>

        -   **Monorhyme (Haufenreim)**  
            In a monorhyme, everything in the stanza rhymes with each other. The pattern would then look like this, with any number of verses per stanza: `AAAA BBBB CCCC`.

            <blockquote>

            **Der Wald** -- unbekannt  
            `a` Im Wald  
            `a` sind die Bäume alt,  
            `a` die Luft ist kalt,  
            `a` meine Stimme schallt.  
            `a` Oh wie schön ist es doch im Wald.

            </blockquote>

        -   **Interlaced rhyme (verschränkter Reim)**  
            The interlaced rhyme has the scheme `ABCABC`.

            <blockquote>

            **Der Kuß im Traume** -- Karoline von Günderrode  
            `a` Der Tag ist karg an liebesüßen Wonnen,  
            `b` Es schmerzt mich seines Lichtes eitles Prangen  
            `c` Und mich verzehren seiner Sonne Gluthen.

            `a` Drum birg dich Aug' dem Glanze irrd'scher Sonnen!  
            `b` Hüll' dich in Nacht, sie stillet dein Verlangen  
            `c` Und heilt den Schmerz, wie Lethes kühle Fluthen.

            </blockquote>

        -   **Tail rhyme (Schweifreim)**  
            The tail rhyme has the pattern `AA B CC B` and always consists of six verses. It is a combination of the rhyming couplet and the enclosed rhyme.

            <blockquote>

            **Der Mond ist aufgegangen** -- Matthias Claudius  
            `a` Der Mond ist aufgegangen  
            `a` Die goldnen Sternlein prangen  
            `b` &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Am Himmel hell und klar:  
            `c` Der Wald steht schwarz und schweiget,  
            `c` Und aus den Wiesen steiget  
            `b` &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Der weiße Nebel wunderbar.

            </blockquote>

        -   **Chain rhyme (Kettenreim)**  
            The chain rhyme has the scheme `ABA BCB CDC`. This rhyme therefore always links three-line stanzas.

            <blockquote>

            **Im ernsten Beinhaus war's** -- Johann Wolfgang von Goethe  
            `a` Im ernsten Beinhaus wars, wo ich beschaute,  
            `b` &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wie Schädel Schädeln angeordnet paßten;  
            `a` &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Die alte Zeit gedacht ich, die ergraute.  
            `b` Sie stehn in Reih geklemmt, die sonst sich haßten,  
            `c` &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Und derbe Knochen, die sich tödlich schlugen,  
            `b` &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Sie liegen kreuzweis, zahm allhier zu rasten.  
            `c` Entrenkte Schulterblätter! was sie trugen,  
            `d` &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Fragt niemand mehr, und zierlich tätge Glieder,  
            `c` &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Die Hand, der Fuß, zerstreut aus Lebensfugen.  
            `d` Ihr Müden also lagt vergebens nieder,  
            `e` &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Nicht Ruh im Grabe ließ man euch, vertrieben  
            `d` &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Seid ihr herauf zum lichten Tage wieder,

            </blockquote>

        -   **Orphan (Waise)**  
            The orphan is not a rhyme scheme in the proper sense. It refers to an unrhymed verse within a stanza (or as its own stanza) that is otherwise rhymed. This gives the verse special emphasis. The rhyme scheme is sometimes written like this: `AABBX`.

            <blockquote>

            **Die Teutoburger Schlacht** -- Joseph Victor von Scheffel  
            `a` Als die Römer frech geworden,  
            `a` zogen sie nach Deutschlands Norden.  
            `b` Vorne mit Trompetenschall  
            `b` ritt der Generalfeldmarschall,  
            `x` Herr Quinctilius Varus.

            </blockquote>

    -   _Rhyme position_  
        The rhyme position describes which syllables of the verses rhyme with each other.

        -   **End rhyme**  
            The most typical position is the end rhyme, in which the last syllable(s) rhyme with each other.

            > Max und Moritz, gar nicht tr**äge**,  
            > Sägen heimlich mit der S**äge**.

        -   **Initial rhyme**  
            The rhyming words are at the beginning of the line.

            > M**unter** malt das Kind ein Bild,  
            > B**unter** wird’s auf dem Papier.

        -   **Internal rhyme**  
            Here, at least one of the rhyming words is inside the verse.

            > Ritzeratze! voller T**ücke**,  
            > In die Br**ücke** eine L**ücke**.

            A special form of internal rhyme is the _middle rhyme_: two words inside two consecutive verses rhyme with each other.

            > Mit Entz**ücken** stellt sie fest,  
            > Dass das Obst sich Pfl**ücken** lässt.

        -   **Adjacent rhyme (Schlagreim)**  
            Two words stand directly next to each other and rhyme.

            > Qu**ellende**, schw**ellende** Nacht

            A special form of the adjacent rhyme is the _echo rhyme_, in which the two words are at the end of the verse.

            > Ihm ist, als ob es tausend St**äbe** g**äbe**

        -   **Overlapping rhyme**  
            The end of a verse rhymes with the first word of the following line.

            > Mein Kind, du musst noch **üben**  
            > Dr**üben** bei der Großmutter!

        -   **Transitional rhyme**  
            The first and the last word of a verse rhyme with each other.

            > G**eld** regiert die W**elt**.

        -   **Caesura rhyme**  
            If verses have a caesura (`/`), there are two further special rhyme positions:

            -   The word before the caesura rhymes with the end of the verse.

                > Kommt her und sch**aut** / was wurde geb**aut**.

            -   The words before the caesura in two verses (long line) rhyme with each other.

                > Uns ist in alten m**æren** / wunders vil geseit  
                > von Helden lobeb**æren**, / von grôzer arebeit.

    -   _Phonology_  
        Phonology describes the quality of the rhyme.

        -   **Perfect rhyme**  
            The syllables after the stressed vowel match exactly, i.e. they are identical. It is the most common kind of rhyme.

            | Examples of perfect rhymes |
            | ------------------------- |
            | Haus -- Maus              |
            | Woche -- koche            |
            | fließt -- vergießt        |
            | Feuer -- teuer            |
            | abzufahren -- offenbaren  |

        -   **Imperfect rhyme**  
            In an imperfect rhyme, syllables are combined that only approximately rhyme. They are therefore not identical.

            | Examples of imperfect rhymes |
            | --------------------------- |
            | lieben -- siegen            |
            | Höh’n -- stehn              |
            | Geläute -- Weite            |
            | Lied -- Gemüt               |
            | sprießen -- grüßen          |

        -   **Identical rhyme**  
            In an identical rhyme, the same word is repeated.

            > Wer sind die tausendmal **tausend**,  
            > Die myriadenmal hundert **tausend**?

        -   **Equivocal rhyme**  
            Here, words are rhymed that sound the same but have different meanings and are often spelled differently – so-called homophones.

            | Examples of equivocal rhymes |
            | ---------------------------- |
            | Waise -- weise               |
            | Geld -- gellt                |

        -   **Assonance**  
            In this case, only the vowels of the rhyming words match, but not the consonants. Another name is vocalic half-rhyme. It is one of the most popular kinds of rhyme in rap.

            > Ich weiß, die primitive Prollschiene  
            > Ist eine ziemlich sichere Goldmine.

        -   **Pararhyme**  
            In this rhyme, the consonants match, but the stressed vowels differ.

            | Examples of pararhymes |
            | ---------------------- |
            | Rosen -- Rasen         |
            | Mond -- Mund           |

        -   **Rich rhyme**  
            The rich rhyme already begins in the penultimate stressed syllable.

            | Examples of rich rhymes                                   |
            | ------------------------------------------------------ |
            | Tugendreiche -- Jugendstreiche                         |
            | Herzensreiche -- Kerzenzeiche                          |
            | viel zu schnelle -- Sinuswelle (mixed with assonance)  |

        -   **Double rhyme**  
            All syllables of two polysyllabic words rhyme.

            | Examples of double rhymes  |
            | -------------------------- |
            | bitter sein -- Ritter sein |

        -   **Spoonerism rhyme (Schüttelreim)**  
            A special form of the double rhyme: the initial consonants of a polysyllabic rhyme are swapped and combined into new words.

            | Examples of spoonerism rhymes |
            | --------------------------- |
            | Satteldecke -- Dattelsäcke  |
            | Schiffsdeck -- Beefsteak    |
            | boßt rief -- Roastbeef      |

        -   **Historical rhyme**  
            In the past, the words were pronounced differently and rhymed. Today, however, the rhyme no longer works.

            | Examples of historical rhymes                                                                  |
            | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
            | lieber -- brüeder (in the Middle Ages, ie and üe were pronounced as separate sounds)           |

        -   **Prefix rhyme (Vorreim)**  
            The rhyming words are extended by prefixes that sound the same.

            | Examples of prefix rhymes |
            | ---------------------- |
            | Beklagen -- Betragen   |

        -   **Single-syllable rhyme**  
            Here, _unstressed / secondary stressed_ syllables are rhymed with each other.

            > Die Blitze zucken w**enn**  
            > Die Hexen wieder flieg**en**

        -   **Alliterative verse (Stabreim)**  
            An old form of rhyme: instead of sound rhymes, the Germanic peoples wrote their poems with alliteration.

            > Eines weiß ich, das ewig lebet: der **T**oten **T**atenruhm

    -   _Morphology and lexis_  
        Some rhymes show special features at word level. This concerns, for example, word formation (morphology) or vocabulary (lexis), from which further kinds of rhyme can be derived.

        -   **Eye rhyme**  
            In an eye rhyme, the spelling matches, but the words do not rhyme when pronounced.

            > Lage -- Garage

        -   **Ear rhyme**  
            In an ear rhyme, the spelling of the words differs clearly, but the sounds rhyme.

            > mobile phone –- derweil schon

        -   **Broken rhyme**  
            Only the first part of a word rhymes, while the second part is in the next line. This is also an enjambment.

            > Jeder weiß, was so ein Mai–  
            > Käfer für ein Vogel sei.

        -   **Split rhyme**  
            A polysyllabic word rhymes with several single words.

            > Und verwundert steht und spricht er:  
            > „Zapperment! Das Ding werd lichter!“

        -   **Twin rhyme**  
            Twin rhymes consist of the same letters in the same order. However, a different word division creates different meanings.

            > Alter Eimer, zeige Treue,  
            > alte Reimer zeiget Reue!

        -   **Grammatical rhyme**  
            Two words with the same word stem are combined. The similarity of sound does not matter.

            > Verse, wie sie Bassus **schreibt**,  
            > Werden unvergänglich bleiben:  
            > Weil dergleichen Zeug zu **schreiben**  
            > Stets ein Stümper übrig bleibt.

        -   **Teasing rhyme (Vexierreim)**  
            The reader expects a certain rhyming word, which does not come.

            > Am Dienstag hört er einen Marsch  
            > Und Bums, da liegt er auf dem **Bauch**.

        -   **Reversed rhyme**  
            In a reversed rhyme, individual letters of the rhyming words are swapped.

            > Du musst dich nicht wu**n**dern,  
            > Weil wir so wu**r**den.

-   **Cadences**  
    The cadence refers to the end of a verse. More precisely, the cadence describes how many syllables at the end of the verse rhyme and which of them are stressed.

    -   _one syllable_  
        The last syllable of a verse rhymes and is stressed. This is called a masculine or blunt cadence.

        > Der Nebel drückt die Dächer schw**er**.  
        > Und durch die Stille braust das M**eer**.

    -   _two syllables_  
        The last two syllables rhyme. The stress is on the penultimate syllable (feminine, sounding cadence).

        > Er stand auf seines Daches Z**innen**,  
        > Er schaute mit vergnügten S**innen**

    -   _three syllables_  
        A stressed syllable at the end of the verse is followed by two unstressed ones. Together they form the rhyme (rich / full cadence).

        > Wir sind die Tr**eibenden** […]  
        > Im immer Bl**eibenden**.

##### Content analysis

-   **Stanzas and structure**  
    What are the individual stanzas about? Can the stanzas be structured in some way (intensification, climax, turning point), or are the stanzas themselves the structure?

-   **Characters**  
    Which characters appear? Who is the speaker? Who is being addressed? What does the audience learn about the characters?

-   **Place and time**  
    Where and when does the action take place? Are the places mentioned (if any appear in the stanzas) real, or do they only have a symbolic meaning? Interpret the place and its meaning.

    In which period is the lyrical narrative set? Time can also have a symbolic meaning, which you have to interpret. For example, autumn in Rainer Maria Rilke's poem "Herbsttag" has a great influence on people's loneliness and generally symbolises the end of summer.

#### Drama (Dramatik)

If the source text is a dramatic text type, the following features must be analysed and interpreted:

##### Dramatic genre

-   **Text type**  
    Is the source text a tragedy, a comedy or a tragicomedy? The first has a very negative mood, the second is meant to entertain the audience and the last is sad on the one hand, but also has a funny side to it. Above all, stories of rogues are set in tragicomedy.

    -   _Comedy_  
        At the centre of the comedy are people of lower social standing, norms and institutions that are exposed to ridicule. The comedy is characterised from the start by a loose and light structure. Prevailing conditions are turned upside down, public figures and political grievances are exposed to biting mockery. The focus is on portraying the inadequacies and weaknesses of people and of human life. Amusement comes from situational and verbal comedy. The language is on a low level so that the language use of lower social classes is represented appropriately. The protagonists of a comedy are also exposed to a conflict, which, however, is overcome.

    -   _Tragedy_  
        Besides the comedy, the tragedy is the second main form of drama. At the centre of the tragedy is a person of higher social standing who faces a tragic, irresolvable conflict that leads to the inevitable downfall of the hero, either in the form of death or another miserable state. The tragedy is meant to show the audience that the human scope for action and decision-making is limited and that people cannot escape their destiny through their own actions. According to Aristotle's ancient poetics, the fate of the tragic hero should above all arouse fear and pity in the audience, so that their emotional purification (catharsis) is brought about. The audience should learn to pity those who deserve it and to fear the things they themselves must fear. The language of the tragedy can be characterised as high, dignified, elevated, precise and to the point.

    -   _Tragicomedy_  
        A mixture of tragedy and comedy is the tragicomedy. The term goes back to the Roman poet Plautus; in German literature, the form gained importance above all from the "Sturm und Drang" onwards. Tragedy and comedy are inseparably linked and reinforce each other. Comic moments increase the effect of tragic contexts.

-   **Analysis vs synthesis**  
    Is the drama analytical or synthetic?

    -   _Analytical dramas_  
        An analytical drama, drama of discovery or drama of revelation is a drama that builds on a certain incident in the backstory of the events portrayed. At first, only the effects of this incident can be seen; their cause is only discovered or revealed step by step in the course of the plot.

        Two levels of action can therefore be identified in an analytical drama:

        -   The backstory, in which an event takes place whose consequences extend into the present on stage, but which is not yet known at the beginning of the performance (to the audience also because it is not shown on stage).
        -   The immediate events on stage, in which the past event is analytically uncovered. This revelation can in turn have an effect on the present portrayed.

        <br/>

    -   _Synthetic dramas_  
        A synthetic drama, drama of development, conflict or goal is a drama that begins with a certain incident or process. It acts as the trigger for the further events and thus unfolds the plot. Even if a backstory can play a certain role, the course of the action is directed towards an event in the future – the eponymous goal.

        At the centre of the events there is usually an internal or external conflict:

        -   An internal conflict exists inside the hero, who is torn between two decisions.
        -   An external conflict, on the other hand, exists between the protagonist and an antagonist, an opponent who pursues a goal opposed to the hero's intentions.

-   **Openness**
    A distinction is made between closed and open dramas.

    -   _Closed dramas_  
        A closed drama contains a main plot that is goal-oriented. Individual plot steps build on each other causally; scenes are not interchangeable. The protagonists of the closed drama are mature and can act autonomously and thus determine what happens. The number of main characters is limited; there are only a few main and secondary characters. The constellation of characters is clear and comprehensible for the audience.

        In a closed drama, the hero and the opponent often face each other. A triangular situation in which three protagonists interact is also possible. The closed drama is characterised by clarifying the backstory in the exposition, an introductory beginning, the emergence of a conflict and a clear resolution at the end.

        The drama is written in verse. However, the protagonists can be individualised through their use of language. The action takes place within a short period of time and is limited to a few locations. It is not reality that is brought to the stage, but an idealised image of it.

        A very well-known closed drama is "Don Carlos, Infant von Spanien", written by Friedrich Schiller in 1787.

    -   _Open dramas_  
        The open drama usually contains several equally important plots; overall, the individual scenes are loosely structured. Connections in content are shown through characters and leitmotifs. The protagonists are anti-heroes who cannot act autonomously but are influenced by their social environment. They have little scope for action and decisions. The constellation of characters in the open drama is rather confusing, since many characters from different social classes are involved.

        The backstory is not explained in detail, and the ending often remains unclear. This kind of drama is written in everyday language; stylistic devices such as parataxis (sequence of main clauses), enumeration, broken sentences and ellipses (omission of parts of a sentence) are often used. The action usually spans several years in total, and many different locations are shown, which can also be far apart. The work "Dantons Tod" by Georg Büchner (1835) is one of the best-known open dramas.

##### The three Aristotelian unities

The three Aristotelian unities describe the unity of place, time and action. For dramatic texts, you have to analyse whether and how these unities have been observed.

-   **Place**  
    Where does the drama take place?

-   **Time**  
    Similar to the interpretation of an epic text, the time that passes in and between the scenes is compared. It is also analysed when the play is supposed to be set.

-   **Action**  
    The analysis of the action includes questions such as: are there subplots, or does the entire drama follow a single main plot? Is the action self-contained, i.e. is there a clear beginning, middle and end?

##### Characters

-   **List of characters**  
    Some dramas have a list of characters (dramatis personae) that explains the characters and their roles in the drama. Use this list to analyse and describe the characters. List the most important characters and interpret why they act in a certain way in certain situations!

-   **Characterisation of the characters**  
    The audience learns in some way which characters appear in the play. Analyse these ways and also describe what characteristics the different characters have. Only refer to the most important characters in the drama. The development of the main character is particularly interesting. Answer whether the events change this character's way of thinking over time and what effect this in turn has on the drama.

    Also discuss the feelings, intentions and thoughts of the most important characters. They usually play an essential role with regard to the ending of all plot lines.

-   **Sociogram**  
    How do the characters relate to each other? Analyse and interpret the connections and relationships of all important characters.

##### Stage design

Mention the explanations of the stage set and the design of the stage in your text, if they exist. You cannot analyse the stage set itself, since there is no stage in the text. However, some authors describe before each scene what the characters' surroundings should look like. Analyse these surroundings.

Describe whether the stage set is realistic, symbolic or anti-illusionistic!

##### Structure of the drama

In dramas, authors have no limits. Some authors therefore describe scenes in as much detail as in a picture book, while others focus much more on the action. Analyse whether structures such as acts, film-like images or scenes can be recognised and how these parts are arranged. How well does _the author_ handle the three Aristotelian unities? How well does _the author_ succeed in building up suspense in the drama, and what approach do _they_ use to maintain this level of suspense so that the audience stays glued to their seats? At which point in the play is the dramatic climax, or is there perhaps a "moment of final suspense"? Is the ending open, or are all the audience's questions answered?

##### Stage directions

Sometimes dramas contain instructions from "off stage" to describe certain actions or to interact directly with the characters. Explain why _the author_ chose this way of narrating. Is it more humorous this way?

There can be instructions for several elements of the drama:

-   direction
-   casting
-   actors
-   costume design
-   make-up
-   props
-   lighting
-   sound
-   ...

##### Language of the drama

Is the play mostly written as a dialogue or predominantly as a monologue? If the characters have dialogues, do they talk past each other or rather with each other? Are the dialogues realistic or absurd? Can you put yourself in the character's shoes psychologically?

What language do the characters speak (dialect, vernacular, standard stage German)? Do the characters speak according to their social position? Is the drama written in verse or in prose? Is there a special "artificial language" in the drama or for individual characters? If monologues occur, when and how are they used (conflict monologues, "messenger report monologue", ...)?

Does a chorus appear? What function does it have? How does it speak? Does the chorus summarise the events, foresee them or only comment on the action?


## Opinion speech (Meinungsrede)

The opinion speech is a subjective, argumentative, persuasive text type that is delivered in front of a specific audience. For this reason, all listeners must be addressed ([salutation](#salutation-1)).

The purpose of the opinion speech is to convince the audience of your own opinion by means of conclusive arguments. Depending on the audience, the opinion speech can be written formally (e.g. `Sie` / `Ihren`) or informally (e.g. `du` / `euren`). The opinion speech is written in the present tense and the present perfect and should not contain any insults or discrimination.

### Characteristics

-   `Ich` (I) should be used
-   convince the audience of my opinion (address the audience)
-   creative introduction that mentions the title and author
-   the main part argues fluently
-   summarising conclusion with an appeal
-   use [stylistic devices](#stylistic-devices)
-   write at most 50 words more than required
-   heading related to the situation (if required)
-   rather long text type; sometimes also medium-length

### Structure

#### Heading

If the task requires a specific heading, it should be used.

#### Salutation

The task describes a situation that explains the setting and the people in front of whom you deliver the opinion speech. From this situation, you have to infer or decide which people you want to and should address in the salutation. All genders must always be addressed if you do not know whether the audience consists exclusively of men or women. If a person of authority or of higher rank than yourself is in the audience, you must speak formally (`Sie` / `Ihren` / ...). If you know that only your friends are in the audience, you can – and it would probably make sense – speak informally (`du` / `euren` / ...).

> Sehr geehrte Schülerinnen und Schüler!  
> Sehr geehrte Professorinnen und Professoren!

> Liebe Klassenkameraden!

#### Introduction

An opinion speech has a creative introduction that should at least mention the author and the title of the source text. However, not all data of the source text has to appear in the introduction. For example, the date or place of publication is not required. Overall, the introduction should comprise about 50 words.

#### Main part

First, summarise the topic of the opinion speech. This beginning of the main part is important so that everyone in the audience knows the necessary information about the topic.

Then mention a few arguments (one paragraph each) and justify them in detail. As help, there are various [speech structure models](#speech-structure-models) to make the opinion speech self-contained and fluent.

Address the audience and link your examples to typical everyday life so that the listeners can relate to these situations and support your opinion.

> Sie wissen das ja auch.  
> Jeder kennt den Moment, wenn der Toast vom Tellerrand kippt und man weiß bereits vorher, dass er 100%ig auf der Marmeladenseite aufklatschen wird.

Throughout the main part, your language should emphasise that the arguments are your own opinion.

##### Speech structure models

###### AIDA formula

| Abbreviation | Content                                                                                            |
| --------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Attention | Create an emotional connection, for example by telling something outrageous.                          |
| Interest  | Arouse the audience's interest by telling the content of the argument in more detail                  |
| Desire    | Present the desired target state.                                                                     |
| Action    | Suggest measures to achieve this desired state.                                                       |

###### ABBA principle

| Abbreviation (German)     | Content                                                                                       |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Anfang (beginning)        | The starting point of the speech, which is meant to win the audience's attention.             |
| Behauptung (claim) or hypothesis | The clear statement or assumption that is represented in the speech.                   |
| Belege (evidence)         | Facts, arguments or examples that support the claim and are meant to convince.                |
| Abschluss (conclusion)    | A summarising part that ends the speech and leaves a lasting message.                         |

###### AAAA principle

| Abbreviation (German) | Content                                                                                   |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Aufhänger (hook)      | The introductory part that arouses interest and captivates the listeners.                 |
| Anekdote (anecdote)   | A short, personal story or episode that brings the topic to life.                         |
| Ansprache (address)   | The main part of the speech, in which the actual message or information is conveyed.      |
| Abschluss (conclusion) | The final part that rounds off the speech, offers a summary or calls for action.         |

###### Three times formula

Divide your main part into three different segments that should answer these questions. See the questions as a guideline.

-   **Background**: How did people behave in the past? What was the situation like before?
-   **Current situation**: What has actually changed? What is the situation like now?
-   **Goals for the future**: What will change? Can the situation be improved in the future?

This model cannot always be applied, since these comparisons do not exist, especially for topics that have only recently emerged.

###### Plus-minus formula

With the plus-minus formula, you contrast the advantages and disadvantages using pro and contra arguments in such a way that your own concern takes effect.

#### Conclusion

Summarise your opinion briefly once more and appeal with suggested solutions that should apply to all arguments.

#### Closing

Just like in a presentation, you thank the audience in the closing.

> Danke für die Aufmerksamkeit!
