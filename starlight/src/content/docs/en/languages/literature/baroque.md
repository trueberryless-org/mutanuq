The **Baroque** is the epoch of contrasts: magnificent palaces and festivities exist alongside war, plague and hunger, joy of life alongside fear of death. The name probably comes from the Portuguese _barroco_, an irregularly shaped pearl, and was originally meant disparagingly ("bombastic", "overloaded").

## Key facts

- c. 1600 – 1720
- Thirty Years' War (1618 – 1648)
- absolutism
- contrasts: this world and the next, joy of life and fear of death
- key motifs: **vanitas**, **memento mori**, **carpe diem**
- most popular form: the **sonnet**

## Historical background

The **Thirty Years' War** devastated large parts of Central Europe. In some areas, a third or more of the population died from fighting, epidemics and hunger. After the Peace of Westphalia in 1648, the territorial princes consolidated their power in **absolutism**: the ruler governed without restriction, and his court became the cultural centre. The best-known model was the French King Louis XIV with the Palace of Versailles. In Austria, Melk Abbey and the Karlskirche in Vienna, among others, bear witness to the Baroque.

## World view and motifs

The experience of war and death shaped the attitude to life. Three Latin key motifs sum it up:

| Motif              | Meaning                                | Message                                                        |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Vanitas**        | vanity, futility, transience           | Everything earthly is transient and worthless.                 |
| **Memento mori**   | "Remember that you will die"           | Death can come at any time – live so that you can stand before God. |
| **Carpe diem**     | "Seize the day"                        | Because life is short, you should enjoy it.                    |

Vanitas and carpe diem are two answers to the same experience of transience. Typical symbols are skulls, hourglasses, burnt-out candles and withering flowers.

## Language and form

Baroque poetry followed strict rules. **Martin Opitz** laid them down in his "Buch von der deutschen Poeterey" (Book of German Poetry, 1624): German verses should match stress and metre, preferably in the **alexandrine** (six-stress iamb with a caesura in the middle). The caesura was well suited to presenting opposites (**antitheses**).

The language is rich in stylistic devices: metaphors, accumulations, antitheses and allegories. The **sonnet**, with two quatrains and two tercets, was the most popular form of poem. To cultivate the German language, scholars founded **language societies** such as the "Fruchtbringende Gesellschaft" (Fruitbearing Society).

## Authors and works

### Andreas Gryphius (1616 – 1664)

As a child, Andreas Gryphius experienced the Thirty Years' War and lost his parents at an early age. His sonnets are marked by the experience of vanitas. In "Tränen des Vaterlandes" (Tears of the Fatherland, 1636), he describes the horrors of war; in "Es ist alles eitel" (All Is Vanity), the transience of everything earthly.

<blockquote>

**Es ist alles eitel** (beginning) – Andreas Gryphius

Du siehst, wohin du siehst, nur Eitelkeit auf Erden.  
Was dieser heute baut, reißt jener morgen ein;  
Wo jetzund Städte stehn, wird eine Wiese sein,  
Auf der ein Schäferskind wird spielen mit den Herden.

</blockquote>

The first verse shows the alexandrine with a caesura: "Du siehst, wohin du siehst, / nur Eitelkeit auf Erden." (Wherever you look, you see only vanity on earth.) The following verses each contrast the present and the future antithetically.

### Hans Jakob Christoffel von Grimmelshausen (c. 1622 – 1676)

His novel "**Der abenteuerliche Simplicissimus Teutsch**" (1668) is the most important German novel of the 17th century. It is a **picaresque novel**: the naive Simplicius grows up with a hermit, gets caught up in the turmoil of the Thirty Years' War and, as a soldier, fool and adventurer, experiences the cruelty and inconstancy of the world. In the end, he withdraws from the world as a hermit himself. Grimmelshausen drew on his own war experiences.

### Other representatives

- **Martin Opitz** (1597 – 1639): rules of poetry in the "Buch von der deutschen Poeterey"
- **Paul Fleming** (1609 – 1640): love poetry and sonnets
- **Christian Hofmann von Hofmannswaldau** (1617 – 1679): gallant, sensual poetry, carpe diem motif
- **Angelus Silesius** (1624 – 1677): mystical epigrams in the "Cherubinischer Wandersmann"
- **Abraham a Sancta Clara** (1644 – 1709): Viennese court preacher, known for vivid, popular sermons

## Genres

- **Poetry:** sonnet, epigram, hymn – the most important genre of the Baroque
- **Epic:** picaresque novel, courtly-historical novel, pastoral novel
- **Drama:** tragedies (for example by Gryphius), Jesuit theatre and magnificent operas at the courts
