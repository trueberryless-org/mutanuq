After the First World War and the pathos of [Expressionism](/en/languages/literature/expressionism/), many authors turned to a **sober, objective** depiction of reality. They described life in the big city, the world of work, the consequences of the war and economic hardship. Literature was meant to be understandable for a wide audience and useful. Alongside New Objectivity, the great novels of classical modernism were also written between the wars.

## Key facts

- c. 1920 – 1933
- Weimar Republic and the First Republic in Austria
- objectivity, sobriety, distance instead of feeling and pathos
- big city, white-collar workers, unemployment, inflation
- utility poetry, reportage, contemporary novel
- new media: film, radio, illustrated magazines
- epic theatre (Brecht)

## Historical background

The **First World War** (1914 – 1918) ended with the collapse of the monarchies: Germany became the Weimar Republic, and Austria-Hungary broke up into several states, including the Republic of (German-)Austria. Inflation (1923) and the Great Depression (from 1929) led to mass unemployment and poverty. At the same time, culture, cinema, jazz and nightlife flourished in Berlin and Vienna in the "Roaring Twenties". Political conflicts intensified until the National Socialists came to power in Germany in 1933 and the corporate state was established in Austria in 1934.

## Features of New Objectivity

- **Sobriety:** Reality is depicted precisely, with distance and without transfiguration or pathos.
- **Documentary elements:** Reportage, factual reports and montage techniques from newspapers and film find their way into literature.
- **Themes:** big city, working life, white-collar workers, unemployment, soldiers returning from the war, the "new woman".
- **Utility poetry:** Poems should be understandable and speak to people's everyday lives, often ironically and satirically.
- **Language:** simple, clear, often colloquial.

## Authors and works

### Erich Kästner (1899 – 1974)

Kästner is best known as a children's author ("Emil und die Detektive", Emil and the Detectives, 1929). In "**Fabian. Die Geschichte eines Moralisten**" (Going to the Dogs, 1931), he describes Berlin during the economic crisis from the perspective of an unemployed advertising copywriter. His ironic, socially critical utility poetry appeared in volumes such as "Herz auf Taille" (1928).

### Bertolt Brecht (1898 – 1956)

Brecht developed **epic theatre**. Unlike Aristotelian theatre, in which the audience identifies and empathises with the characters, epic theatre is meant to make the audience think critically. This is the purpose of the alienation effect: actors address the audience directly, songs interrupt the action, and placards announce what is going to happen. The audience should recognise that social conditions can be changed.

- "Die Dreigroschenoper" (The Threepenny Opera, 1928, music by Kurt Weill), with the song of Mack the Knife
- "Mutter Courage und ihre Kinder" (Mother Courage and Her Children, written in exile in 1939): a canteen woman wants to profit from the Thirty Years' War and loses all three of her children.
- "Der gute Mensch von Sezuan" (The Good Person of Szechwan, written in exile): the good Shen Te can only survive by disguising herself as her hard-hearted cousin Shui Ta.

### Alfred Döblin (1878 – 1957)

"**Berlin Alexanderplatz**" (1929) is the most important German big-city novel. Franz Biberkopf, a released prisoner, wants to go straight but fails again and again in the big city of Berlin. Döblin uses montage technique: newspaper reports, advertising slogans, song lyrics, tram timetables and interior monologues are edited together like in a film.

### Erich Maria Remarque (1898 – 1970)

"**Im Westen nichts Neues**" (All Quiet on the Western Front, 1929) depicts the First World War from the perspective of the young soldier Paul Bäumer, without heroism and in sober language. The novel became a worldwide success and was banned and burned by the National Socialists.

## Austrian literature between the wars

In Austria, the end of the Habsburg Monarchy played a major role. Many works deal with the downfall of the old order.

- **Joseph Roth** (1894 – 1939): "Radetzkymarsch" (The Radetzky March, 1932) tells of the decline of the monarchy through three generations of the Trotta family.
- **Robert Musil** (1880 – 1942): "Der Mann ohne Eigenschaften" (The Man Without Qualities, unfinished, from 1930) draws an ironic picture of the monarchy, which Musil calls "Kakania" (after "k. u. k."). His early novel "Die Verwirrungen des Zöglings Törleß" (The Confusions of Young Törless, 1906) is also well known.
- **Ödön von Horváth** (1901 – 1938): his folk plays such as "Geschichten aus dem Wiener Wald" (Tales from the Vienna Woods, 1931) and "Kasimir und Karoline" expose brutality and petty-bourgeois attitudes behind the façade of cosiness. His characters speak an "educated jargon" that disguises their thoughts. His novel "Jugend ohne Gott" (Youth Without God, 1937) criticises the rise of fascism.
- **Stefan Zweig** (1881 – 1942): novellas such as "Brief einer Unbekannten" (Letter from an Unknown Woman) and the later "Schachnovelle" (The Royal Game / Chess Story, 1942), in which a Gestapo prisoner comes close to madness in solitary confinement by replaying chess games. In his autobiography "Die Welt von Gestern" (The World of Yesterday), he describes the lost old Europe.
- **Karl Kraus** (1874 – 1936): the tragedy "Die letzten Tage der Menschheit" (The Last Days of Mankind, 1918/19) assembles hundreds of scenes and quotations from the First World War into an indictment of war, the press and empty phrases.

## Genres

- **Epic:** contemporary novel, big-city novel, war novel, reportage, novella
- **Drama:** epic theatre, folk play, topical play
- **Poetry:** utility poetry, cabaret and chanson lyrics
