A company is usually structured into departments, the so-called **line organisation** (see [organisation](/en/economy_and_law/organisation/)). Projects, however, work across these departments. The **project organisation** defines how the project is embedded in the company and what authority the project manager has over the departments.

## Influence project organisation

In the **influence project organisation** (also staff project organisation), the existing line organisation remains unchanged. The project manager is assigned to management as a staff position. They coordinate, plan and inform, but have no authority to give instructions to employees. Decisions are still made by the heads of department.

- **Advantages:** little organisational effort, employees stay in their departments, flexible use of staff
- **Disadvantages:** the project manager can only work through persuasion, decisions take a long time, nobody feels fully responsible
- **Suitable for:** smaller projects with low risk that only affect a few departments

## Pure project organisation

In the **pure project organisation** (also autonomous or dedicated project organisation), a separate organisational unit is created for the project. For the duration of the project, the employees are completely taken out of their departments and report to the project manager both professionally and disciplinarily.

- **Advantages:** full focus on the project, fast decisions, clear responsibility, strong identification of the team
- **Disadvantages:** high effort to set up, employees are missing in their departments, uncertainty about returning after the project, resources are often not fully utilised
- **Suitable for:** large, long projects that are very important for the company

## Matrix project organisation

The **matrix project organisation** is a mixed form. Employees remain in their departments but work part of their time for the project. The project manager decides what is done in the project and when. The head of department decides who does it and how it is implemented technically.

- **Advantages:** flexible use of specialists, expertise stays in the departments, clear responsibility of the project manager
- **Disadvantages:** employees have two superiors, conflicts over resources and priorities, high coordination effort
- **Suitable for:** medium to large projects in which several departments work together; the most common form in practice

## Comparison

| Criterion                          | influence     | matrix                  | pure            |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------- | ----------------------- | --------------- |
| Authority of the project manager   | none          | for project matters     | complete        |
| Commitment of employees to the project | low       | partial                 | complete        |
| Organisational effort              | low           | medium                  | high            |
| Potential for conflict             | low           | high                    | medium          |
| Project size and importance        | small         | medium to large         | large           |

## Committees in a project

Regardless of the form of organisation, most projects have the following bodies:

- **Client** (project sponsor): commissions the project, provides the resources and makes fundamental decisions.
- **Steering committee:** in larger projects, a body of representatives of the client, the business departments and the project manager. It decides on conflicts, changes and crises.
- **Project manager:** runs the project operationally.
- **Core team:** the most important team members, who accompany the project for its entire duration.
- **Project staff:** work on individual work packages.
- **Experts:** are consulted on specific technical questions when needed.

The **project organisation chart** shows these bodies and their relationships graphically. It belongs in the project handbook.
