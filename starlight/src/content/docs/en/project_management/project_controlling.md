:::note
Project controlling is a term that is not clearly defined in the literature, but the following can be said about it.
:::

Project controlling is a cross-functional management instrument that supports the company's decision-making and steering process with regard to projects through targeted gathering and processing of information. This controlling concept aims to collect, process and communicate relevant information about projects to the decision-makers in the company in order to ensure the efficiency and success of projects.

The core areas of controlling are quality assurance, documentation and reporting. It deals with planning, steering, analysis and control in projects and thus helps with result-oriented decisions (operational and strategic goals). Controlling is close to management.

### Advantages

- **Cost efficiency**: Project controlling helps to keep an eye on costs and to ensure that projects stay within budget.
- **Time management**: It makes it possible to meet schedules and deadlines, which ensures that projects are completed on time.
- **Quality control**: By monitoring and measuring performance, quality standards can be maintained.
- **Risk management**: Project controlling helps to identify risks early and to take measures to minimise them.
- **Communication** and **transparency:** It promotes open communication within the team and with stakeholders to keep everyone up to date.

### Disadvantages

- **Cost** and **time**: Implementing project controlling requires resources and time, which may be limited in smaller companies.
- **Bureaucracy**: Excessive control can lead to excessive bureaucracy and limit flexibility in project management.
- **Employee resistance**: Some employees might perceive project controlling as excessive surveillance and resist it.
- **Complexity**: Project controlling requires the implementation of tools, processes and training, which can mean additional complexity.
- **Sources of error**: If project controlling is not implemented correctly, misinformation and misunderstandings can occur.

## Four aspects - cybernetic control loop

Project controlling deals with these four aspects, which, similar to a control loop in engineering, are run through again and again:

- **Planning**: preparing the resources required to achieve goals
- **Monitoring**: observing and comparing the ACTUAL state of a project with the TARGET state defined in the planning
- **Analysis**: finding out the consequences of deviations from the TARGET state
- **Steering**: implementing corrective measures so that the project does not fail

Controlling also makes it easier for project managers and employees to access information, since it is prepared for them.

There are many [methods](#tools--methods) available to controllers to help them in all four aspects.

## The role of the project controller

Since the main tasks of controlling are quality assurance, documentation and reporting, controllers mainly have to carry out analytical tasks. These include the following activities:

- preparing the accounting data so that it is useful / usable for management
- ensuring that the goals are achieved by means of
  - target/actual comparison
  - identifying deviations
  - assessing consequences
  - proposing measures
  - checking implementation

> The controller is the financial executive of a large or medium-sized company who combines the responsibilities. _- Goodman_

## Tools / methods

Over time, many tools have been developed that support the [four aspects](#four-aspects---cybernetic-control-loop) of project controlling.

#### Cost and performance control

- target/actual comparison
- ABC analysis
- earned value analysis
- milestone trend analysis
- financial plan

#### Reporting

- key figure analysis
- use of suitable software

#### Experience

- interviews
- know-how databases

### ABC analysis

> _Which customers generate the most revenue for our company?_

A well-known principle says: "20% of customers account for 80% of revenue" (Pareto principle). It is important for the company to know which customers get a high priority in areas such as customer support, services etc. Customers are therefore divided into A, B and C customers according to their share of revenue. In the case of customer dissatisfaction, it is important that the company resolves requests from A customers (the most important customers, who account for about 80% of revenue) faster than those of C customers, for example, because otherwise the A customer may no longer be a customer, causing the company enormous revenue losses. In project controlling, the ABC analysis is applied in the same way to work packages or cost items: the few A items that cause most of the costs are monitored particularly closely.

### Schedule control

Regular and timely reporting of the "actual dates" to the project manager is the prerequisite for effective schedule control. For each work package (target date), it must be stated whether the date is met, exceeded or brought forward.

#### Reasons

- change in the scope of work or the quality requirements
- unforeseeable problems
- unrealistic estimation of the effort
- staff shortages due to illness or team members leaving
- low productivity due to poor coordination, lack of experience or little expertise

#### Measures

- mobilising additional staff
- increasing working hours / productivity
- outsourcing / outtasking

### Progress control

Progress control (also performance control) is about determining the completed share of a work package. There are several approaches for this:

- **Status step technique**: The work package is divided into defined steps (e.g. design, implementation, testing, acceptance), each of which is assigned a degree of completion.
- **50-50 technique**: 50% complete as soon as the work package has started; 100% when finished
- **0-100 technique**: 0% as long as not finished; 100% when finished; only makes sense for small work packages
- **Quantity proportionality**: Applicable if measurable or countable result units exist. A completed unit (e.g. an assembled PC) represents a certain increase in the degree of completion or earned value (see below).
- **Time proportionality**: For certain project activities, such as project management, for which an "even distribution" over the entire course of the project is assumed. The degree of completion results from the elapsed time.

### Earned value analysis

The earned value is a key figure that helps to answer the following questions:

> _Is my project within the **cost plan** at the time of control or not?_

- Yes, if the earned value is higher than the actual costs. This means that you are within budget or even below it.
- No, if the earned value is lower than the actual costs. In this case, you are exceeding the budget and your project is "behind" in terms of costs.

> _Is my project on **schedule** at the time of control or not?_

- Yes, if the earned value is higher than the planned costs. This means that you have made more progress than originally planned, and your project is "ahead" in terms of time.
- No, if the earned value is lower than the planned costs. In this case, you have made less progress than planned, and your project is "behind" in terms of time.

A diagram for the earned value analysis looks like this:

![Earned value analysis](/images/project_management/earned_value_analysis.png)

At the `status date`, the current degree of completion (what percentage of the work packages has already been completed?) and the ACTUAL costs are measured. From this, an `earned value` is calculated, which is shown by the red line.

Now you can compare the `earned value` with the `planned cost` or `planned value` to find out whether the work packages are on schedule. You can also compare the `earned value` with the `actual cost` to find out whether you have already spent more or less than planned.

This image clearly shows all four possible scenarios:

![Earned value analysis examples](/images/project_management/earned_value_analysis-2.png)

With the earned value analysis, many further key figures can be calculated that give the controller information about the project status. The most important are:

| Key figure                     | Formula                            | Meaning                                                 |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Planned value (PV)             | from the plan                      | planned cost of the work scheduled up to the status date |
| Earned value (EV)              | degree of completion · total budget | value of the work actually completed                   |
| Actual cost (AC)               | measured                           | costs actually incurred                                 |
| Schedule variance (SV)         | EV − PV                            | negative: project is behind schedule                    |
| Cost variance (CV)             | EV − AC                            | negative: project is more expensive than planned        |
| Schedule performance index (SPI) | EV / PV                          | less than 1: delay                                      |
| Cost performance index (CPI)   | EV / AC                            | less than 1: budget overrun                             |

## Controlling process

Most projects start with a customer explaining the idea of the product in a meeting and wanting the company to implement these requirements quickly, cheaply and with high quality. The company starts planning by first defining the goals. Project controlling can provide the planning employees with plenty of information that could be useful. After frequent exchanges between the requirements specification (Lastenheft) and the functional specification (Pflichtenheft), the project can then officially start.

From the implementation phase onwards, the task of controlling becomes even more important. It now has to regularly take a status sample, which is examined for deviations (for example using a target/actual comparison). If deviations occur, controlling must define measures that flow directly into the implementation. In addition, some dates in the planning will probably have to be changed and communicated to the customer. This process repeats until the product is finished.

## Possible sources of deviations

### Planning errors

- lack of experience
- parts of the project are forgotten
- wrong estimate of the resources required
- complexity overwhelms employees
- time and cost pressure

### Unforeseeable events

- new requirements during implementation
- technical problems
- employees leaving
- bankruptcy of suppliers

### Execution errors

- insufficient employee qualifications
- insufficient communication and coordination in the team
- failure to follow specifications and standards
- lack of motivation
