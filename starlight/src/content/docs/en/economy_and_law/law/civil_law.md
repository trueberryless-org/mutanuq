**Civil law** governs legal relationships between private persons. Its most important basis is the **ABGB** (General Civil Code). It is divided into, among other things, the law of persons, property law, the law of obligations, family law and inheritance law.

## Law of persons

### Natural and legal persons

- **Natural persons** are all human beings.
- **Legal persons** are entities created by law that can themselves have rights and obligations, such as a limited company (GmbH), a public limited company (AG), an association or a municipality. They act through their **bodies**, for example the managing director.

### Legal capacity

**Legal capacity** is the ability to have rights and obligations, for example to own property or to inherit. For natural persons, it begins at **birth** (on condition of live birth, even for unborn children) and ends at death.

### Capacity to contract

**Capacity to contract** is the ability to take on legal obligations through one's own actions, for example by concluding a contract. It depends on age:

| Age                   | Term                         | Capacity to contract                                                                  |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| under 7               | children                     | generally none; only minor everyday transactions (e.g. buying chewing gum) become valid once they are performed |
| 7 to under 14         | minors under 14              | also age-appropriate everyday transactions; transactions that only bring advantages (e.g. accepting gifts) |
| 14 to under 18        | minors aged 14 and over      | also disposing of their own income and pocket money, as long as this does not endanger meeting their living needs; concluding an employment contract (except an apprenticeship contract) |
| from 18               | adults                       | full capacity to contract                                                             |

If a minor concludes a transaction that exceeds their capacity to contract, it is **provisionally invalid**: it only becomes valid with the consent of the parents (or the legal guardian).

:::tip[Example]
15-year-old Lea buys a used laptop for €300 from her apprenticeship wages. The transaction is valid if it does not endanger meeting her living needs. However, she cannot conclude a mobile phone contract with a 24-month commitment without her parents' consent.
:::

Other capacities are **tortious capacity** (responsibility for damage, generally from 14) and **criminal responsibility** (from 14).

## Property law

Property law governs rights to **things**. A distinction is made between movable things (e.g. a car) and immovable things (land and buildings).

### Ownership and possession

- **Ownership** is the legal control over a thing. The owner may in principle do as they please with it and exclude others from it.
- **Possession** is the actual control over a thing with the will to keep it as one's own.
- **Holding** (Innehabung) is mere actual custody without this will.

:::tip[Example]
If you rent an e-bike, you are the holder, and the rental company remains the owner. If someone steals the e-bike, they take possession of it but do not become the owner.
:::

### Acquiring ownership

To acquire ownership, you need a **title** (a legal basis, e.g. a purchase contract) and a **mode** (the transfer):

- for movable things by **handing them over**,
- for real estate by **registration in the land register**.

**Acquisition in good faith** from a non-owner is possible in certain cases, for example when buying a thing from a business in the ordinary course of business. With stolen goods, however, it is usually excluded when buying from private individuals.

### Right of lien

A **lien** secures a claim: if the debt is not paid, the creditor can satisfy the claim from the pledged thing. For real estate, this is called a **mortgage**, which is entered in the land register – for example with a home loan.

## Law of obligations

The law of obligations governs **obligations**, i.e. legal relationships in which one person (the debtor) owes a performance to another (the creditor).

Obligations arise

- from **contracts**, such as a purchase contract, lease, contract for work or employment contract (see [contracts](/en/economy_and_law/law/contracts/)),
- from **causing damage** (see [damages](/en/economy_and_law/law/warranty_and_damages/#damages)),
- from **unjust enrichment**, for example if someone transfers money by mistake.

### Limitation periods

Rights that are not asserted for a long time become **time-barred**: they can then no longer be enforced in court. The general limitation period is **30 years**. However, many claims become time-barred after only **3 years**, for example claims from deliveries and services, wage claims or claims for damages (from the time the damage and the person who caused it become known).
