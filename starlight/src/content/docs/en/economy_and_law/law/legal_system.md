## What is law?

**Law** is the set of rules that govern how people live together and that the state can enforce. It is meant to ensure peace, security and justice.

Besides law, there are other rules for living together:

| Rules               | Example                                    | Consequence of a violation            |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| **Law**             | Theft is prohibited.                       | state sanction (e.g. punishment)      |
| **Morality / ethics** | You should help people in need.          | guilty conscience, disapproval        |
| **Custom / tradition** | You greet people when entering a room.  | social rejection                      |

Law and morality often overlap but are not the same: not everything that is morally reprehensible is prohibited, and some legal provisions (such as traffic rules) are morally neutral.

## Sources of law

- **Statutory law (written law):** Laws are passed by parliament; **ordinances** are issued by administrative authorities on the basis of a law.
- **Customary law:** rules that are followed for a long time and generally because they are considered legally binding. It hardly plays a role in Austria.
- **Case law:** Decisions of the supreme courts (**jurisprudence**) are not binding like a law, but they strongly shape how laws are interpreted.
- **EU law:** Since joining the EU in 1995, the law of the European Union also applies in Austria.

## Hierarchy of the legal system

According to the Austrian legal scholar **Hans Kelsen**, the "father" of the Austrian Federal Constitution of 1920, the legal system is structured hierarchically. Every norm must comply with the higher-ranking norm:

1. **Basic principles of the Federal Constitution** (democratic, republican, federal, rule of law, separation of powers, liberal) – can only be changed by a referendum
2. **Constitutional laws** – require a two-thirds majority in the National Council
3. **Simple laws** – a majority in the National Council is sufficient
4. **Ordinances** – issued by administrative authorities
5. **Official decisions and judgements** – concern individual cases

The **Constitutional Court** checks whether laws comply with the constitution and can repeal unconstitutional laws.

## Public and private law

| Public law                                             | Private law                                           |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| governs the relationship between the state and citizens or between state bodies | governs the relationships between citizens |
| **superiority and subordination**                      | **equality** of the parties                           |
| constitutional law, administrative law, criminal law, tax law, procedural law | civil law (ABGB), business law (UGB), labour law (partly) |

The most important law in private law is the **Allgemeines bürgerliches Gesetzbuch (ABGB, General Civil Code)** of 1811 – one of the oldest civil codes still in force in Europe. For entrepreneurs, the **Unternehmensgesetzbuch (UGB, Business Code)** also applies.

## Structure of the state and separation of powers

Austria is a **democratic republic** and a **federal state** with nine federal provinces. State power is divided between three independent branches:

| Branch                         | Task                  | Bodies (federal level)                            |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| **Legislature**                | passes laws           | National Council and Federal Council (parliament) |
| **Executive** – administration | implements the laws   | Federal President, Federal Government, ministries, authorities |
| **Judiciary**                  | administers justice   | independent judges                                |

In the provinces, the **provincial parliaments** pass provincial laws, and the provincial governments implement them.

### How a federal law is made

1. **Legislative initiative:** government bill, motion by members of parliament, popular initiative (from 100,000 signatures) or motion by the Federal Council
2. Discussion in a **committee** of the National Council
3. **Resolution** in the National Council (usually three readings)
4. The **Federal Council** can raise an objection, which the National Council can override with a confirming resolution.
5. **Authentication** by the Federal President and countersignature by the Federal Chancellor
6. **Promulgation** in the Federal Law Gazette – only then does the law come into force

## The courts

- **Ordinary courts** decide civil cases (disputes between private parties) and criminal cases: district courts, regional courts, higher regional courts and the **Supreme Court (OGH)**.
- **Administrative courts** review decisions of administrative authorities; at the top is the **Supreme Administrative Court (VwGH)**.
- The **Constitutional Court (VfGH)** reviews laws and ordinances for their constitutionality and protects fundamental rights.

More about civil proceedings can be found under [court proceedings and insolvency](/en/economy_and_law/law/civil_procedure_and_insolvency/).

## EU law

The law of the European Union takes **precedence** over national law.

- **Regulations** apply directly in all member states, for example the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).
- **Directives** must first be transposed into national law by the member states. For example, the Austrian Distance and Off-Premises Contracts Act and the Consumer Warranty Act are based on EU directives.

The **Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU)** in Luxembourg ensures that EU law is interpreted uniformly.
