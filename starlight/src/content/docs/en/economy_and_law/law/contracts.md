## Concluding a contract

A **contract** is concluded through matching declarations of intent by at least two persons: an offer and its acceptance. Both must agree on the essential points. In a purchase, for example, the goods and the price.

### Offer and acceptance

- An offer must be so specific that the other party only has to say "yes". It binds the person making it: between persons who are present, it must be accepted immediately; between absent persons, within a reasonable period (or the period set).
- Shop window displays, catalogues, brochures and online shops are not offers but only an invitation to make an offer. The customer only makes the offer when taking the goods to the checkout or submitting an order.
- An acceptance with changes counts as a new offer (counteroffer).

:::tip[Example]
In an online shop, a monitor is mistakenly shown for €19 instead of €199. The listing is only an invitation to make an offer. By ordering, the customer makes an offer that the retailer can accept or not. An automatic order confirmation is usually only an acknowledgement of receipt and not yet an acceptance.
:::

### Form

In principle, there is **freedom of form**: contracts can be concluded orally, in writing or implicitly (through actions, such as getting on a tram). The law only prescribes a form for certain contracts, for example the written form for guarantees by private persons or a notarial deed for the articles of association of a GmbH and for gifts without immediate handover. A written contract does, however, make proof easier.

### Validity and defects

A contract is only valid if

- the contracting parties have capacity to contract (see [capacity to contract](/en/economy_and_law/law/civil_law/#capacity-to-contract)),
- its content is possible and permitted,
- the declarations of intent are free from defects.

| Defect                     | Example                                                           | Consequence                               |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| violation of the law or of public morals | buying drugs, usury (exploiting an emergency for a gross imbalance between performance and consideration) | void, invalid from the outset |
| impossible performance     | buying a car that has already burnt out                           | void                                      |
| Mistake                | false idea about essential points (mistake about the transaction) | rescission or adjustment if the mistake was caused by the other party, should have been obvious to them or was pointed out in time |
| Fraud (fraudulent misrepresentation) | the seller deliberately conceals accident damage        | voidable                                  |
| Duress                 | signature under threat of violence                                | voidable                                  |
| Reduction by more than half (_laesio enormis_) | you receive less than half of what you give | voidable (not in transactions between businesses) |

## Types of contracts

| Contract           | Content                                                            |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Purchase contract | transfer of a thing in return for money                         |
| Exchange contract | thing for thing                                                 |
| Lease          | letting a thing for use in return for payment                      |
| Loan for use   | letting a thing for use free of charge                             |
| Loan agreement | providing money (or other fungible things) that must be returned   |
| Contract for work | producing a result, e.g. programming a website             |
| Contract of service (employment contract) | performing services in personal dependence (see [labour law](/en/economy_and_law/law/labour_law/)) |
| Leasing contract | letting an asset in return for leasing instalments, often with a purchase option (see [financing](/en/economy_and_law/accounting/financing/#leasing)) |

## The purchase contract

The **purchase contract** is the most common transaction. The seller undertakes to hand over the goods and transfer ownership, the buyer to pay the purchase price and accept the goods.

### Business and consumer transactions

| Transaction                        | Parties                                 | applicable law                          |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Consumer transaction (B2C)     | business and consumer                   | ABGB and [consumer protection law](/en/economy_and_law/law/consumer_protection/) |
| Mutual business transaction (B2B) | two businesses                       | ABGB and UGB                            |
| Private transaction (C2C)      | two private persons                     | ABGB                                    |

Consumers are specially protected because they are usually inferior to businesses in knowledge and bargaining power.

### Content of the purchase contract

Besides the goods and the price, the following are often agreed:

- **Delivery terms:** delivery time, place of delivery, packaging and transport costs (e.g. "free domicile" or "ex works")
- **Payment terms:** payment in advance, on delivery (cash on delivery) or on credit (later), a cash discount for early payment (e.g. "2 % discount within 10 days, net within 30 days"), rebates
- **Retention of title:** the goods remain the property of the seller until they have been paid for in full.

If nothing is agreed, the statutory rules apply, or the **general terms and conditions (GTC)** of the business, provided they have become part of the contract.

## Performance

A contract is **performed** when both parties fulfil their obligations properly, i.e. at the right time, at the right place, in the right quantity and quality.

- **Place of performance:** Unless agreed otherwise, debts in goods are to be collected (the buyer collects the goods from the seller), and money debts are to be delivered (the debtor must transmit the money at their own cost and risk; for bank transfers, the amount must reach the creditor's account on time).
- **Time of performance:** Unless agreed otherwise, the creditor can demand performance immediately.

## Breaches of contract

If a contract is not performed or not performed properly, this is called a **breach of contract**.

### Late delivery (default of the debtor)

The seller does not deliver on time. The buyer can

- insist on performance or
- withdraw from the contract after setting a reasonable grace period.

In a **fixed-date transaction** (performance only makes sense on a certain date, e.g. a wedding cake), the contract is automatically cancelled if performance is late, unless the buyer immediately declares that they still insist on performance. If the seller is at fault for the delay, the buyer can also claim damages.

### Default of acceptance (default of the creditor)

The buyer does not accept the goods that were properly offered. The seller can deposit the goods at the buyer's cost and risk, insist on performance or withdraw after setting a grace period. In transactions between businesses, the seller may also sell the goods (**self-help sale**).

### Late payment

The buyer does not pay on time. The seller can insist on payment or withdraw after setting a grace period (with retention of title, they can then demand the goods back). They are also entitled to **default interest**: by law 4 % per year, in transactions between businesses 9.2 percentage points above the base rate, as well as reminder and collection costs.

If payment is still not made despite **reminders**, the creditor can initiate the court [payment order procedure](/en/economy_and_law/law/civil_procedure_and_insolvency/#payment-order-procedure).

### Defective delivery

If the goods delivered are defective, the buyer has rights under the [warranty](/en/economy_and_law/law/warranty_and_damages/).
