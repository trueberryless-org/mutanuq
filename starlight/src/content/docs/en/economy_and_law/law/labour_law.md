**Labour law** governs the relationships between employers and employees. Because employees are economically dependent on their employer, it contains many **mandatory** protective provisions that must not be changed to the employees' disadvantage.

## Hierarchy of labour law

Rules on several levels apply to an employment relationship. As a rule, a lower level may only provide **more favourable** rules for the employee (**favourability principle**):

1. **Laws** (e.g. the Salaried Employees Act, the Working Hours Act, the Holiday Act, the Labour Constitution Act)
2. **Collective agreement**
3. **Works agreement**
4. **Employment contract**
5. **Instructions** from the employer

## Collective labour law

### Social partners and collective agreements

The **social partners** are the organisations representing employers (the **Economic Chamber** and the Chamber of Agriculture) and employees (the **trade unions** in the Austrian Trade Union Federation and the **Chamber of Labour**). Every year, they negotiate the **collective agreements** for the individual sectors.

A collective agreement regulates, among other things,

- **minimum wages and salaries** by job group and years of experience,
- the annual pay rises,
- special payments (holiday and Christmas bonuses),
- working hours, allowances and overtime supplements,
- notice periods.

Because almost all employers are members of the Economic Chamber, collective agreements apply to around 98 % of employment relationships in Austria. That is why there is no statutory minimum wage. For IT companies, the collective agreement for employees of companies in the field of automatic data processing and information technology usually applies.

### Works council and works agreements

In businesses with at least **five** permanent employees, a **works council** can be elected. It represents the interests of the workforce towards the management, has rights to information, participation and control, and must be notified, for example, before any dismissal.

**Works agreements** are written agreements between the works council and the employer, for example on working time models, working from home or monitoring measures (e.g. time recording systems).

## Individual labour law

### Employment contract

With an **employment contract**, the employee undertakes to perform services for the employer in **personal dependence**: they are bound to working hours, place of work and instructions and are integrated into the business. In return, the employer pays remuneration.

This must be distinguished from

- the **freelance service contract**: services without personal dependence (no fixed working hours, no instructions),
- the **contract for work**: a specific **result** is owed (e.g. a finished app), not working time.

The employment contract requires no specific form. However, the employer must immediately issue a **statement of employment terms** that records the essential rights and duties in writing, such as the start date, place of work, job, remuneration, working hours, holidays, notice periods and the applicable collective agreement.

A **probationary period** of at most one month is often agreed, during which either side can end the employment at any time without giving reasons.

### Duties of the parties

| Duties of the employee                              | Duties of the employer                              |
| --------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| **Duty to work**: personal, careful work            | **Duty to pay**: punctual payment of wages or salary |
| **Duty of loyalty**: confidentiality about business secrets, no competing activities | **Duty of care**: protection of life, health and personality, occupational safety |
| following instructions                              | equal treatment, issuing a reference                |

### Working hours

- **Normal working hours** are in principle **8 hours per day** and **40 hours per week**; many collective agreements provide for less (e.g. 38.5 hours).
- **Overtime** must be compensated with a supplement of at least **50 %** (or with time off in the ratio 1 : 1.5).
- Maximum working hours are **12 hours per day** and **60 hours per week**; on average over 17 weeks, 48 hours must not be exceeded.
- After 6 hours, employees are entitled to a **break** of at least 30 minutes, and between two working days to a rest period of at least 11 hours.
- Stricter rules apply to young people under 18.

### Holidays and sick leave

- **Holiday entitlement** is **5 weeks (30 working days on a six-day basis or 25 on a five-day basis)** per working year, 6 weeks after 25 years of service. Holidays are agreed between the parties and cannot be ordered unilaterally.
- In case of **illness**, the employee is entitled to **continued payment of remuneration**, depending on the length of employment for at least 6 weeks in full and 4 weeks at half pay. They must report the illness immediately and present a medical certificate on request.

### Special payments

Most collective agreements provide for a **holiday bonus** (14th salary) and a **Christmas bonus** (13th salary). They are not required by law but are taxed favourably (see [payroll](/en/economy_and_law/accounting/payroll/)).

## Termination of employment

| Type                          | Description                                                                                    |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Expiry**                    | A fixed-term employment ends automatically on the agreed date.                                 |
| **Termination by mutual agreement** | Both sides agree on the end. This is possible at any time, preferably in writing.        |
| **Notice of termination**     | One-sided termination with a **notice period** and a **termination date**; no reason has to be given. |
| **Summary dismissal**         | Immediate termination by the employer for an **important reason** (e.g. theft, persistent refusal to work). |
| **Premature resignation**     | Immediate termination by the employee for an important reason (e.g. salary is not paid).       |
| **Termination during probation** | at any time without notice or reason                                                        |

### Notice periods

If the **employer** gives notice, the statutory periods for salaried employees (and since 2021 in principle also for manual workers) depend on the length of employment:

| Years of service | Notice period   |
| ---------------- | --------------- |
| up to 2 years    | 6 weeks         |
| from 2 years     | 2 months        |
| from 5 years     | 3 months        |
| from 15 years    | 4 months        |
| from 25 years    | 5 months        |

Notice can in principle only be given to the **end of a quarter**, unless agreed otherwise (an agreement to the 15th or the last day of a month is permitted). If the **employee** gives notice, the period is generally **one month** to the end of a month.

Certain people enjoy special **protection against dismissal**, for example pregnant women, parents on parental leave, works council members, apprentices and people with recognised disabilities. A dismissal for a prohibited motive (e.g. because of trade union membership) or a socially unjustified dismissal can be challenged at the labour and social court.

### Severance pay

For employment relationships that began in 2003 or later, the new severance pay system applies: from the second month, the employer continuously pays **1.53 %** of the gross remuneration into an **employee provision fund**. The money stays with the employee when they change jobs and can be paid out or reinvested under certain conditions.

### Reference

When employment ends, the employee is entitled to a written **reference** stating the duration and type of work. It must not contain any information that makes it harder for the employee to find a new job.
