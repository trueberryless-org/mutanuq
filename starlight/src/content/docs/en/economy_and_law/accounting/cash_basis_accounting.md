**Cash-basis accounting** is the simplest way of determining profit. It is mainly used by sole proprietors, freelancers and small partnerships.

## Who may use it?

- sole proprietorships and partnerships that are **not** obliged to keep accounts under the Business Code, i.e. whose turnover does not exceed **€700,000** in two consecutive years,
- members of liberal professions (e.g. doctors, architects), regardless of their turnover.

Corporations must always use [double-entry bookkeeping](/en/economy_and_law/accounting/bookkeeping/).

## The cash principle

The profit is simply the difference between the business income and expenses of a year:

$$
\text{Profit} = \text{Business income} - \text{Business expenses}
$$

What matters is **when the money actually flows** (cash principle), not when the service was provided or the invoice was issued. So an invoice issued in December that the customer only pays in January is income of the new year.

Unlike in double-entry bookkeeping, receivables, liabilities, provisions and stocks of goods are not recorded. There is also no balance sheet.

:::note
**Regularly recurring** income and expenses (e.g. rent, interest) that flow shortly before or after the turn of the year (within 15 days) are allocated to the year to which they economically belong.
:::

## Exceptions

Some transactions are not treated according to the cash principle:

- **Fixed assets** such as computers, machines or vehicles are **depreciated** over their useful life, just as in double-entry bookkeeping. Only [low-value assets](/en/economy_and_law/accounting/bookkeeping/#depreciation) up to €1,000 may be deducted immediately.
- **Loans** are neither income nor expenses. Only the **interest** is a business expense.
- **Private withdrawals** (money for private purposes) and **private contributions** do not change the profit.

## VAT

In cash-basis accounting, VAT can be treated in two ways:

- **Gross method:** VAT is treated as income, and input VAT and the VAT paid to the tax office are treated as expenses.
- **Net method:** VAT and input VAT are left out, and only net amounts are recorded.

Businesses that use the [small business exemption](/en/economy_and_law/accounting/taxes/#small-business-exemption) do not charge VAT and may not deduct input VAT.

## Flat-rate expenses

Under certain conditions, small businesses can claim a **flat rate** as business expenses instead of their actual expenses. This saves record-keeping effort.

:::tip[Example]
A self-employed web designer (a small business without VAT) has the following payments in a year:

| Transaction                                | Income    | Expense  |
| ------------------------------------------ | --------- | -------- |
| fees paid during the year                  | €38,000   |          |
| fee for a December project, paid in January | (next year) |        |
| software licences and hosting              |           | €1,800   |
| laptop for €2,400 (useful life 3 years)    |           | €800 (depreciation) |
| social insurance                           |           | €7,500   |
| further training                           |           | €900     |
| taking out a loan of €5,000                | (no income) |        |
| loan interest                              |           | €250     |

$$
\text{Profit} = 38\,000 - (1\,800 + 800 + 7\,500 + 900 + 250) = 26\,750\ \text{€}
$$

This profit is subject to [income tax](/en/economy_and_law/accounting/taxes/#income-tax).
:::
