**Double-entry bookkeeping** records every business transaction twice: once on the debit side of one account and once on the credit side of another. This makes it possible to determine both the assets and the profit of the business at any time. Mistakes also become visible, because both sides must match.

## Obligation to keep accounts

- Corporations (limited companies and public limited companies) must use double-entry bookkeeping regardless of their turnover.
- Sole proprietors and partnerships must use double-entry bookkeeping if their turnover exceeds €700,000 in two consecutive years (or €1 million once).
- Smaller businesses may use the simpler [cash-basis accounting](/en/economy_and_law/accounting/cash_basis_accounting/).

Records must be complete, correct, timely and orderly and must be kept for **7 years**. Every entry needs a voucher ("no entry without a voucher"), such as an invoice, a bank statement or a receipt.

## Stocktaking and inventory

At the beginning and end of each financial year, the business must record its assets and debts:

- Stocktaking is the activity: counting, measuring, weighing and valuing all assets and debts.
- The inventory is the result: a detailed list of all assets and debts by type, quantity and value.

The concise [balance sheet](/en/economy_and_law/accounting/annual_accounts/#balance-sheet) is prepared from the inventory.

## Accounts

An **account** is a two-sided record in the shape of a T. The left side is called debit, the right side credit.

### Balance sheet accounts

**Balance sheet accounts** record assets and capital. They are opened from the balance sheet and closed into the balance sheet again at the end of the year.

| Type of account                       | Opening balance | Increases | Decreases |
| ------------------------------------- | --------------- | --------- | --------- |
| Asset accounts (e.g. bank, cash, machines, receivables) | debit | debit | credit |
| Liability and equity accounts (e.g. equity, bank loan, trade payables) | credit | credit | debit |

### Income statement accounts

**Income statement accounts** record everything that changes equity:

- **Expense accounts** (e.g. cost of goods sold, salaries, rent, depreciation): expenses are recorded as debits.
- **Revenue accounts** (e.g. sales revenue, interest income): revenue is recorded as credits.

At the end of the year, the income statement accounts are closed via the **income statement**. If revenue exceeds expenses, there is a profit, which increases equity.

### Chart of accounts

So that all businesses record transactions in the same way, Austria uses a **standard chart of accounts** that divides accounts into ten classes:

| Class | Content                                     |
| ----- | ------------------------------------------- |
| 0     | fixed assets                                |
| 1     | inventories                                 |
| 2     | other current assets (receivables, cash, bank) |
| 3     | liabilities and provisions                  |
| 4     | operating revenue                           |
| 5     | cost of materials and goods                 |
| 6     | personnel expenses                          |
| 7     | other operating expenses, depreciation      |
| 8     | financial income and expenses, taxes on income |
| 9     | equity and closing accounts                 |

## Journal entries

A **journal entry** states which account is debited and which is credited: "debit to credit". This is how to find it:

1. Which accounts are affected?
2. Are they asset, liability, expense or revenue accounts?
3. Does the balance increase or decrease?
4. So on which side is the entry made?

:::tip[Examples]
| Business transaction                                    | Journal entry                               | Amount   |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | -------- |
| cash withdrawal from the bank account                   | cash to bank                                | €500     |
| purchase of a laptop on credit (€1,000 net)             | office equipment €1,000 and input VAT €200 to trade payables €1,200 | €1,200 |
| payment of rent by bank transfer                        | rent expense to bank                        | €800     |
| sale of a service on credit (€2,000 net)                | receivables €2,400 to revenue €2,000 and VAT €400 | €2,400 |
| customer pays the invoice                               | bank to receivables                         | €2,400   |
:::

### VAT in bookkeeping

- When buying, the VAT paid to the supplier is recorded as input VAT, a claim against the tax office.
- When selling, the VAT charged to the customer is recorded as a liability towards the tax office.
- The difference (the VAT payable) is paid to the tax office monthly or quarterly (see [taxes](/en/economy_and_law/accounting/taxes/#value-added-tax)).

## Depreciation

Fixed assets such as machines, computers or vehicles wear out over several years. That is why their acquisition costs are not recorded as an expense in the year of purchase but spread over their **useful life**. This annual loss of value is called depreciation.

With **straight-line depreciation**, the same amount is written off every year:

$$
\text{Annual depreciation} = \frac{\text{Acquisition costs}}{\text{Useful life}}
$$

:::tip[Example]
A server costs €6,000 net and is used for 3 years. The annual depreciation is €2,000. After one year, its book value is €4,000.

If an asset is acquired in the **second half** of the financial year, only half of the annual depreciation may be claimed in the first year (half-year rule).
:::

**Low-value assets** with acquisition costs of up to €1,000 net may be written off in full immediately in the year of acquisition.

Besides straight-line depreciation, **declining-balance depreciation** (with a constant percentage of the remaining value) is also possible.

## Provisions and reserves

| Term             | Meaning                                                                     | Example                                      |
| ---------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Provision    | debt capital for expenses that have already been caused but whose amount or timing is still uncertain | expected legal costs, warranty obligations, tax arrears |
| Reserve      | equity: profits that are not distributed but kept in the business      | revenue reserve for future investments       |

Provisions reduce the profit of the current year, while reserves are only formed from the profit.

## Expenses and sales revenue

- An expense is the consumption of value in a financial year, regardless of whether it has already been paid (e.g. cost of goods sold, salaries, depreciation).
- Sales revenue is the income from selling goods and services (net, without VAT).

An expense is not the same as a **payment**: if a business buys a server for €6,000, that is a payment of €6,000, but the expense in the first year is only the depreciation of €2,000.
