At the end of each financial year, businesses that are obliged to keep accounts prepare **annual accounts**. They consist of the balance sheet and the income statement; corporations also add notes and, from a certain size, a management report. Corporations must submit them to the company register, where anyone can inspect them.

## Balance sheet

The **balance sheet** compares assets and capital on a reporting date. It shows what the capital is used for (assets) and where it comes from (equity and liabilities). Both sides are always equal.

| Assets (use of funds)                           | Equity and liabilities (source of funds)        |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| A. Fixed assets: long-term in the business  | A. Equity                                   |
| intangible assets (software, licences)          | share capital                                   |
| property, plant and equipment (land, buildings, machines, IT) | reserves                          |
| financial assets (investments, securities)      | net profit                                      |
| B. Current assets: short-term               | B. Provisions                               |
| inventories                                     | C. Liabilities (debt capital)               |
| receivables                                     | long-term (e.g. bank loans)                     |
| cash, bank balances                             | short-term (e.g. trade payables)                |

The assets side is ordered by **liquidity** (from hard to liquidate to cash), the equity and liabilities side by maturity (from long-term to short-term capital).

$$
\text{Assets} = \text{Equity} + \text{Debt capital}
$$

## Income statement

The **income statement** compares the revenue and expenses of a financial year and determines the result for the year (profit or loss). In Austria, it is usually prepared in vertical format using the total cost method:

| Item                                             | Example (€)    |
| ------------------------------------------------ | -------------- |
| Sales revenue                                    | 800,000        |
| + other operating income                         | 10,000         |
| − cost of materials and goods                    | −250,000       |
| − personnel expenses                             | −350,000       |
| − depreciation                                   | −40,000        |
| − other operating expenses                       | −90,000        |
| = Operating result (EBIT)                    | 80,000     |
| + financial income − financial expenses          | −5,000         |
| = Result before taxes                        | 75,000     |
| − taxes on income (e.g. 23 % corporate income tax) | −17,250      |
| = Net income for the year                    | 57,750     |

### Effect of business transactions on profit

Not every business transaction changes the profit:

| Business transaction                              | affects profit?  |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------- |
| sale of goods (revenue)                           | yes, increases profit |
| payment of salaries                               | yes, reduces profit |
| depreciation of a machine                         | yes, reduces profit (without a cash flow) |
| purchase of a machine                             | no, cash is exchanged for fixed assets |
| taking out a loan                                 | no, more cash but also more debt |
| repaying a loan                                   | no (only the interest is an expense) |
| customer pays an open invoice                     | no, a receivable is exchanged for a bank balance |
| forming a provision                               | yes, reduces profit |

## Key figures

**Key figures** make it possible to assess annual accounts and compare them with other years or businesses.

### Capital structure

$$
\text{Equity ratio} = \frac{\text{Equity}}{\text{Total capital}} \cdot 100\,\%
$$

A high equity ratio means financial independence and security in crises. Under the Austrian Business Reorganisation Act, a ratio below **8 %** (combined with a notional debt repayment period of more than 15 years) indicates a need for reorganisation.

$$
\text{Debt-to-equity ratio} = \frac{\text{Debt capital}}{\text{Equity}}
$$

### Fixed asset coverage

According to the **golden balance sheet rule**, assets that are tied up in the long term should also be financed in the long term:

$$
\text{Fixed asset coverage} = \frac{\text{Equity} + \text{long-term debt}}{\text{Fixed assets}} \cdot 100\,\% \;\ge\; 100\,\%
$$

### Liquidity

**Liquidity** is the ability to make payments on time when they fall due.

$$
\text{Cash ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash}}{\text{Short-term liabilities}} \qquad
\text{Quick ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash} + \text{short-term receivables}}{\text{Short-term liabilities}}
$$

The quick ratio should be at least **100 %**.

### Profitability

**Profitability** indicates how well the capital employed earns a return:

$$
\text{Return on equity} = \frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Equity}} \cdot 100\,\%
$$

$$
\text{Return on total capital} = \frac{\text{Net income} + \text{interest on debt}}{\text{Total capital}} \cdot 100\,\%
$$

$$
\text{Return on sales} = \frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Sales revenue}} \cdot 100\,\%
$$

### Cash flow

**Cash flow** shows how much money a business generates on its own. In simplified form:

$$
\text{Cash flow} \approx \text{Net income} + \text{Depreciation} + \text{Increase in long-term provisions}
$$

Depreciation is added because it reduces the profit but does not lead to an outflow of cash.

:::tip[Example]
A business has equity of €200,000, total capital of €500,000, net income of €30,000, interest on debt of €10,000 and sales revenue of €600,000.

- Equity ratio: $\frac{200\,000}{500\,000} = 40\,\%$
- Return on equity: $\frac{30\,000}{200\,000} = 15\,\%$
- Return on total capital: $\frac{30\,000 + 10\,000}{500\,000} = 8\,\%$
- Return on sales: $\frac{30\,000}{600\,000} = 5\,\%$

Because the return on total capital (8 %) is higher than the interest rate on debt ($\frac{10\,000}{300\,000} \approx 3.3\,\%$), additional debt increases the return on equity (**leverage effect**), but it also increases the risk.
:::
