Payroll accounting calculates every month what employees are paid and which contributions the employer has to pay. It is one of the most complex tasks in accounting, because many laws and the relevant collective agreement have to be taken into account.

## Basic terms

- Traditionally, manual workers receive wages and salaried employees receive a salary. Today, both are generally called remuneration.
- **Gross pay** is the agreed remuneration before deductions.
- **Net pay** is the amount transferred to the employee's account.
- The minimum amount is set by the collective agreement (see [labour law](/en/economy_and_law/law/labour_law/#social-partners-and-collective-agreements)).

## From gross to net

Two deductions are withheld from gross pay and paid by the employer to the relevant authorities:

1. the employee's social insurance contributions (to the Austrian Health Insurance Fund),
2. wage tax (to the tax office).

$$
\text{Net pay} = \text{Gross pay} - \text{Social insurance (employee)} - \text{Wage tax}
$$

### Social insurance

For salaried employees, the employee's social insurance contributions amount to around 18 % of gross pay in total. They consist of contributions to

- health insurance,
- pension insurance (the largest share),
- unemployment insurance (reduced for low incomes),
- as well as the Chamber of Labour levy and the housing subsidy contribution.

Contributions are only payable up to the maximum contribution base; the part of income above it is contribution-free. People who earn less than the marginal earnings threshold are only insured against accidents and pay no contributions.

### Wage tax

Wage tax is the income tax of employees. The employer calculates it as follows:

1. The social insurance contributions are deducted from gross pay (tax base).
2. The tax base is extrapolated to a year.
3. The [income tax rates](/en/economy_and_law/accounting/taxes/#tax-rates) are applied to it.
4. Tax credits (e.g. the commuter tax credit, the family bonus) are deducted.
5. The result is converted back to one month.

The commuter tax credit is available to all employees and covers travel costs to work as a flat rate. Further costs can be claimed with the [employee tax assessment](/en/economy_and_law/accounting/taxes/#wage-tax-and-employee-tax-assessment).

:::tip[Example (simplified)]
A software developer earns €3,000 gross per month (paid 14 times a year).

| Item                                            | Amount         |
| ----------------------------------------------- | -------------- |
| gross pay                                       | €3,000.00      |
| social insurance, employee (approx. 18 %)       | −€540.00       |
| = tax base for wage tax                         | €2,460.00      |
| wage tax (approx.)                              | −€290.00       |
| = net pay                                       | ≈ €2,170.00    |

The exact wage tax depends on the current tax rates and tax credits. For precise calculations, the Ministry of Finance provides a gross-to-net calculator.
:::

## Special payments

The special payments provided for in the collective agreement, a holiday bonus (14th salary) and a Christmas bonus (13th salary), are taxed favourably:

- An allowance of €620 per year is tax-free.
- The part above it is taxed at only 6 % up to one sixth of the regular annual pay (with 14 equal payments, that is exactly two monthly salaries).

As a result, much more of a special payment is left over after tax than of a normal monthly salary. Social insurance contributions are still due.

## Additional personnel costs

For the employer, an employee costs considerably more than their gross pay. On top of it come additional personnel costs of around 30 % of gross pay:

| Contribution                                            | Recipient          | Amount (approx.) |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------ | ---------------- |
| employer's social insurance contributions (health, pension, unemployment and accident insurance, insolvency protection) | health insurance fund | around 21 % |
| employer's contribution to the Family Burdens Equalisation Fund | tax office  | around 3.7 %     |
| supplement to the employer's contribution               | tax office         | around 0.3 to 0.4 % (depending on the province) |
| municipal tax                                           | municipality       | 3 %              |
| employee provision fund (severance pay)                 | provision fund     | 1.53 %           |

:::tip[Example]
For the software developer with a gross salary of €3,000, the employer pays about $3\,000 \cdot 1.30 = 3\,900$ € per month. Over a year (14 payments), she costs the company around €54,600, while she herself receives around €30,000 net.
:::

## Payslips and reports

The employer must

- issue a payslip every month showing gross pay, all deductions and net pay,
- register employees with social insurance before they start work,
- report and pay the contributions every month,
- send an annual payslip (form L16) to the tax office after the end of the year.
