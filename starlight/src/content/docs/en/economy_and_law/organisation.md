A business needs a clear organisation so that everyone knows who is responsible for what and how work is done. A distinction is made between the **organisational structure** (who does what?) and the **process organisation** (how, when and in what order is work done?).

## Business functions

Typical functions of a business are:

| Function                     | Tasks                                                                |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Procurement                  | purchasing materials, goods and services, warehousing                |
| Production                   | making the products or providing the services (e.g. software development) |
| Marketing and sales          | market research, advertising, sales, customer care (see [marketing](/en/economy_and_law/marketing/)) |
| Finance and accounting       | raising capital, bookkeeping, cost accounting, controlling (see [accounting](/en/economy_and_law/accounting/)) |
| Human resources              | workforce planning, recruiting, training, payroll                    |
| Research and development     | new products and processes                                           |
| Management                   | planning, deciding, organising and controlling the whole             |

## Organisational structure

### Elements

- Task: The overall task of the business is broken down into subtasks (task analysis) and combined again into sensible units (task synthesis).
- Position: the smallest organisational unit, an area of responsibility for one person, described in a job description with tasks, authority and responsibility.
- Managerial position: a position with the authority to give instructions to other positions.
- Staff position: a position without authority to give instructions that advises and supports a manager, for example a legal department or an assistant to the management.
- Department: several positions under common management.

The span of control indicates how many employees report directly to a manager. The number of hierarchy levels is called the depth of the hierarchy.

The organisational structure is shown in an **organisation chart**: positions and departments as boxes, reporting lines as connecting lines.

### Single-line system

In the single-line system, each position has exactly one superior. Instructions and reports only follow the official chain of command.

- Strengths: clear responsibilities, unambiguous instructions, easy control
- Weaknesses: long communication paths, overloaded upper management, little flexibility

### Line-and-staff system

The line-and-staff system adds staff positions to the single-line system that give managers specialist advice (e.g. legal, controlling, IT security).

- Strengths: specialists relieve management, clear reporting lines remain
- Weaknesses: conflicts between staff (knowledge) and line (decisions); staff have influence without responsibility

### Multi-line system

In the multi-line system, a position can receive instructions from several superiors, each for their own area of expertise (principle of the shortest path).

- Strengths: specialised managers, short communication paths
- Weaknesses: contradictory instructions, unclear responsibility, conflicts of authority

### Functional and divisional organisation

- In a functional organisation, departments are formed by activity (purchasing, production, sales). It suits smaller businesses with a uniform range of products.
- In a divisional organisation, divisions are formed by product, customer group or region, such as "software development", "IT infrastructure" and "training". Each division works largely independently, often as a profit centre with its own profit responsibility. This suits large businesses with different products but leads to duplicated structures.

### Matrix organisation

A matrix organisation combines two structures, usually functions and products or projects. Each position has two superiors: one responsible for the specialist area (e.g. the head of development) and one for the product or project.

- Strengths: flexibility, specialist knowledge is used across departments, good for projects
- Weaknesses: a lot of coordination, conflicts between the superiors, unclear responsibility

### Project organisation

A separate project organisation is created for temporary undertakings. The project manager can only coordinate (staff project organisation), share authority with the line managers (matrix project organisation) or lead a dedicated team with full authority (pure project organisation). More on this can be found under [project management](/en/project_management/).

### Modern forms of organisation

Many IT companies rely on flat hierarchies, self-organising cross-functional teams (such as agile Scrum teams) and network structures. This speeds up decisions and encourages personal responsibility, but it requires a high degree of independence from employees.

## Process organisation

Process organisation designs the work processes: which activities are carried out in which order, by whom, where, when and with which tools? The goals are short lead times, good capacity utilisation, high quality and low costs.

A **business process** is a sequence of activities that creates value for a customer, for example handling an order from receipt to payment. A distinction is made between core processes (directly creating value), support processes (e.g. IT, human resources) and management processes (e.g. planning, controlling).

### Representing processes

| Representation                     | Description                                                                  |
| ---------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Flowchart                          | activities and decisions as symbols, connected by arrows                     |
| Event-driven process chain (EPC)   | alternating events and functions, connected by logical operators             |
| BPMN (Business Process Model and Notation) | internationally standardised notation with activities, events, gateways and swimlanes for responsibilities |
| Gantt chart                        | chronological sequence of activities                                         |
| Network diagram                    | dependencies between activities and the critical path                        |

Analysing and improving processes is closely linked to information technology: today, many processes are supported or automated by software such as ERP systems.
